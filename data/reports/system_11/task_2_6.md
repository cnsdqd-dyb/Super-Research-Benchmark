# Research Report: Impact of Thermal Expansion Coefficient Mismatch on Interface Cracking and Stress Concentration in Metal Matrix Composites

## Abstract

Metal Matrix Composites (MMCs) are advanced materials designed to achieve high specific strengths and high-temperature resistance, making them suitable for aerospace and other high-performance applications. However, the mismatch in the coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between the fiber and the matrix can lead to significant residual stresses and interface cracking during thermal cycling. This report investigates the extent to which CTE mismatch drives the evolution of interface cracking and stress concentration fields in MMCs. The study is based on a review of existing literature and experimental findings, focusing on the mechanisms of degradation and the impact on mechanical properties.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Background

Metal Matrix Composites (MMCs) are composite materials consisting of a metal matrix reinforced with fibers, particles, or whiskers. The primary advantage of MMCs is their ability to combine the high strength and stiffness of the reinforcement with the ductility and formability of the metal matrix. However, the CTE mismatch between the reinforcement and the matrix can lead to significant residual stresses and interface cracking, especially under thermal cycling conditions. This mismatch is a critical factor in the degradation of mechanical properties and the overall performance of MMCs.

### 1.2. Objectives

The primary objective of this report is to analyze the extent to which the CTE mismatch in MMCs drives the evolution of interface cracking and stress concentration fields under thermal cycling fatigue. The report will also discuss the mechanisms of degradation and the impact on the mechanical properties of the composites.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1. Thermal Expansion Mismatch and Residual Stresses

The CTE mismatch between the fiber and the matrix in MMCs can lead to the development of residual stresses during the cooling process from the processing temperature to room temperature. These residual stresses can be significant and can cause various forms of damage, including interface cracking and delamination. Wu and Han [1] studied the thermal fatigue behavior of Al/SiCp composites and found that thermal cycling resulted in interface degradation and a decrease in mechanical properties. The degradation mechanisms were associated with debonding and cracking of the fiber/matrix interfaces for composites with weak interfaces, while for strong interfaces, the degradation was linked to plastic deformation of the matrix and the formation of interfacial reaction products.

### 2.2. Interface Degradation Mechanisms

The degradation mechanisms in MMCs under thermal cycling can be broadly categorized into two types: mechanical and chemical. Mechanical degradation includes debonding, cracking, and plastic deformation, while chemical degradation involves the formation of interfacial reaction products. Wei [2] found that the SiC cracking and interface debonding between SiC and the matrix are the main mechanisms when the crack-tip interacts with SiC. The crack propagation speed is influenced by the variation in matrix microhardness, and the micro-cracks at the SiC/matrix interface can coalesce with the main cracks, leading to rapid crack propagation.

### 2.3. Stress Concentration and Interface Cracking

The CTE mismatch can lead to significant stress concentration at the fiber/matrix interface, which can initiate and propagate cracks. Pan [3] reported that the micro-cracks at the SiC/matrix interface, induced by repeated thermal cycles, can coalesce with the main cracks, leading to rapid crack propagation. The stress concentration is particularly high in regions with large SiC particles, and reducing the size of the SiC particles can increase the resistance to thermal cycling crack.

### 2.4. Multilayer Interfacial Transition Layers

Recent studies have shown that multilayer interfacial transition layers can effectively mitigate stress concentration at composite interfaces, thereby enhancing the efficient transfer of loads. These layers can be designed to have a gradient modulus, which can significantly improve the interfacial shear strength, interlaminar shear strength, and flexural strength of the composite. For example, composites with a modulus gradient rate of −1.4 exhibited a 95.2% increase in interfacial shear strength, a 23.4% increase in interlaminar shear strength, and a 32.1% increase in flexural strength compared to conventional composites [4].

## 3. Experimental Findings

### 3.1. Thermal Cycling Tests

Several studies have conducted thermal cycling tests on MMCs to investigate the evolution of interface cracking and stress concentration. Wu and Han [1] examined the transverse sections of Al/SiCp composites before and after thermal cycling using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and electron-probe micro-analysis (EPMA). They found that for composites with weak interfaces, the degradation mechanisms were associated with debonding and cracking of the fiber/matrix interfaces, while for strong interfaces, the degradation was linked to plastic deformation of the matrix and the formation of interfacial reaction products.

### 3.2. Residual Stress Measurements

Residual stress measurements in MMCs have been conducted using various techniques, including X-ray diffraction and neutron diffraction. Requena [5] used neutron diffraction techniques to measure the stress concentration in 25 vol% SiC/2124 Al-alloy composites caused by thermal cycles. They found that the thermal stress was partially released in the creep process above 90°C, which was hindered by submicron SiC particles.

### 3.3. Interface Design Strategies

To mitigate the effects of CTE mismatch, several interface design strategies have been proposed. Arsenault et al. [6] developed a computer program to optimize the properties of the interface material to minimize the residual stress in the matrix. The optimal interface thermal expansion coefficient was found to be between the values of the fiber and the matrix, and the interface thickness was maximized to reduce the residual stress.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Impact of CTE Mismatch on Interface Cracking

The CTE mismatch between the fiber and the matrix in MMCs is a significant factor in the evolution of interface cracking and stress concentration fields under thermal cycling. The mismatch leads to the development of high residual stresses, which can cause debonding, cracking, and plastic deformation of the matrix. The extent of interface cracking is influenced by the strength of the interface, with weak interfaces being more susceptible to debonding and strong interfaces being more prone to plastic deformation.

### 4.2. Stress Concentration and Crack Propagation

The stress concentration at the fiber/matrix interface is a critical factor in the initiation and propagation of cracks. The stress concentration is particularly high in regions with large reinforcement particles, and reducing the size of the particles can increase the resistance to thermal cycling crack. The coalescence of micro-cracks at the interface with the main cracks can lead to rapid crack propagation, significantly reducing the mechanical properties of the composite.

### 4.3. Mitigation Strategies

To mitigate the effects of CTE mismatch, several strategies have been proposed, including the use of multilayer interfacial transition layers and the optimization of interface material properties. Multilayer interfacial transition layers with a gradient modulus can effectively reduce stress concentration and enhance the efficient transfer of loads. Optimizing the interface thermal expansion coefficient and thickness can also minimize the residual stress in the matrix, thereby improving the overall performance of the composite.

## 5. Conclusion

The CTE mismatch between the fiber and the matrix in MMCs is a significant driver of interface cracking and stress concentration fields under thermal cycling fatigue. The mismatch leads to the development of high residual stresses, which can cause various forms of degradation, including debonding, cracking, and plastic deformation. The extent of interface cracking is influenced by the strength of the interface, with weak interfaces being more susceptible to debonding and strong interfaces being more prone to plastic deformation. Stress concentration at the fiber/matrix interface is a critical factor in the initiation and propagation of cracks, and reducing the size of the reinforcement particles can increase the resistance to thermal cycling crack. To mitigate the effects of CTE mismatch, strategies such as the use of multilayer interfacial transition layers and the optimization of interface material properties are essential.
