## Abstract

Hydrogen is increasingly recognized as a key player in the transition to a low-carbon economy, particularly in industrial sectors such as steel, cement, and chemicals. ## Introduction

Hydrogen has the potential to significantly reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in industrial processes by providing a low-carbon alternative to fossil fuels. However, the economic feasibility of hydrogen as a thermal management solution is constrained by several factors, including the high costs of production, storage, and transportation. One critical aspect that influences these costs is the enthalpy balance in thermodynamic models, which affects the efficiency and energy requirements of hydrogen systems.

## Enthalpy Balance in Hydrogen Systems

### Thermodynamic Principles

Enthalpy, \( H \), is a measure of the total heat content of a system and is defined as:
\[ H = U + PV \]
where \( U \) is the internal energy, \( P \) is the pressure, and \( V \) is the volume. In the context of hydrogen systems, the enthalpy balance is crucial for understanding the energy inputs and outputs during production, storage, and utilization.

### Production

Hydrogen can be produced through various methods, including electrolysis, steam methane reforming (SMR), and autothermal reforming (ATR). Each method has different enthalpy requirements and efficiencies. For example, electrolysis, which splits water into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity, is an energy-intensive process. The enthalpy balance in electrolysis systems is influenced by the efficiency of the electrolyzer and the source of electricity. Renewable energy sources, such as wind and solar, can reduce the overall enthalpy input, making the process more sustainable and potentially cost-effective [1].

### Storage

Hydrogen storage is a significant challenge due to its low volumetric energy density. Common storage methods include high-pressure gaseous storage, cryogenic liquid storage, and solid-state storage using metal hydrides. Each method has different enthalpy requirements and associated costs. For instance, cryogenic storage requires significant energy for liquefaction and maintaining low temperatures, which affects the overall enthalpy balance and economic viability [2].

### Transportation

Transporting hydrogen from production sites to end-users is another critical aspect. Pipeline transport, trucking, and shipping are the primary methods, each with its own enthalpy and cost considerations. Pipeline transport is generally more efficient for large-scale and long-distance transport, but it requires significant initial investment and maintenance costs. Trucking and shipping are more flexible but less efficient in terms of energy consumption and enthalpy balance [3].

## Economic Boundaries of Hydrogen Thermal Management

### Cost Analysis

The economic boundaries of hydrogen thermal management are primarily determined by the costs of production, storage, and transportation. These costs are influenced by the enthalpy balance and the efficiency of the systems involved.

#### Production Costs

The cost of producing hydrogen varies depending on the method used. Green hydrogen, produced through electrolysis using renewable energy, is currently more expensive than gray hydrogen, produced through SMR. However, the cost of green hydrogen is expected to decrease as renewable energy costs decline and electrolyzer efficiency improves [4]. The enthalpy balance in electrolysis systems plays a crucial role in determining the overall production cost. For example, a more efficient electrolyzer can reduce the energy input required, thereby lowering the production cost.

#### Storage Costs

The cost of storing hydrogen is influenced by the method used and the enthalpy requirements. High-pressure gaseous storage is relatively inexpensive but has lower energy density, while cryogenic liquid storage is more expensive but has higher energy density. The enthalpy balance in storage systems affects the energy required for compression and liquefaction, which in turn impacts the overall cost. For instance, the energy required to compress hydrogen to 700 bar can be significant, affecting the economic viability of high-pressure storage [5].

#### Transportation Costs

The cost of transporting hydrogen is influenced by the method used and the distance involved. Pipeline transport is generally more cost-effective for large-scale and long-distance transport, but it requires significant initial investment. Trucking and shipping are more flexible but less efficient in terms of energy consumption. The enthalpy balance in transportation systems affects the energy required for compression and liquefaction, which impacts the overall cost. For example, the energy required to compress hydrogen for trucking can be significant, affecting the economic viability of this method [6].

In the steel sector, hydrogen can be used as a reductant to replace carbon in the iron reduction process. The enthalpy balance in hydrogen-based steel production systems affects the energy requirements and costs. In the cement sector, hydrogen can be used to replace fossil fuels in the kiln process.
