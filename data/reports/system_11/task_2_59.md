# Research Report: Balancing Money Supply Control and Risk Isolation in CBDC Smart Contract Policy Tools

## Abstract
Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) are poised to revolutionize the financial landscape by offering a digital form of fiat currency issued and backed by central banks. One of the key features of CBDCs is their programmability, which allows for the implementation of smart contracts to control the use and distribution of the currency. This report explores whether CBDC smart contract policy tools effectively balance money supply control and risk isolation, or if they risk amplifying policy accidents. The analysis is based on a review of recent literature and expert opinions on CBDCs, focusing on the security, regulatory, and economic implications of programmable money.

## 1. Introduction
Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) are digital forms of a country's fiat currency, issued and backed by the central bank. They offer several advantages, including more effective and safer payment methods, reduced transaction times, and enhanced financial inclusion. However, the introduction of CBDCs also presents significant challenges, particularly in the areas of security, regulatory compliance, and economic stability. One of the most innovative features of CBDCs is their programmability, which allows for the implementation of smart contracts to control the use and distribution of the currency. This report investigates whether these smart contract policy tools can effectively balance money supply control and risk isolation, or if they introduce new risks that could amplify policy accidents.

## 2. Security Considerations for CBDCs
### 2.1 General Security Requirements
Security is a paramount concern for CBDCs, as it is for any financial system. The security requirements for CBDCs include:
- **Prevention of Counterfeiting and Fraud**: Measures must be in place to prevent users from copying, modifying, or double-spending the same asset. Cryptographic techniques, such as digital signatures, are essential for ensuring the authenticity and integrity of CBDC transactions.
- **Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Countering the Financing of Terrorism (CFT)**: CBDCs must incorporate mechanisms to prevent illicit financial activities. This includes the ability to trace transactions and identify users, while balancing privacy concerns.
- **Consumer Protection**: CBDCs should be designed to protect end-user assets and sensitive personal information. Secure applications and robust security protocols are necessary to prevent unauthorized access and data breaches.
- **Financial Stability**: CBDCs must not undermine the stability of the financial system. This includes measures to prevent runs on financial institutions and to ensure the smooth operation of the payment system.

### 2.2 Specific Security Challenges
- **Phishing Attacks and Malware**: Users may be targeted by phishing attacks and malware to obtain credentials or private keys. Central banks must implement robust information security programs to mitigate these risks.
- **Malicious Insiders**: Insiders with privileged access may attempt to steal assets or manipulate the system. Strong internal controls and regular audits are essential to prevent such activities.
- **Nation-State Espionage**: Nation-states may engage in espionage to access critical infrastructure or disrupt the financial system. Legal frameworks and international cooperation are necessary to address these threats.

## 3. Programmability and Smart Contracts in CBDCs
### 3.1 Definition and Benefits
Programmability in CBDCs refers to the ability to embed rules and conditions into the digital currency, allowing for automated and condition-based transactions. Smart contracts are self-executing contracts with the terms of the agreement directly written into code. The benefits of programmable CBDCs include:
- **Enhanced Functionality**: Users can set rules for their payments, such as time-limited transactions, conditional payments, and automatic tax deductions.
- **Efficiency**: Smart contracts can automate processes, reducing the need for intermediaries and speeding up transactions.
- **Policy Implementation**: Central banks can use programmability to implement monetary policy tools, such as setting interest rates on CBDC deposits or varying the supply of CBDCs.

### 3.2 Policy Tools and Their Effects
- **Tiered Remuneration**: This tool involves setting different interest rates on CBDC deposits based on the amount held. It can discourage a flight to safety during times of stress but may also discourage the use of CBDC as a medium of exchange.
- **Access Limits**: Limiting the amount of CBDC that can be held or transferred can reduce the risk of runs on financial institutions. However, this can also harm the medium of exchange role of CBDC and increase the incentives for regulatory arbitrage.
- **Transaction Size Limits**: Similar to access limits, this tool can discourage large-scale transactions that may pose financial stability risks.
- **Holding Limits**: Setting limits on the amount of CBDC that can be held can prevent hoarding and ensure that the currency remains widely available. However, if the limits are not set appropriately, they can be ineffective or even amplify runs.
- **Convertibility Limits**: Allowing for the conversion of CBDC to other forms of money can help manage demand and prevent runs. However, if these limits are not well-calibrated, they can lead to pre-emptive runs and the creation of secondary markets.

### 3.3 Risks and Challenges
- **Technical Vulnerabilities**: The use of smart contracts and programmability introduces new technical vulnerabilities, such as the risk of software failures, malicious attacks, and errors in the code.
- **Legal and Regulatory Uncertainty**: The legal status and treatment of programmable CBDCs are not well-defined, leading to uncertainty about their acceptance and treatment in legal and regulatory frameworks.
- **Centralization and Control**: Programmable CBDCs may blur the boundaries between central bank independence and monetary policy execution, potentially leading to fiscal dominance and undermining inflation control.
- **Privacy and Surveillance**: The ability to trace transactions and control the use of CBDCs can raise concerns about privacy and the potential for state surveillance.
- **Operational Risks**: The reliance on technology and third-party operators can introduce operational risks, such as the failure of validators or nodes, which can compromise the integrity of the entire network.

## 4. Balancing Money Supply Control and Risk Isolation
### 4.1 Policy Objectives and Design Choices
The design of CBDCs and their programmability must align with the policy objectives of the central bank, including:
- **Monetary Stability**: Ensuring that the CBDC does not undermine the stability of the financial system or the central bank's ability to control the money supply.
- **Financial Inclusion**: Promoting access to financial services for all citizens, particularly those without traditional bank accounts.
- **Security and Privacy**: Protecting user assets and personal information while maintaining the integrity of the system.
- **Regulatory Compliance**: Ensuring that CBDCs comply with existing legal and regulatory frameworks, including AML/CFT requirements.

### 4.2 Institutional Guardrails
To balance money supply control and risk isolation, central banks can implement the following institutional guardrails:
- **Legal Limits on Fiscal Usage**: Establishing legal limits on the fiscal usage of CBDCs to prevent fiscal dominance and ensure that monetary policy remains independent.
- **Legislative and Judicial Oversight**: Providing legislative and judicial oversight of programmable spending tools to ensure that they are used in a transparent and accountable manner.
- **Public Reporting**: Requiring public reporting on monetary-fiscal coordination to enhance transparency and accountability.
- **Technical Separation**: Implementing technical separation of wallets and functions to prevent the misuse of CBDCs and ensure that the system remains secure.

### 4.3 Risk Management Strategies
- **Robust Security Protocols**: Implementing robust security protocols, including encryption, multi-factor authentication, and regular security audits, to protect against cyber threats.
- **Decentralized Architecture**: Using a decentralized architecture, such as distributed ledger technology (DLT), to reduce the risk of central points of failure and enhance resilience.
- **Interoperability**: Ensuring that CBDCs are interoperable with existing financial systems and can be seamlessly integrated into the broader payment ecosystem.
- **User Control**: Allowing users to control the programmability of their CBDCs, such as setting rules for their payments, while maintaining the ability to enforce AML/CFT controls.

## 5. Conclusion
CBDC smart contract policy tools have the potential to enhance the functionality and efficiency of digital currencies while providing central banks with new tools to implement monetary policy. However, these tools also introduce new risks and challenges, particularly in the areas of security, regulatory compliance, and economic stability. To balance money supply control and risk isolation, central banks must carefully design and implement CBDCs with a focus on institutional guardrails, robust security protocols, and user control. By doing so, they can ensure that CBDCs achieve their policy objectives while minimizing the risk of policy accidents.
