# In Emergency Triage, How Does the Shock Index Balance Early Recognition of Shock Against the Risk of Over-Intervention When Assessing Vital Signs and Organ Perfusion?

## Abstract

The shock index (SI), defined as the ratio of heart rate to systolic blood pressure (\(\text{SI} = \frac{\text{HR}}{\text{SBP}}\)), is a widely studied and utilized metric in emergency triage. This report examines the balance between early recognition of shock and the risk of over-intervention when using the shock index to assess vital signs and organ perfusion. We review the literature to evaluate the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of the shock index in various clinical settings, including trauma, sepsis, and pediatric populations. The report also discusses the limitations and potential improvements of the shock index, such as the modified shock index (MSI) and age-adjusted shock index (SIPA).

## Introduction

Emergency triage is a critical process in healthcare, particularly in emergency departments (EDs), where the goal is to identify and prioritize patients who require immediate medical attention. Traditional vital signs, such as heart rate (HR) and systolic blood pressure (SBP), are essential for assessing a patient's hemodynamic status. However, these signs may remain within normal limits during the early stages of shock, leading to underdiagnosis and delayed treatment. The shock index (SI) has emerged as a valuable tool for early recognition of shock, but its use also carries the risk of over-intervention. This report aims to explore how the shock index balances these two critical aspects of emergency triage.

## Shock Index: Definition and Calculation

The shock index (SI) is defined as the ratio of heart rate (HR) to systolic blood pressure (SBP):

\[
\text{SI} = \frac{\text{HR}}{\text{SBP}}
\]

A normal SI ranges from 0.5 to 0.7. An SI greater than 0.9 is often considered indicative of shock or hemodynamic instability. The SI is a simple and quick calculation that can be performed at the bedside, making it a valuable tool in emergency settings.

## Early Recognition of Shock

### Trauma

In trauma patients, the shock index has been shown to be an accurate predictor of mortality and the need for emergent interventions. A retrospective chart review of 2,445 patients admitted to a Level I trauma center over a 5-year period found that the shock index is a reliable prehospital predictor of mortality [1]. However, the panel noted that the shock index does not significantly improve field identification of seriously injured patients beyond existing physiologic measures. The use of the shock index in the field remains unclear, but it may become more applicable as vital signs and triage criteria are routinely recorded and collected on mobile devices [1].

### Sepsis

In sepsis, the shock index has been evaluated as a predictor of adverse outcomes. A study of 274 febrile adult patients found that an SI of ≥1 had a sensitivity of 90% for predicting sepsis, 85% for ICU admission, and 100% for 28-day mortality [2]. The modified shock index (MSI), calculated as HR divided by mean arterial pressure (MAP), showed even higher specificity (59%–100%) for all outcomes of interest. These findings suggest that the shock index and its derivatives can be useful in identifying patients with sepsis and predicting adverse outcomes [2].

### Pediatric Populations

In pediatric patients, the shock index has been adapted to account for age-related differences in vital signs. The pediatric adjusted shock index (SIPA) has been shown to be more reliable than standard adult cutoffs in identifying severely injured children and predicting in-hospital mortality [3]. A prospective study of 386 pediatric patients with blunt liver and spleen injury (BLSI) found that SIPA improved specificity for all parameters compared to SI >0.9, potentially reducing over-triage in the initial phase of resuscitation [3].

## Risk of Over-Intervention

### Sensitivity and Specificity

The shock index's sensitivity and specificity are crucial for balancing early recognition of shock against the risk of over-intervention. A study of 3,380 patients found that an SI threshold of 0.72, determined by Youden's index, had a sensitivity of 0.53 and a specificity of 0.67 for identifying "Priority 1" patients [4]. At a threshold of 1.00, the sensitivity dropped to 0.19, but the specificity increased to 0.92. While the higher threshold had high specificity, its low sensitivity makes it unsuitable for use in isolation as a triage tool [4].

### Over-Triage and Under-Triage

The American College of Surgeons (ACS) defines an optimal triage tool as having over-triage rates <35% and under-triage rates <5%. The shock index, when used with appropriate cutoffs, can achieve these goals. For example, a study of 333,995 pediatric trauma patients found that age-adjusted SI cutoffs (13.3 for ages 0–9, 18.4 for ages 10–14, and 20.9 for ages 15–18) achieved a balance of 30.94% over-triage and 9.17% under-triage [5]. These findings suggest that the shock index, when adjusted for age, can effectively balance early recognition of shock with the risk of over-intervention.

## Limitations and Future Directions

### Limitations

Despite its utility, the shock index has several limitations. It is a single-point measurement and may not capture the dynamic changes in a patient's condition over time. Additionally, the shock index can be influenced by factors such as age, medication, and underlying medical conditions, which may affect its accuracy. The use of the shock index in the field also requires accurate and timely measurement of vital signs, which can be challenging in prehospital settings.

### Future Directions

Future research should focus on the following areas:

1. **Longitudinal Studies**: Evaluating the shock index over time to identify trends and changes in a patient's condition.
2. **Combined Metrics**: Combining the shock index with other physiological and clinical markers to improve the accuracy of triage.
3. **Technology Integration**: Developing mobile and wearable devices that can automatically calculate and monitor the shock index in real-time.
4. **Age- and Condition-Specific Cutoffs**: Establishing more precise cutoffs for different age groups and medical conditions to improve the specificity and sensitivity of the shock index.

## Conclusion

The shock index is a valuable tool in emergency triage for early recognition of shock, particularly in trauma and sepsis. However, its use must be balanced against the risk of over-intervention. Age-adjusted and modified versions of the shock index, such as SIPA and MSI, can improve specificity and reduce over-triage. Future research and technological advancements will further enhance the utility of the shock index in emergency care.
