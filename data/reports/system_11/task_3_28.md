# Institutional Guarantees for Balancing Economic Benefits with Cultural Heritage in Creative Industries

## Abstract
This research report explores the institutional mechanisms that ensure the long-term value chains in creative industries (CCIs) balance economic benefits with cultural heritage. The analysis is grounded in a review of key literature and policy frameworks, focusing on the role of cultural policies, economic support measures, and cross-sectoral collaboration. The report highlights the importance of viewing culture as an economic and social investment, mainstreaming culture in broader policy agendas, and improving the evidence base for informed decision-making. Case studies from Japan, Scotland, and France illustrate practical applications of these principles in cultural heritage preservation and sustainable development.

## 1. Introduction
The cultural and creative industries (CCIs) are recognized as significant contributors to economic growth, social cohesion, and cultural heritage preservation. However, balancing economic benefits with the preservation of cultural heritage remains a complex challenge. This report examines the institutional guarantees that can ensure this balance, drawing on insights from various sources and case studies.

## 2. Theoretical Framework
### 2.1 Value Chain in Cultural Heritage
The cultural heritage value chain is a relational ecosystem that spans recognition, creation, preservation, curation, interpretation, and engagement. Each stage involves the contributions of individuals, institutions, and narratives to the sustainability and meaning-making of cultural heritage (Takei, 2025).

### 2.2 Economic and Social Impact of CCIs
CCIs are among the fastest-growing sectors globally, contributing significantly to job creation, economic growth, and social inclusion. According to UNESCO, the cultural and creative sector accounts for 6.1% of the global economy, generating annual revenues of US$ 2,250 billion and nearly 30 million jobs worldwide (UNESCO, 2023).

### 2.3 Policy Frameworks
Several policy frameworks emphasize the importance of integrating culture into broader development agendas. The OECD and UNESCO advocate for viewing culture as an economic and social investment, culture-proofing policies, and mainstreaming culture in areas such as social cohesion, innovation, health, and sustainable development (OECD, 2021; UNESCO, 2023).

## 3. Institutional Guarantees
### 3.1 Viewing Culture as an Economic and Social Investment
Policymakers must recognize culture as an investment rather than a cost. This involves allocating resources to support cultural and creative sectors, fostering innovation, and promoting sustainable economic growth (OECD, 2021).

### 3.2 Culture-Proofing Policies
Culture-proofing policies ensure that creative professionals and firms have equal access to employment, innovation, and business support measures. This includes adapting policies on employment, social protection, and entrepreneurship to the unique needs of the cultural and creative sectors (OECD, 2021).

### 3.3 Mainstreaming Culture in Broader Policy Agendas
Integrating culture into wider policy agendas, such as social cohesion, innovation, health, and sustainable development, can enhance the overall impact of cultural policies. This requires collaboration between government entities, local authorities, and other stakeholders (OECD, 2021).

### 3.4 Improving the Evidence Base
Enhancing internationally comparable statistics and the evidence base on the scale, scope, and impacts of cultural and creative sectors is crucial for informed decision-making. This involves collecting and analyzing data on the economic, social, and cultural contributions of CCIs (OECD, 2021).

### 3.5 Building Capacity
Building the capacity of national and local governments to integrate culture into broader economic and social development strategies is essential. This includes training and support for policymakers, cultural professionals, and local communities (OECD, 2021).

## 4. Case Studies
### 4.1 Takaoka Craft Ichibamachi (Takaoka, Japan)
Takaoka Craft Ichibamachi is a local initiative that engages designers, artisans, and local residents in a collaborative storytelling process. This project has a strong regional identity and a significant impact on tourism, demonstrating how cultural heritage can be preserved and promoted through community involvement (Takei, 2025).

### 4.2 Glasgow Museums (Glasgow, Scotland)
Glasgow Museums play a vital role in the cultural heritage value chain by preserving and curating historical artifacts and promoting educational programs. The museums' curatorial narratives and visitor engagement strategies contribute to sustainable cultural tourism and social inclusion (Takei, 2025).

### 4.3 Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art (Nice, France)
The Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art (MAMAC) in Nice is an institution that supports the creation and interpretation of modern and contemporary art. The museum's programs and exhibitions foster innovation and creativity, contributing to the cultural and economic development of the region (Takei, 2025).

## 5. Conclusion
The institutional guarantees that ensure the long-term value chains in creative industries balance economic benefits with cultural heritage include viewing culture as an economic and social investment, culture-proofing policies, mainstreaming culture in broader policy agendas, improving the evidence base, and building capacity. These mechanisms are essential for fostering sustainable development and preserving cultural heritage. Case studies from Japan, Scotland, and France illustrate the practical applications of these principles, demonstrating the potential for cultural and creative sectors to drive economic, social, and cultural growth.
