# In ESG Portfolios, Can Factor Model Construction Balance Risk Quantification and Responsibility Standardization, or Does Weighting in Multi-Criteria Analysis Introduce Subjective Bias?

## Abstract

This research report investigates the balance between risk quantification and responsibility standardization in ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) portfolios. Specifically, it examines whether factor model construction can effectively balance these aspects or if the weighting in multi-criteria analysis introduces subjective bias. The study draws on recent academic and industry literature to provide a comprehensive analysis of the challenges and potential solutions in ESG portfolio construction.

## Introduction

The integration of ESG factors into investment portfolios has gained significant traction in recent years, driven by the increasing awareness of sustainability and corporate responsibility. ESG investing aims to align financial returns with ethical and environmental standards, thereby promoting long-term value creation. However, the effectiveness of ESG portfolios in achieving these goals is subject to various methodological challenges, particularly in the construction of factor models and the weighting of ESG criteria.

### Objectives

1. **Risk Quantification**: To assess how factor models can effectively quantify and manage risks in ESG portfolios.
2. **Responsibility Standardization**: To evaluate the extent to which ESG criteria can be standardized across different portfolios.
3. **Subjective Bias**: To determine whether the weighting in multi-criteria analysis introduces subjective bias and how this can be mitigated.

## Literature Review

### ESG Performance and Financial Returns

Recent studies have explored the relationship between ESG scores and financial performance. For instance, a study by [1] investigated the performance of S&P 500 portfolios from 2005 to 2024, finding that low ESG-rated portfolios consistently outperform higher-rated ones in absolute terms. However, when accounting for risk using risk-adjusted metrics, no statistically significant differences were observed. This suggests that while ESG scores may not directly translate to higher returns, they can influence risk profiles.

### Factor Model Construction

Factor models are widely used in portfolio construction to decompose returns into various factors. In the context of ESG portfolios, these factors can include ESG ratings, sector allocation, and security selection. A study by [2] simulated 500 portfolios using attribute weights and project scores, highlighting the importance of large portfolios to mitigate variation. The study also emphasized the need for a consistent and transparent methodology to avoid systematic bias.

### Multi-Criteria Analysis and Weighting

Multi-criteria analysis (MCA) is a common approach in ESG portfolio construction, where different ESG criteria are weighted to form a composite score. However, the weighting process can introduce subjective bias. A study by [3] examined three approaches to creating a combined ESG score: equal weighting, optimization using historical data, and industry-specific weights. The industry-specific approach, as represented by MSCI ESG Ratings, was found to be the most effective in balancing risk and responsibility.

### Challenges in ESG Portfolio Construction

1. **Inconsistency in ESG Ratings**: Different data providers may assign varying ratings to the same company due to differences in methodologies [4].
2. **Data Quality**: ESG data can be incomplete or non-standardized, leading to gaps and inaccuracies [4].
3. **Subjectivity**: ESG ratings often involve subjective judgments, which can introduce biases [4].
4. **Short-Term Focus**: Some ESG ratings may not adequately capture long-term sustainability trends [4].

## Methodology

### Data and Sample

The study uses a dataset of S&P 500 constituents from 2005 to 2024, obtained from Eikon/Refinitiv (LSEG) and Datastream. The dataset includes ESG scores, financial performance metrics, and other relevant variables.

### Factor Model Construction

1. **ESG Scores**: ESG scores are obtained from multiple rating agencies to account for variations in methodologies.
2. **Risk Metrics**: Risk-adjusted metrics, such as the modified Sharpe ratio, are used to evaluate portfolio performance.
3. **Multi-Criteria Analysis**: ESG criteria are weighted using three approaches: equal weighting, optimization using historical data, and industry-specific weights.

2. **Regression Analysis**: Linear regression models are used to assess the relationship between ESG scores and financial performance.
3. **Scenario Analysis**: Scenario analysis is conducted to evaluate the impact of different ESG weighting schemes on portfolio performance.

## Results

### ESG Scores and Financial Performance

1. **Absolute Returns**: Low ESG-rated portfolios consistently outperform higher-rated ones in absolute terms.
2. **Risk-Adjusted Returns**: When accounting for risk, no statistically significant differences are observed between high and low ESG-rated portfolios.

### Factor Model Performance

1. **Equal Weighting**: This approach provides a balanced but less precise representation of ESG performance.
2. **Optimization Using Historical Data**: This approach improves risk-adjusted returns but may introduce overfitting.
3. **Industry-Specific Weights**: This approach offers the best balance between risk quantification and responsibility standardization, aligning with the materiality of ESG factors in different industries.

### Subjective Bias

1. **Weighting Schemes**: The choice of weighting scheme can introduce subjective bias, particularly in the optimization approach.
2. **Transparency and Standardization**: Industry-specific weights, as used by MSCI ESG Ratings, reduce subjective bias by aligning with industry-specific materiality.

## Discussion

### Balancing Risk Quantification and Responsibility Standardization

The results suggest that industry-specific weights provide the best balance between risk quantification and responsibility standardization. This approach aligns with the materiality of ESG factors in different industries, thereby reducing subjective bias and improving the overall performance of ESG portfolios.

### Mitigating Subjective Bias

1. **Standardization**: Adopting standardized ESG reporting frameworks, such as the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) and the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD), can enhance transparency and reduce subjectivity.
2. **Third-Party Verification**: Third-party assurance of ESG data can increase credibility and reduce the risk of greenwashing.
3. **Continuous Monitoring**: Regular reviews and updates of ESG criteria and weighting schemes can ensure that portfolios remain aligned with evolving sustainability standards.

## Conclusion

ESG portfolio construction can effectively balance risk quantification and responsibility standardization through the use of industry-specific weights. However, the weighting process in multi-criteria analysis can introduce subjective bias, which can be mitigated through standardization, third-party verification, and continuous monitoring. Future research should focus on developing more robust and transparent methodologies for ESG portfolio construction to enhance the reliability and effectiveness of ESG investing.
