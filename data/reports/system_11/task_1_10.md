# Research Report: Optimizing Targeted Delivery Stability and Permeability of Nanomedicine Carriers by Controlling Size Distribution Amidst the Competition Between Serum Protein Adsorption and Cell Uptake

## Abstract

Nanomedicine carriers have emerged as a promising platform for targeted drug delivery, offering enhanced stability, solubility, and controlled release. However, the interaction of these carriers with biological systems, particularly the formation of a protein corona and subsequent cellular uptake, remains a critical challenge. This report investigates how controlling the size distribution of nanomedicine carriers can optimize targeted delivery stability and permeability, focusing on the competition between serum protein adsorption and cell uptake. The report synthesizes findings from recent studies to elucidate the role of nanoparticle size, PEGylation, and surface chemistry in modulating these interactions.

## 1. Introduction

Nanomedicine carriers, such as nanoparticles (NPs), have revolutionized drug delivery by enhancing the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of therapeutic agents. However, the success of these carriers is contingent on their ability to navigate biological barriers and achieve targeted delivery. One of the primary challenges is the formation of a protein corona upon exposure to biological fluids, which can alter the physicochemical properties of the NPs and affect their cellular uptake and biodistribution. This report explores how controlling the size distribution of NPs can optimize targeted delivery stability and permeability by balancing the competition between serum protein adsorption and cell uptake.

## 2. Nanoparticle Size and Protein Corona Formation

### 2.1. Influence of Size on Protein Corona

The size of NPs significantly influences the formation of the protein corona. Smaller NPs have a higher surface area-to-volume ratio, which can lead to increased protein adsorption. However, the nature and composition of the adsorbed proteins can vary with NP size. Studies have shown that the adsorption of serum proteins on NPs is modulated by the steric interactions between PEG chains grafted on the NP surface [1]. For instance, at low PEG densities (below 0.16 PEG/nm\(^2\)), macrophage uptake is serum-dependent, meaning it relies on the presence of adsorbed serum proteins. Conversely, at high PEG densities (above 0.64 PEG/nm\(^2\)), macrophage uptake is serum-independent, suggesting a different mechanism of interaction [2].

### 2.2. PEGylation and Protein Adsorption

PEGylation, the process of attaching polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains to the NP surface, is a widely used strategy to reduce protein adsorption and enhance the circulation time of NPs. The length and density of PEG chains play a crucial role in this process. Long PEG chains (e.g., 10 kDa) with a dense brush conformation (RF/D > 8) are more effective in inhibiting macrophage uptake and increasing systemic circulation time [3]. However, shorter PEG chains (e.g., 5 kDa) can also reduce protein adsorption, especially when they adopt a brush conformation (RF/D ∼ 1.5) [4]. The steric hindrance provided by PEG chains restricts the adsorption of proteins, thereby reducing the formation of a protein corona.

## 3. Cellular Uptake Mechanisms

### 3.1. Serum-Dependent Uptake

Serum-dependent uptake of NPs is primarily mediated by the adsorbed protein corona. The composition of the protein corona can influence the recognition and internalization of NPs by macrophages and other cells. For example, proteins from cluster A (green) adsorb preferentially at low PEG densities, leading to efficient serum-dependent uptake [2]. This mechanism is often receptor-mediated, with proteins such as albumin and immunoglobulins facilitating the interaction between NPs and cell surface receptors.

### 3.2. Serum-Independent Uptake

Serum-independent uptake, on the other hand, is less efficient but can occur at high PEG densities. This mechanism is likely receptor-independent and may involve fluid phase pinocytosis. NPs grafted with high-density PEG are often confined to large vesicles within cells, suggesting a different internalization pathway [2]. The reduced efficiency of serum-independent uptake can be advantageous for targeted delivery, as it minimizes non-specific uptake by non-target cells.

## 4. Optimizing Size Distribution for Targeted Delivery

### 4.1. Size-Dependent Protein Adsorption

The size of NPs can be optimized to control the formation of the protein corona and subsequent cellular uptake. Smaller NPs (e.g., <100 nm) experience less non-specific uptake by macrophages, making them more suitable for targeted delivery [5]. However, the size of the NP also affects the thickness of the protein corona. For example, the protein corona on NPs up to 50 nm in size is more likely to form a monolayer, while larger NPs (e.g., >100 nm) may form aggregates with proteins [6]. Therefore, optimizing the size distribution of NPs can help balance the trade-off between protein adsorption and cellular uptake.

### 4.2. PEGylation and Size Synergy

The synergy between PEGylation and NP size can further enhance targeted delivery. High-density PEGylation can reduce protein adsorption and non-specific uptake, while smaller NPs can improve targeting efficiency. For instance, NPs with a dense brush conformation of PEG (RF/D > 8) and a size of <100 nm can effectively evade macrophage recognition and achieve higher targeting specificity [3]. This combination of size and PEGylation can optimize the stability and permeability of NPs in the biological environment.

## 5. Case Studies and Applications

### 5.1. Transferrin-Conjugated Nanoparticles

Transferrin (Tf) is a single-chain glycoprotein that can be conjugated to NPs to enhance targeted delivery to cells expressing transferrin receptors. Studies have shown that transferrin-conjugated NPs exhibit significantly higher cellular uptake compared to unconjugated NPs [7]. However, the immunogenicity of macromolecular ligands like Tf can be a limitation. To overcome this, researchers have developed low molecular weight ligands and cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) to improve cellular uptake and targeting efficiency [8].

### 5.2. Stimuli-Responsive Nanocarriers

Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers are designed to release their cargo in response to specific triggers within the tumor microenvironment (TME). These triggers can include acidic pH, redox imbalance, elevated glutathione (GSH), and dysregulated enzymes. By integrating stimuli-responsive elements with optimized size and PEGylation, nanocarriers can achieve precise and controlled drug release, enhancing therapeutic efficacy and reducing side effects [9].

## 6. Conclusion

Controlling the size distribution of nanomedicine carriers is a critical factor in optimizing targeted delivery stability and permeability. The formation of a protein corona and subsequent cellular uptake are influenced by the size, PEGylation, and surface chemistry of NPs. By carefully designing NPs with the appropriate size and PEG density, it is possible to minimize non-specific uptake and enhance targeted delivery. Future research should focus on developing advanced nanocarriers that integrate multiple targeting strategies and stimuli-responsive elements to overcome biological barriers and achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes.
