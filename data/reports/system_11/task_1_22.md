# Research Report: Behavioral Game Theory in Investment Decision-Making

## Abstract

This report explores the integration of behavioral game theory, particularly prospect theory, into investment decision-making. The primary focus is on whether the behavioral game theory model balances rational equilibrium with bias correction or if the parameterization of prospect theory amplifies loss aversion effects. We analyze the implications of loss aversion and probability weighting on investment choices, drawing from various studies and theoretical frameworks. The findings suggest that while behavioral game theory can capture and correct for biases, the parameterization of prospect theory can indeed amplify loss aversion, leading to suboptimal investment decisions.

## Introduction

Traditional economic models assume that investors are rational agents who make decisions based on maximizing expected utility. However, behavioral economics and prospect theory challenge this assumption by incorporating psychological biases such as loss aversion and probability weighting. This report investigates whether the behavioral game theory model, which integrates these biases, achieves a balance between rational equilibrium and bias correction or if it amplifies loss aversion, leading to suboptimal investment decisions.

## Behavioral Game Theory and Prospect Theory

### Behavioral Game Theory

Behavioral game theory extends traditional game theory by incorporating psychological and cognitive biases. It aims to provide a more realistic model of strategic decision-making by accounting for the systematic departures from rationality observed in human behavior. Key concepts include:

- **Loss Aversion**: The tendency to prefer avoiding losses to acquiring equivalent gains.
- **Probability Weighting**: The tendency to overweight small probabilities and underweight large probabilities.

### Prospect Theory

Prospect theory, developed by Kahneman and Tversky (1979), is a psychological model of decision-making under risk. It challenges the expected utility theory by accounting for the following biases:

- **Reference Point**: Decisions are made relative to a reference point, which can be the status quo or a specific target.
- **Loss Aversion**: Losses are weighted more heavily than gains.
- **Diminishing Sensitivity**: The impact of changes in wealth decreases as the magnitude of the change increases.
- **Probability Weighting**: Small probabilities are overweighted, and large probabilities are underweighted.

## Rational Equilibrium vs. Bias Correction

### Rational Equilibrium

In traditional economic models, rational equilibrium is achieved when all agents make decisions that maximize their expected utility. This equilibrium is based on the assumption that agents are fully rational and have perfect information. However, empirical evidence suggests that investors often deviate from this rationality due to cognitive and emotional biases.

### Bias Correction

Behavioral game theory aims to correct for these biases by incorporating them into the decision-making process. For example, Benartzi and Thaler (1995) analyze the static portfolio problem of an investor who is loss-averse and finds that such investors are reluctant to allocate much to stocks, even if the expected return on the stock market is high. Barberis, Huang, and Santos (2001) extend this to a dynamic equilibrium model and find that loss aversion captures several aggregate market phenomena.

## Amplification of Loss Aversion

### Parameterization of Prospect Theory

The parameterization of prospect theory can significantly influence investment decisions. The loss aversion parameter \(\lambda\) represents the degree to which losses are weighted more heavily than gains. For instance, if \(\lambda = 2.25\) (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992), a loss of $1,000 is perceived as equivalent to a gain of $2,250.

### Impact on Investment Decisions

The parameterization of loss aversion can lead to suboptimal investment decisions. For example, a highly loss-averse investor may avoid risky investments, such as stocks, even if the expected return is high. This can result in a lower overall portfolio return. Additionally, the probability weighting function can further amplify this effect by making small probabilities of losses appear more significant.

### Empirical Evidence

Empirical studies support the idea that loss aversion and probability weighting can lead to suboptimal investment decisions. For instance, a study by Schmidt and Traub (2002) finds that the estimated values for the coefficient of loss aversion vary, but the effect of loss aversion on investment decisions is consistent. Similarly, the work of Tversky and Kahneman (1992) shows that people tend to overestimate the likelihood of low-probability events and underestimate the likelihood of high-probability events, leading to risk-averse behavior.

## Case Studies and Applications

### Energy Efficiency Investment

A study on energy efficiency investment (NBER, 2018) demonstrates the impact of loss aversion and probability weighting. The study finds that loss aversion is so strong that an enormous subsidy is required to overcome it. When the loss aversion parameter \(\lambda\) is varied, a higher value of \(\lambda\) results in a lower level of energy efficiency investment. This highlights the amplification effect of loss aversion on investment decisions.

### Stock Market Fluctuations

The reaction of investors to stock market fluctuations is another example of the amplification of loss aversion. Narrow framing, a derivative result of prospect theory, causes investors to evaluate new gambles in isolation, ignoring other relevant risks. This leads to increased sensitivity to stock market fluctuations and suboptimal investment decisions (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979).

## Conclusion

The integration of behavioral game theory and prospect theory into investment decision-making provides a more realistic model of how investors make decisions. While these models can correct for biases, the parameterization of prospect theory, particularly the loss aversion parameter, can amplify loss aversion effects. This can lead to suboptimal investment decisions, such as avoiding risky but potentially high-return investments. Future research should focus on developing strategies to mitigate the amplification of loss aversion and improve the overall efficiency of investment decisions.
