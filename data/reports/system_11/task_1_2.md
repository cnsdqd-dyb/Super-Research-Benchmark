# Research Report: Influence of Local Chemical Ordering on Dislocation Motion and Twin Boundary Energy in High-Entropy Alloys

## Abstract
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) are complex concentrated solid solutions that exhibit unique microstructures and mechanical properties. One of the key structural features of these alloys is the development of chemical short-range orders (CSROs) and local chemical ordering (LCO). This report investigates how these local chemical structures influence the interaction mechanisms between dislocation motion and twin boundary energy during plastic deformation. Through a review of recent studies and computational simulations, we explore the roles of CSROs and LCO in enhancing the hardness and mechanical performance of HEAs, particularly in the context of dislocation behavior and twin boundary interactions.

## Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) are multi-principal element alloys that contain more than four elements in equimolar or nearly equimolar ratios. The high configurational entropy in these alloys leads to unique microstructures and mechanical properties, such as high strength, ductility, and resistance to deformation. One of the distinguishing features of HEAs and MEAs is the presence of chemical short-range orders (CSROs) and local chemical ordering (LCO), which are structural constituents that differ from traditional alloys. These local chemical structures play a crucial role in the mechanical behavior of HEAs, particularly in dislocation motion and twin boundary interactions.

## Local Chemical Ordering in HEAs
### Definition and Formation
Local chemical ordering (LCO) refers to the preferential arrangement of atoms in the first and next couple of nearest-neighbor atomic shells, leading to the formation of short-range ordered regions within the alloy. These regions are characterized by the preference or avoidance of certain elemental species, which can significantly affect the mechanical properties of the alloy. The formation of LCO is driven by enthalpic interactions between the elements, which can lead to the development of complex microstructures.

### Theoretical and Computational Models
Theoretical models and computational simulations have been used to predict and understand the formation and behavior of LCO in HEAs. These models often rely on the conventional concept of short-range ordering, where the preference or avoidance of elemental species is considered. However, the atomic-scale structural image of LCO is still not fully understood, and further research is needed to elucidate the exact mechanisms of LCO formation and its impact on the mechanical properties of HEAs.

## Influence of LCO on Dislocation Motion
### Dislocation Nucleation and Propagation
LCO can significantly influence the nucleation and propagation of dislocations in HEAs. The local compositional fluctuations caused by LCO can lead to inhomogeneous nucleation of dislocations at low stress, which is different from the high stress-driven homogeneous nucleation observed in pure BCC metals. Additionally, LCO can cause intermittent slipping of screw dislocations by the trapping-detrapping mechanism, reducing the moving rate of edge dislocations.

### Dislocation Cross-Slip and Jog Formation
LCO can also affect the cross-slip and jog formation of dislocations. The rugged atomic and energy landscape created by LCO can increase the activation barriers for dislocation motion, leading to new twists in defect energetics. This can result in the formation of dislocation jogs and kinks, which can further influence the mechanical behavior of the alloy.

## Interaction with Twin Boundaries
### Twin Boundary Formation and Energy
Twin boundaries are another important microstructural feature in HEAs that can interact with dislocations. Twin boundaries provide a horizontal slipping path for dislocations, but they can also pin dislocations, limiting their horizontal slip. The energy of twin boundaries (γB) can be influenced by the local chemical order, leading to a wide range of γB values for different LCO configurations.

### Twinning and Mechanical Properties
The interaction between twin boundaries and dislocations can significantly affect the mechanical properties of HEAs. Twin boundaries can effectively block dislocation propagation, contributing to hardening. However, in the later stages of plastic deformation, twin boundaries can cause a softening effect due to the formation of steps and partial slip, which can become new nucleation sites for dislocations. This complex interaction between twin boundaries and dislocations can lead to a balance between hardening and softening, influencing the overall mechanical performance of the alloy.

## Experimental Evidence and Case Studies
### CoCrNi Medium-Entropy Alloy (MEA)
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of nanoindentation on CoCrNi MEA have shown that both LCO and twin boundaries enhance the hardness of the alloy. The concurrent strengthening effect of LCO and twin boundaries is more pronounced at different temperatures, with a stronger improvement in hardness observed at 70 K, 300 K, and 700 K. The plastic zones underneath the indentation reveal that both LCO and twin boundaries inhibit dislocation extension, leading to a more localized and controlled deformation.

### TiZrNbVAl Lightweight High-Entropy Alloy (LHEA)
The addition of oxygen to TiZrNbVAl LHEA has been shown to regulate LCO, leading to an increase in yield strength from 787.8 MPa to 1029.5 MPa. The microstructure evolution indicates that LCO promotes the transition of dislocation from plane slip to multistage slip, improving the homogenizing deformation ability and ductility from 12.8% to 18.5%.

### CrCoNi and CoCrFeNi High-Entropy Alloys
High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and atom probe tomography (APT) studies on CrCoNi and CoCrFeNi HEAs with (Al/Ti) additions have revealed a planar-slip-induced strain-hardening mechanism. This mechanism operates at higher stresses compared to transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) and twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) mechanisms, leading to a significant increase in the work hardening rate and tensile strength.

## Conclusion
The local chemical ordering (LCO) in high-entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) plays a crucial role in the interaction mechanisms between dislocation motion and twin boundary energy. LCO can influence dislocation nucleation, propagation, cross-slip, and jog formation, leading to a more controlled and localized deformation. Twin boundaries, in turn, can block dislocation propagation, contributing to hardening, but can also cause softening in the later stages of plastic deformation. The concurrent strengthening effect of LCO and twin boundaries can significantly enhance the mechanical properties of HEAs, making them promising materials for various high-performance applications.
