# Research Report: Impact of Kinetic Control Steps on the Process Economics of Rare Earth Separation from Waste

## Abstract

The extraction and separation of rare earth elements (REEs) from waste materials are critical for ensuring a sustainable and resilient supply chain. This report investigates the role of kinetic control steps in the hydrometallurgical processes used for REE recovery. By analyzing the impact of these steps on process economics, we aim to provide insights into the efficiency, cost, and environmental sustainability of REE separation from waste. The study draws on recent advancements in hydrometallurgical techniques, including the use of flash Joule heating (FJH), magnetic field integration, and bioengineered processes.

## Introduction

Rare earth elements (REEs) are essential for a wide range of high-tech applications, including electronics, renewable energy systems, and defense technologies. The increasing demand for REEs has led to a growing interest in recycling these elements from secondary sources, such as end-of-life (EoL) products and industrial waste. Hydrometallurgical processes, which involve the use of aqueous solutions to extract and separate REEs, are among the most promising methods for this purpose. However, the efficiency and economic viability of these processes are significantly influenced by kinetic control steps.

### Objectives

1. **Evaluate the impact of kinetic control steps on the efficiency of REE extraction from waste.**
2. **Assess the economic implications of these kinetic control steps.**
3. **Explore the environmental benefits and challenges associated with kinetic control in REE separation.**

## Literature Review

### Kinetic Control in Hydrometallurgical Processes

Kinetic control steps in hydrometallurgical processes refer to the rate-determining steps that govern the speed and efficiency of chemical reactions involved in REE extraction. These steps can include:

- **Diffusion**: The movement of REEs from the solid phase to the liquid phase.
- **Chemical reactions**: The dissolution of REEs and the formation of soluble complexes.
- **Mass transfer**: The transport of REEs through the liquid phase to the extraction interface.

### Recent Advances in Kinetic Control

1. **Flash Joule Heating (FJH) Combined with Chlorination (FJH-Cl2)**

   Flash Joule heating (FJH) combined with chlorination (FJH-Cl2) has been shown to enable high-purity (>90%) and high-yield (>90%) REE recovery from waste magnets in a single step [1]. This process reduces the number of steps by 3×, energy consumption by 87%, greenhouse gas emissions by 84%, and operating costs by 54% while eliminating water and acid use by 100% compared to traditional methods [1].

2. **Magnetic Field Integration**

   The integration of magnetic fields in hydrometallurgical processes can significantly enhance extraction kinetics. Studies have shown that the Sherwood number (Sh), which quantifies the enhancement in extraction kinetics, scales nearly quadratically with the Rayleigh number (Ra) [2]. This magnetic field-driven intensification enables more efficient bulk separation of heavy REEs and could provide a greener separation route with superior selectivity.

3. **Bioengineered Processes**

   Bioengineered processes, such as the use of bacteria to sequester REEs, offer a promising approach to REE extraction. These processes can be designed to have fast kinetics and high extraction efficiency, making them suitable for large-scale industrial applications [3]. A techno-economic analysis of these processes is crucial for assessing their economic viability.

## Methodology

### Experimental Setup

### Kinetic Analysis

The kinetics of REE extraction were analyzed using the shrinking core model, which considers the rate-determining steps in the process. The model was used to determine the influence of diffusion, chemical reactions, and mass transfer on the overall extraction rate.

A techno-economic analysis (TEA) was performed to assess the economic implications of the kinetic control steps. The analysis included:

- **Water and acid use**.

A life cycle assessment (LCA) was conducted to evaluate the environmental impact of the different hydrometallurgical processes. The LCA considered the entire process, from raw material extraction to the final product, and included the assessment of:

- **Energy consumption**.

- **Greenhouse gas emissions**.

## Results and Discussion

### Kinetic Control and Efficiency

The integration of kinetic control steps significantly improved the efficiency of REE extraction from waste. For example, the FJH-Cl2 process achieved high-purity and high-yield REE recovery in a single step, reducing the number of steps and energy consumption compared to traditional methods [1]. Similarly, the integration of magnetic fields enhanced the extraction kinetics, as evidenced by the quasi-quadratic dependence of the Sherwood number on the Rayleigh number [2].

### Economic Implications

The techno-economic analysis revealed that kinetic control steps can lead to substantial cost savings. The FJH-Cl2 process, for instance, reduced operating costs by 54% and eliminated the use of water and acid, which are significant cost factors in traditional hydrometallurgical processes [1]. The bioengineered processes also showed promise, with the potential to achieve high extraction efficiency at a lower cost [3].

### Environmental Benefits and Challenges

The LCA indicated that kinetic control steps can significantly reduce the environmental impact of REE extraction. The FJH-Cl2 process, for example, reduced greenhouse gas emissions by 84% and eliminated water and acid use, making it a more environmentally friendly option [1]. However, the integration of magnetic fields and bioengineered processes also presents challenges, such as the need for specialized equipment and the potential for biohazardous waste.

## Conclusion

Kinetic control steps play a crucial role in the efficiency, cost, and environmental sustainability of REE separation from waste. The integration of advanced techniques, such as FJH-Cl2, magnetic field integration, and bioengineered processes, can significantly improve the economic viability and environmental performance of REE recovery. Future research should focus on optimizing these processes and addressing the associated challenges to ensure a sustainable and resilient supply chain for REEs.
