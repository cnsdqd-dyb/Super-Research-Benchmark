# Research Report: How Do Stability Boundaries in Magnetohydrodynamics Constrain Fusion Reactor Design Space Regarding Beta Limits?

## Abstract

Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) stability is a critical factor in the design and operation of fusion reactors, particularly in tokamaks. The beta limit, defined as the ratio of plasma pressure to magnetic field pressure, is a key parameter that constrains the performance and operational limits of these devices. This report explores the role of MHD stability boundaries in determining the beta limits in fusion reactors, focusing on the impact of various MHD instabilities such as neoclassical tearing modes (NTMs) and resistive wall modes (RWMs). The report also discusses the potential of advanced tokamak configurations and the use of high-temperature superconductors (HTS) to enhance stability and achieve higher beta values.

## 1. Introduction

MHD stability is fundamental to the performance and operational limits of tokamaks, which are the leading candidates for magnetic confinement fusion reactors. The beta limit, given by:
\[
\beta = \frac{2 \mu_0 \langle p \rangle}{B_0^2}
\]
where \(\langle p \rangle\) is the average plasma pressure and \(B_0\) is the toroidal magnetic field at the magnetic axis, is a critical parameter that determines the maximum achievable plasma pressure. High beta values are essential for economic operation of fusion reactors, as they directly impact the fusion power and energy confinement time. However, MHD instabilities can severely limit the achievable beta, leading to disruptions and reduced performance.

## 2. MHD Instabilities and Beta Limits

### 2.1 Neoclassical Tearing Modes (NTMs)

NTMs are a class of MHD instabilities that can significantly limit the beta in tokamaks. These instabilities arise due to the interaction between the plasma pressure and the magnetic field, leading to the formation of magnetic islands. The onset of NTMs is typically associated with a critical beta value, \(\beta_{\text{NTM}}\), which is lower than the ideal MHD beta limit. Experiments in JET have shown that decreased plasma rotation can reduce the NTM onset beta limit [1]. Advanced tokamak scenarios with reversed shear can mitigate NTMs by reducing the bootstrap current, which tends to make the islands smaller [2].

### 2.2 Resistive Wall Modes (RWMs)

RWMs are another critical class of MHD instabilities that can limit the beta in tokamaks, especially in advanced-performance steady-state scenarios. These modes arise when the plasma pressure exceeds the ideal n = 1 external kink "no-wall" beta limit. RWMs can be controlled by plasma rotation or by providing feedback control of the mode's non-axisymmetric field [1]. In ITER, RWMs are expected to set the beta limit and disruption-onset beta in steady-state scenarios [1].

### 2.3 External Kink Modes

External kink modes, particularly the n = 1 mode, are also significant in determining the beta limit. The stability of these modes is influenced by the magnetic well, which is a measure of the magnetic field configuration. Experiments in NCSX have shown that the beta limit increases from 2.2% to 5.2% as the magnetic well varies from -10% to 7% [3]. This indicates that the magnetic well plays a crucial role in enhancing the stability of external kink modes.

## 3. Advanced Tokamak Configurations

Advanced tokamak configurations, such as those with reversed shear or negative shear, offer the potential to achieve higher beta values by stabilizing MHD instabilities. These configurations can reduce the bootstrap current, which is a major driver of NTMs, and improve the stability of external kink modes. The NCSX configuration, which is closely related to advanced tokamaks, has demonstrated robust stability to vertical modes and external kink modes due to its 3D shaping and quasi-axisymmetry [3].

## 4. High-Temperature Superconductors (HTS)

The use of HTS in tokamak magnets can significantly enhance the performance and stability of fusion reactors. HTS materials enable higher magnetic fields, which can increase the beta limit and reduce the size of the reactor. Recent developments in HTS technology, such as the VIPER cable, have demonstrated the feasibility of using HTS in high-performance superconducting magnets [4]. These advances can lead to more compact and efficient fusion devices, making the high-field pathway to fusion a promising approach.

## 5. Active Control of MHD Instabilities

Active control techniques, such as electron cyclotron current drive (ECCD) and feedback control of external magnetic fields, have shown promising results in stabilizing MHD instabilities. ECCD has been used to stabilize NTMs in DIII-D, and detailed measurements of currents driven by ECCD have shown good agreement with theoretical models [5]. Feedback control of RWMs using external coils has also been successful in stabilizing these modes [1]. These techniques are essential for achieving and maintaining high beta values in fusion reactors.

## 6. Conclusion

MHD stability boundaries play a crucial role in constraining the beta limits in fusion reactors. NTMs and RWMs are the primary instabilities that limit the achievable beta, and advanced tokamak configurations and HTS technology offer promising solutions to enhance stability and achieve higher beta values. Active control techniques, such as ECCD and feedback control, are essential for stabilizing these instabilities and ensuring the safe and efficient operation of fusion reactors. Further research and development in these areas are necessary to fully realize the potential of fusion as a viable energy source.
