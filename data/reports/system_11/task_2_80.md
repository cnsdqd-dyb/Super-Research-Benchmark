## 2. Geometry in Ballet Choreography

### 2.1. Geometric Patterns and Spatial Arrangements

Geometry plays a crucial role in ballet choreography, influencing the formation of patterns and the spatial arrangement of dancers. The positions of dancers on the stage can be described using mathematical coordinates, and choreographers often use geometric shapes to create visually striking formations.

#### 2.1.1. Synchrony in Timing

Choreographers ensure that dancers move in unison, maintaining precise timing. This is achieved through constraints such as:
\[
x_1(t_1) = x_2(t_2) \quad \text{and} \quad y_1(t_1) = y_2(t_2) \quad \text{for} \ t_1 = t_2
\]
where \((x_i(t), y_i(t))\) denotes the position of the \(i\)-th dancer at time \(t\).

#### 2.1.2. Spatial Patterns

Choreographers design movements to form geometric shapes such as circles, lines, and polygons. For example, a circle can be described by:
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n \left( x_i(t) - x_0 \right)^2 + \left( y_i(t) - y_0 \right)^2 = r^2
\]
where \((x_0, y_0)\) is the center of the circle and \(r\) is the radius.

### 2.2. Historical and Contemporary Examples

#### 2.2.1. Oskar Schlemmer and the Bauhaus

Oskar Schlemmer, a key figure in the Bauhaus movement, created the "Triadic Ballet," which combined geometric costumes with robotic dance movements. The precision and symmetry of these performances continue to captivate audiences, demonstrating the visionary power of Schlemmer's work [1].

#### 2.2.2. Rudolf Laban and Space Harmony

Rudolf Laban, a pioneer in movement analysis, developed the concept of "space harmony," which links spatial exercises to musical scales and geometric shapes. Laban's work influenced the development of Labanotation, a system for recording choreography, and his principles continue to be applied in contemporary choreography [2].

#### 2.2.3. William Forsythe and Neo-Classical Ballet

William Forsythe, a contemporary choreographer, has expanded the dimensions of ballet by deconstructing and reimagining classical techniques. His work often incorporates geometric and mechanical principles, creating dynamic and visually striking performances [3].

## 3. Mechanical Balance in Ballet

### 3.1. Biomechanics of Ballet

Ballet requires a high degree of balance and control, which are achieved through biomechanical principles. Dancers must maintain their center of gravity and distribute their weight effectively to perform complex movements.

#### 3.1.1. Balance and Support

The balance of a ballet dancer is crucial, especially in positions such as en pointe (on the tips of the toes). The support polygon, the area of contact between the foot and the ground, is small, making balance challenging. Dancers use techniques such as shifting weight and adjusting their center of mass to maintain stability [4].

#### 3.1.2. Dynamic Movements

Dynamic movements, such as jumps and turns, require precise control of the body's momentum. Dancers must calculate the force and direction of their movements to execute these actions smoothly and gracefully [5].

### 3.2. Mathematical Optimization in Choreography

Mathematical optimization techniques can enhance the precision and expressiveness of ballet choreography. By analyzing the error between geometric shapes and actual dance movements, choreographers can refine their sequences to achieve greater symmetry and beauty.

#### 3.2.1. Motion Capture and Analysis

Advanced motion capture techniques allow choreographers to record and analyze the movements of dancers. This data can be used to optimize the paths and formations of dancers, ensuring that they form the desired geometric shapes accurately [6].

#### 3.2.2. Least Squares Fitting

Least squares fitting is a mathematical method used to fit geometric shapes to the movements of dancers. This technique can be applied to optimize the formation of circles, squares, and other shapes, enhancing the visual appeal of the performance [7].

## 4. Conclusion

Geometry and mechanical balance are integral to the art of ballet choreography. By applying principles of spatial geometry, biomechanics, and mathematical optimization, choreographers can create performances that are both technically precise and aesthetically beautiful. The works of Oskar Schlemmer, Rudolf Laban, and William Forsythe exemplify the integration of these concepts, demonstrating the enduring relevance of geometry and mechanical balance in ballet.
