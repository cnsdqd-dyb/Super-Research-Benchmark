### Executive Summary  
Contemporary design transcends traditional color science by integrating neuroaesthetic principles, sensory processing diversity, and context-dependent applications. This report synthesizes empirical and theoretical advances to demonstrate how modern design frameworks extend color psychology beyond universalist models. Key findings reveal that:  
1. **Neuroaesthetics** redefines color perception as a triadic neural process (sensory-motor, emotion-valuation, meaning-knowledge systems) [4, 6, 7].  
2. **Sensory-inclusive design** necessitates tailored color strategies for neurodiverse populations, rejecting one-size-fits-all approaches [2, 10].  
3. **Functional color application** in built environments (e.g., healthcare, education) prioritizes psychological well-being over aesthetic convention [1, 8, 12].  
Methodological rigor in spectral control and participant profiling is critical to avoid past pitfalls [3, 13].  

---

### 1. Introduction  
Traditional color science emphasizes universal psychological responses (e.g., red = arousal) but overlooks individual neurobiological variability [3, 13]. Contemporary design, informed by neuroscience and sensory processing research, extends these dimensions by addressing:  
- **Context-dependence**: Color effects vary with environment (e.g., clinical vs. educational settings) [1, 12].  
- **Perceptual diversity**: Neurodivergent populations exhibit distinct color sensitivities [2, 10].  
- **Multisensory integration**: Color interacts with tactile, auditory, and spatial stimuli [8, 9].  

---

### 2. Theoretical Framework: From Universality to Triadic Processing  
Traditional models associate hues with fixed emotional outcomes (e.g., blue promotes calm). Neuroaesthetics reframes this via the **aesthetic triad** [4, 6, 7]:  

\begin{align*}
\text{Aesthetic Experience} = \underbrace{\text{Sensory-Motor}}_{\substack{\text{Visual processing} \\ \text{Embodied response}}} + \underbrace{\text{Emotion-Valuation}}_{\substack{\text{Affective appraisal} \\ \text{(e.g., pleasure/disgust)}}} + \underbrace{\text{Meaning-Knowledge}}_{\substack{\text{Contextual framing} \\ \text{(e.g., cultural/semantic)}}}
\end{align*}

This model explains why color effects are non-uniform. For example:  
- **Red** may enhance athletic dominance (emotion-valuation) [13] but induce anxiety in autistic individuals due to sensory hyper-reactivity [2, 10].  
- **Blue/Green** promote tranquility in healthcare settings by reducing sensory deprivation risk (sensory-motor) [1, 12].  

---

### 3. Neuroaesthetic Extensions to Color Science  
#### 3.1 Neural Mechanisms of Color Perception  
fMRI and EEG studies confirm that color engages distributed networks:  
- **Sensory-motor systems**: Primary visual cortex (V1–V4) processes hue, while parietal regions integrate spatial context [6, 11].  
- **Emotion-valuation**: Amygdala and orbitofrontal cortex assign affective value, modulated by individual state [4, 7].  
- **Meaning-knowledge**: Prefrontal cortex contextualizes color based on experience (e.g., clinical vs. home environments) [1, 6].  

#### 3.2 Methodological Advances  
Contemporary research corrects historical flaws via:  
- **Spectral precision**: Controlling wavelength composition (\( \lambda \pm 5\text{nm} \)) to isolate hue effects [3].  
- **Participant stratification**: Grouping by sensory profiles (hyper/hypo-responsive) rather than diagnostic labels [2].  

---

### 4. Contextual Applications in Built Environments  
#### 4.1 Healthcare Design  
- **Functional color** prioritizes patient well-being in "captive" spaces (e.g., avoiding overstimulation in corridors) [1].  
- **Sensory deprivation mitigation**: Soft blues/greens in patient rooms reduce anxiety by providing low-arousal visual anchors [1, 8].  

#### 4.2 Neurodiverse-Inclusive Spaces  
- **Cool palettes (blue/green)**: Lower arousal for hyper-responsive individuals (85% of neurodivergent cohorts) [10].  
- **Avoidance of fluorescents**: Neon hues disrupt gamma oscillations in ASD brains, triggering sensory overload [10].  
- **Respite zones**: Neutral-color retreat spaces counter sensory fatigue [12].  

#### 4.3 Educational Environments  
- **Color zoning**: Warm tones (e.g., peach) in social areas foster engagement; cool tones in study areas aid concentration [10, 12].  
- **Dynamic adaptation**: Adjustable LED systems accommodate fluctuating sensory states [2].  

---

### 5. Future Directions & Recommendations  
1. **Personalized Color Metrics**: Develop AI-driven tools to map individual sensory profiles to optimal color parameters [2, 9].  
2. **Cross-Modal Standards**: Establish guidelines for color-texture-sound interactions (e.g., matte surfaces reduce glare + auditory echo) [8, 12].  
3. **Policy Integration**: Update building codes (e.g., WELL Standard) to mandate sensory-inclusive color practices [2].  
4. **Longitudinal Studies**: Track color impact on neuroplasticity in clinical populations [3, 6].  

---

### Conclusion  
Contemporary design transforms color from a passive aesthetic element into an active neurobiological modulator. By leveraging the aesthetic triad, sensory diversity frameworks, and spectral precision, it extends traditional color psychology into a dynamic, contextually responsive discipline. Future success hinges on interdisciplinary collaboration—uniting neuroscientists, designers, and policymakers—to codify evidence-based practices that enhance human well-being across the perceptual spectrum.
