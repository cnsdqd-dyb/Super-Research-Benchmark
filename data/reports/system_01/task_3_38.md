The interaction between regenerative braking power matching (the balance between energy regenerated and consumed) and grid harmonic distortion during train density changes is complex, involving power electronics, grid dynamics, and train operations. Here's a breakdown of the key influences:

1.  **The Core Problem: Regenerative Braking & Power Flow**
    *   **Regeneration:** During braking, train traction converters act as inverters, converting DC bus power (from the overhead line (OHLE) or third rail) back to AC and feeding it into the grid.
    *   **Power Consumption:** During traction, converters act as rectifiers, drawing power from the grid.
    *   **Power Matching:** Ideally, the power generated by braking trains in one location should be consumed by traction-running trains nearby. Perfect local consumption minimizes net power flow into/from the substations (SBSs).
    *   **Density Impact:** Higher train density increases the *likelihood* of local consumption (braking power is closer to another traction train). Lower density reduces this likelihood, forcing more net power reversal (feed-in or draw-in) to/from the SBS.

2.  **Harmonic Generation Sources (Primarily Train Converters)**
    *   **Pulse Width Modulation (PWM):** Modern AC drive converters use high-frequency PWM to control voltage/frequency. This switching generates characteristic low-order harmonics (e.g., 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th).
    *   **Non-Linear Loads:** The rectifier/inverter stages present highly non-linear loads to the grid, drawing distorted current waveforms even under DC bus voltage control.
    *   **Converter Topology & Control:** Specific converter designs (e.g., diode/IGBT hybrid, GTO, IGBT-based) and control strategies (voltage vs. current control) have inherent harmonic signatures.

3.  **Influence of Density Changes & Power Matching on Harmonic Distortion**
    *   **High Density (Potentially Better Local Matching):**
        *   **Reduced Net Power Flow:** More frequent local consumption means less net reactive power (VAR) and real power reversal to the SBS.
        *   **Current Harmonic Cancellation:** If multiple converters operating with *similar phase-shifted* PWM strategies inject harmonics into the *same* network segment (common OHLE) at *high density*, **in-phase** harmonic currents can potentially **add together**, *increasing* the total harmonic current amplitude. Conversely, if the phases are naturally phase-shifted in the grid topology, harmonics might partially cancel. This depends heavily on the specific phase-shifting techniques used at SBSs (e.g., 12-pulse, 24-pulse) and grid impedance.
        *   **Reduced VAR Stress:** Lower net power reversal generally means less severe voltage dips/sags/swells, which can indirectly reduce harmonic amplification in a weak grid.
    *   **Low Density (Poor Local Matching):**
        *   **Increased Net Power Flow:** Reduced local consumption means more switching between rectifier (draw) and inverter (feed) modes at SBSs, and higher net power flow over longer distances.
        *   **SBS Converter Operation:** SBS converters handling net feed-in (high density in one area) or severe net draw (low density overall) are exposed to significantly higher DC bus voltages during feed-in or lower voltages during deep draw-in. Their control systems work harder to maintain stability, which can exacerbate harmonic generation.
        *   **Amplification in Weak Grids:** Low system strength (high grid impedance) combined with high localized harmonic currents (from braking trains) causes **amplified harmonic voltage distortion** (Vthd). The simple Ohm's Law analogy: V_distortion = I_harmonic * Z_grid. High Z_grid + high I_harmonic = high V_distortion. Low density often correlates with increased net power flow, potentially increasing harmonic currents.
        *   **Transient Harmonics:** Frequent and rapid switching of many converters between modes during sparse schedules (peak braking vs. peak traction) can generate high-frequency transient harmonics.

4.  **Key Factors Modifying the Influence**
    *   **SBS Converter Design & Control:** Sophisticated active front-end (AFE) converters with multi-pulse loads, soft-switching, and advanced harmonic mitigation control actively reduce harmonics regardless of load flow.
    *   **Grid Architecture & Impedance:** Strength and configuration of the feeding traction substations, presence of shunt reactors/capacitors, and overall network topology significantly impact how harmonics propagate and amplify.
    *   **Phasing & Phase-Shifting:** Strategic phase-shifting at SBSs is crucial for cancelling low-order harmonics generated by different converter groups feeding the same section. Lasers, bends, and overall supply system design matter.
    *   **Reactive Power Compensation:** Local compensation (capacitor banks, SVCs, STATCOMs) is vital for voltage stability and reduces harmonic distortion caused by excessive VAR flows. Its effectiveness can be load-dependent.
    *   **Train Scheduling & Operations:** Peak-hour braking might coincide with peak traction on other lines/schedules, improving matching. Off-peak patterns might not.

**Summary & Conclusion:**

Changes in train density directly impact the balance between regeneration and consumption, which in turn influences harmonic distortion on the traction grid:

1.  **High Density:** Favors local consumption (better matching), generally reducing *net* feeder loading and voltage stress. However, it can **increase** harmonic *currents* due to the sheer number of converters operating simultaneously near each other. Harmonic **voltage distortion** then depends critically on **local grid strength**. In a **strong grid**, this might be manageable. In a **weak grid**, the high harmonic current density can cause **significant voltage distortion**.
2.  **Low Density:** Reduces local consumption, increasing *net* power reversal to/from SBSs. This stresses SBS converters and can promote harmonic generation under extreme load conditions (deep feed-in or draw-in). The longer-distance net power flow itself can transport lower-frequency harmonics. Crucially, this low-density scenario is often accompanied by **higher grid impedance effects** over longer stretches, which **amplifies voltage distortion** for any harmonic current present.

**In essence:** While high density might increase the raw source of harmonics (many converters), it often coincides with better grid conditions (shorter segments between SBSs). The highest **harmonic voltage distortion (THDv)** risk arises during **density transitions** or in areas where the grid is **inherently weak (high impedance)** and experiences **significant net regenerative power feeding into it** (e.g., high braking density in a lightly loaded feeding section). Effective grid planning, strong design (low Z_grid), strategic phase-shifting, and advanced SBS controls are essential to mitigate these harmonic impacts.
