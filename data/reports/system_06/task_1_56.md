# How is the Tension Between Competitive Knowledge Spillovers and Cooperative Resource Integration Balanced in Creative Clusters?

## Abstract

Creative clusters represent dynamic ecosystems where the dual imperatives of knowledge sharing and competitive advantage coexist in inherent tension. This report examines the mechanisms through which creative clusters balance the competing demands of competitive knowledge spillovers—where firms risk losing proprietary innovations to rivals—and cooperative resource integration, where collaborative efforts can generate collective value that exceeds individual capabilities. Drawing upon recent empirical research and theoretical frameworks from innovation studies, industrial marketing, and cluster dynamics, this analysis reveals that successful creative clusters employ a sophisticated array of balancing mechanisms, including strategic knowledge disclosure practices, trust-based network structures, modular collaboration approaches, and institutional intermediaries that facilitate selective openness while protecting core competencies. The findings suggest that the tension between spillovers and integration is not merely a challenge to be resolved but rather a productive dynamic that, when properly managed, drives innovation performance and cluster-level competitiveness. Understanding these balancing mechanisms has significant implications for cluster governance, firm strategy, and regional innovation policy.

## 1. Introduction

The phenomenon of creative clusters has attracted substantial scholarly attention over the past three decades, driven by the recognition that geographic concentration of creative and cultural industries generates measurable economic benefits. Research commissioned by the UK Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport identified 55 creative clusters across the United Kingdom, with over 90% of creative industries turnover generated within these concentrated geographic areas [1]. The DCMS Sector Vision of 2023 subsequently set an ambitious goal of adding £50 billion in gross value added through creative clusters by 2030, underscoring the strategic importance of these agglomerations for national economic prosperity.

However, the concentration of creative firms in geographic clusters creates an fundamental tension that lies at the heart of cluster dynamics. On one hand, clusters derive their competitive advantage from knowledge spillovers—the diffusion of ideas, innovations, and best practices among co-located firms that would not occur absent geographic proximity. As the foundational Nesta report on creative clusters articulated, "industrial clustering benefits businesses by giving them access to skilled staff and shared services, and the opportunity to capture valuable knowledge spillovers" [1]. This spillover mechanism is widely recognized as the primary driver of innovation performance in cluster settings, enabling firms to learn from neighbors, observe emerging practices, and benefit from the collective research and development investments of the cluster community.

On the other hand, creative firms operate in competitive markets where proprietary knowledge constitutes a critical source of sustainable competitive advantage. The same knowledge that generates positive spillovers for the cluster may, if appropriated by competitors, erode the originating firm's market position and return on innovation investment. This creates what scholars have termed the "paradox of disclosure" or the fundamental tension between openness and appropriability [2]. The challenge for creative clusters, therefore, is not merely to facilitate knowledge sharing—which would be straightforward—but to achieve an optimal balance where sufficient knowledge flows to generate collective innovation while individual firms retain enough proprietary advantage to justify continued investment in creative activities.

This report examines how creative clusters navigate this tension between competitive knowledge spillovers and cooperative resource integration. The analysis proceeds through several interconnected dimensions: the theoretical foundations of the tension, the mechanisms through which clusters balance competing demands, the role of organizational diversity and network structure in moderating these dynamics, and the practical implications for cluster governance and policy. By synthesizing recent empirical research with established theoretical frameworks, this report provides a comprehensive understanding of how successful creative clusters achieve productive balance between cooperation and competition.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of the Tension

### 2.1 The Nature of Knowledge in Creative Clusters

Understanding the tension between spillovers and integration requires first examining the distinctive characteristics of knowledge as a resource in creative industries. Knowledge in creative clusters is not monolithic; rather, it exists along a spectrum from highly tacit, embedded, and firm-specific knowledge to more codified, transferable, and widely accessible information. This distinction has profound implications for how knowledge can be shared while still preserving competitive advantage.

The conversion of information to knowledge represents a collective learning process that unfolds within industry cluster settings [3]. While the cost of transferring information over large distances has declined substantially due to progress in internet and data storage technologies, significant limitations persist regarding how knowledge can be transferred, particularly through mechanisms of spillovers and economic gains. Tacit knowledge—know-how that is deeply embedded in individual practitioners, organizational routines, and cultural practices—requires sustained interaction, observation, and practice to transfer effectively. This characteristic creates both a barrier to knowledge leakage (since tacit knowledge cannot be easily extracted without the knowledge holder's participation) and a mechanism for controlled knowledge sharing (since the knowledge holder can selectively engage in knowledge transfer relationships).

Creative industries are characterized by particularly high levels of knowledge complexity, where innovations often require the integration of multiple knowledge domains and where the boundaries between distinct creative disciplines become increasingly blurred. Research on digital platform-based ecosystems has documented how collaboration and competition evolve through sequential phases: selective cooperation, allied competition, and selective coopetition [4]. These phases demonstrate how switching between different multi-level strategies spanning market segments, products, and technological components represents a viable solution for organizations adapting to complex competitive environments. The evolution from cooperation through competition to coopetition reflects the dynamic nature of knowledge relationships in creative ecosystems.

### 2.2 The Paradox of Disclosure

The tension between knowledge sharing and competitive advantage has been formalized in the literature as the "paradox of disclosure," originally articulated by Arrow and subsequently elaborated in numerous studies of innovation and knowledge management [2]. The paradox arises from the recognition that firms seeking to collaborate and benefit from others' knowledge must inevitably reveal some of their own proprietary knowledge, creating vulnerability to opportunism and knowledge misappropriation. This tension is particularly acute in creative industries, where intellectual property protections may be difficult to enforce and where competitive advantage often derives from creative innovations that can be reverse-engineered or imitated once revealed.

Abundant previous research has investigated the tensions described as the "fundamental paradox" of disclosure [2]. The discussion about paradoxical tensions between appropriability and openness to collaborating in innovation has been rekindled by research streams on open innovation, which point out that the tendency to open up the innovation process is likely to stress these potentially paradoxical tensions. This extends beyond the selling of ideas to apply to other external interactions among innovative firms. Scholars have identified three key areas requiring further research: the association between openness and appropriability, the way managing these tensions affects organizational performance, and the fundamental nature of tensions between openness and appropriability—whether they constitute a dilemma requiring trade-offs or a paradox that can be transcended through creative organizational approaches.

The management of intellectual property emerges as crucial for sustaining competitive advantage, yet numerous studies signal intellectual property management and appropriability challenges, particularly linked to innovation collaboration [2]. The intricate linkages between appropriability and openness remain subjects of ongoing scholarly debate, with evidence suggesting that the relationship is more complex than simple trade-off models would suggest.

### 2.3 Knowledge Spillovers: Mechanisms and Dynamics

Knowledge spillovers in creative clusters operate through multiple mechanisms that differentially affect the balance between collective benefit and individual advantage. The DCMS scoping study identified several key mechanisms through which knowledge generated by creative firms diffuses to other organizations [5]. The process begins with a firm in the creative industries investing in research, which yields a new product innovation that feeds into its own output. However, knowledge spillovers occur simultaneously: the knowledge embedded in that innovation is acquired, through various possible mechanisms, by other firms, and the new innovation affects the production of their output and their investment activities.

The primary mechanisms identified for knowledge spillovers include supply chain interactions, where firms buying from or selling to creative industries absorb knowledge through commercial relationships [5]. Labor turnover represents another significant channel, as employees moving between firms carry tacit knowledge, skills, and practices from their previous employers to new organizations. Geographic proximity facilitates informal knowledge exchange through social networks, professional associations, and casual interactions that would not occur absent co-location. The density of creative industry firms in particular areas creates opportunities for knowledge spillovers that are difficult to replicate in less concentrated settings.

Research examining the relationship between creative industry activity and other firms' outcomes has sought to establish causation through instrumental variables approaches, using historical locations of art schools and coal mines as instruments [5]. Studies have also examined how innovation activity of firms is associated with the extent to which firms buy from or sell to firms in creative industries. These methodological advances have strengthened the empirical foundation for understanding spillover dynamics, though challenges in establishing definitive causation persist.

## 3. Balancing Mechanisms in Creative Clusters

### 3.1 Organizational Diversity as a Balancing Strategy

One of the most robust findings in recent cluster research concerns the positive impact of organizational diversity on innovation performance. Research published in the International Journal of Innovation Studies demonstrates that two types of organizational diversity—diversity of nationality and diversity of organization type—positively impact the innovation performance of clusters [6]. This finding suggests that diversity serves as a mechanism for balancing the tension between spillovers and integration, as heterogeneous cluster compositions create multiple knowledge pathways while reducing direct competitive overlap.

The diversity of nationality brings different cultural perspectives, knowledge bases, and creative traditions into proximity, generating novel combinations that would not emerge from homogeneous populations. More practically, firms from different national contexts may have complementary rather than directly competitive market positions, reducing the competitive costs of knowledge sharing while preserving the benefits of collective learning. When firms serve different geographic markets or customer segments, knowledge spillovers become less threatening because the revealing firm is unlikely to face direct competition from the receiving firm.

Diversity of organization type similarly contributes to productive knowledge exchange by bringing together firms at different positions in the value chain and with different strategic orientations. A cluster containing large cultural conglomerates, small independent companies, educational institutions, and public sector cultural organizations creates a multi-layered ecosystem where knowledge flows along multiple channels. Research on the Clwstwr programme—a place-based innovation programme for creative and cultural sectors in Wales—demonstrates how shifting power away from large cultural conglomerates towards local, smaller independent companies can generate innovation while addressing issues of environmental sustainability, diversity, and inclusion [7]. Universities, in particular, emerge as well-placed to serve as active cultural intermediaries in critically-informed cluster-building, drawing on critical literature to develop alternative economic models of innovation designed to improve social, cultural, and economic outcomes.

### 3.2 Network Structure and Embeddedness

Beyond organizational diversity, the structural characteristics of cluster networks significantly influence how effectively the tension between spillovers and integration is managed. Research indicates that cluster network size and embeddedness have positive moderating effects on the relationship between organizational diversity and innovation performance [6]. Larger, more densely embedded networks provide more pathways for knowledge exchange while the strength of network ties enables trust-based relationships that reduce the risks of knowledge misappropriation.

In strong industry clusters, supportive knowledge-related institutions often function as both initiators and managers of knowledge interactions between members throughout the supply chain [8]. These institutions—including industry associations, research centers, and governmental agencies—provide infrastructure for knowledge exchange that individual firms could not develop independently. They establish norms for knowledge sharing, provide mechanisms for dispute resolution, and create forums where firms can interact without the immediate pressures of commercial competition.

The degree of interfirm mobility of employees within an industry cluster has been shown to correlate with the degree of tacit knowledge sharing [8]. Professional rotations create channels for knowledge transfer that are neither fully public (available to all cluster members) nor fully private (confined within individual firms). When employees move between organizations, they carry accumulated knowledge and relationships that facilitate ongoing exchange, creating a form of "structural embeddedness" that balances access and protection. This mechanism is particularly important in creative industries, where tacit creative skills are central to competitive advantage and where formal intellectual property protections may be less effective than in technology-intensive industries.

### 3.3 Trust, Norms, and Common Knowledge

Well-functioning industrial clusters build over time a pool of common knowledge that is shared across all parts of the community [8]. This common knowledge serves as a foundation for collaborative activities inside the cluster. The building blocks of this foundation include a common code of behavior that helps build trust in collaborations, and the development of common culture stemming from a common worker-pool used by all parties of the cluster.

Trust operates as a crucial mechanism for reducing the transaction costs and risks associated with knowledge sharing. When firms trust that partners will not opportunistically appropriate shared knowledge, they are more willing to engage in collaborative relationships that generate innovation benefits. Trust develops through repeated interactions, reputation effects, and institutional mechanisms that punish opportunistic behavior. In creative clusters, the development of trust enables firms to navigate the paradox of disclosure by creating bilateral or multilateral relationships where knowledge sharing occurs within bounded, trust-based communities rather than in fully open environments.

Common culture and shared norms reduce the cognitive distance between cluster participants, making it easier to communicate complex creative ideas and to recognize valuable knowledge when it is encountered. When firms share common frameworks for understanding creative quality, market dynamics, and innovation processes, knowledge exchange becomes more efficient and effective. This common foundation does not eliminate competition but rather channels it into forms that are compatible with ongoing cooperation.

### 3.4 Modular Collaboration and Selective Coopetition

The evolution of digital platform-based ecosystems provides a model for how organizations can balance competition and cooperation through strategic structuring of their relationships. Research identifies three sequential phases through which ecosystem relationships evolve: selective cooperation, allied competition, and selective coopetition [4]. These phases demonstrate how switching between different multi-level strategies spanning market segments, products, and technological components represents a viable solution for adapting in complex competitive environments.

Selective cooperation involves carefully chosen partnerships where firms collaborate on specific projects or domains while maintaining competition in others. This approach allows firms to access complementary knowledge and resources without exposing their full range of capabilities to potential competitors. Allied competition occurs when firms that compete in some dimensions cooperate in others, recognizing that the benefits of cooperation in areas of mutual interest can outweigh the costs of competition in areas of divergence. Selective coopetition represents the most sophisticated integration of competition and cooperation, where firms maintain ongoing relationships that include both collaborative and competitive elements.

The modular approach to collaboration—where firms cooperate on specific projects or components while competing on others—enables knowledge integration while preserving competitive advantage. This structure is particularly relevant for creative industries, where projects often require collaboration across multiple creative disciplines but where the final creative outputs may compete directly in the marketplace. By modularizing their relationships, firms can achieve the benefits of collective innovation while limiting the scope of knowledge exposure.

## 4. The Role of Intermediaries and Institutional Infrastructure

### 4.1 Universities as Cultural Intermediaries

Universities occupy a distinctive position in creative cluster ecosystems, serving as bridges between commercial enterprises, cultural institutions, and the broader knowledge infrastructure. Research on the Clwstwr programme demonstrates how universities can function as active cultural intermediaries in cluster development [7]. Universities draw on critical literature of market-based models to develop alternative economic models of innovation designed to improve social, cultural, and economic outcomes. They leverage research and innovation expertise in a sector dominated by small companies and freelancers, providing capabilities that individual creative firms could not develop independently.

The university role in creative clusters extends beyond knowledge production to encompass knowledge brokering—connecting different cluster participants, facilitating knowledge exchange, and providing neutral forums for collaboration. As institutions that are neither purely commercial nor purely cultural, universities can navigate the tensions between economic and cultural values that often characterize creative industries. They can facilitate collaboration between firms that might otherwise view each other as competitors, providing institutional infrastructure that reduces the transaction costs of cooperation.

### 4.2 Cluster Governance and Policy Interventions

Effective cluster governance requires understanding the mechanisms through which the spillover-integration tension can be productively managed. Policy interventions can support cluster development by strengthening the institutional infrastructure that enables trust-based knowledge exchange, facilitating connections between diverse cluster participants, and establishing norms and mechanisms for intellectual property management that balance individual and collective interests.

The Nesta framework for creative cluster development emphasizes several key principles that support productive tension management [1]. Catalyzing existing clusters rather than attempting to build new ones from scratch leverages established relationships and trust foundations. Thinking about which sectors work well together recognizes that knowledge spillovers are most productive when participants have complementary rather than identical knowledge bases. Helping remove barriers to collaboration reduces transaction costs and enables more extensive knowledge exchange. Building bridges as well as towers—connecting different parts of clusters and connecting clusters to external resources—expands the knowledge pathways available while maintaining the concentrated interactions that proximity enables.

The DCMS Sector Vision target of adding £50 billion in GVA through creative clusters by 2030 reflects the substantial economic stakes involved in effective cluster development [1]. Achieving this target will require not only maintaining the knowledge spillover benefits that clusters provide but also addressing the tensions that can undermine cluster effectiveness when they are not properly managed.

## 5. Implications and Future Directions

### 5.1 Theoretical Implications

The analysis presented in this report suggests that the tension between competitive knowledge spillovers and cooperative resource integration in creative clusters is not a problem to be solved but rather a dynamic to be managed. The most successful creative clusters do not eliminate the tension between openness and appropriability but rather create institutional mechanisms, organizational structures, and relational practices that enable productive navigation of this tension. This perspective aligns with emerging research suggesting that the relationship between openness and appropriability may be more paradoxical than dilemmatic—requiring creative organizational solutions rather than simple trade-offs.

The findings support a more nuanced understanding of knowledge spillovers than simple models of knowledge as a public good that diffuses freely once created. Knowledge spillovers in creative clusters are actively shaped by the strategies, structures, and relationships that cluster participants develop. Firms do not passively receive spillovers but actively manage their knowledge exposure through selective disclosure, relationship choice, and organizational design. Understanding spillovers therefore requires attention not only to the characteristics of knowledge but to the social and institutional structures that mediate knowledge flows.

### 5.2 Practical Implications

For cluster managers and policymakers, the analysis suggests several practical implications. First, supporting organizational diversity—particularly diversity of organization type and nationality—can enhance cluster innovation performance by creating multiple knowledge pathways while reducing direct competitive overlap. Second, investing in network infrastructure that enables trust-based relationships can reduce the transaction costs of knowledge sharing and enable more extensive collaboration. Third, developing institutional mechanisms for managing intellectual property that balance individual and collective interests can address the paradox of disclosure without eliminating the competitive dynamics that drive innovation.

For individual firms operating in creative clusters, the analysis suggests the importance of strategic approaches to knowledge management. Firms benefit from developing sophisticated capabilities for selective knowledge disclosure—sharing enough knowledge to access the benefits of cluster membership while protecting core competencies. Building diverse relationships across the cluster can provide access to multiple knowledge sources while reducing dependence on any single partner. Engaging in modular collaboration—cooperating on specific projects while competing on others—can enable knowledge integration while preserving competitive advantage.

### 5.3 Future Research Directions

Several directions for future research emerge from this analysis. First, longitudinal studies tracking how the balance between spillovers and integration evolves as clusters mature could illuminate the dynamic nature of tension management. Second, comparative research across different types of creative clusters could identify how industry-specific characteristics shape the mechanisms through which tension is managed. Third, research on digital platforms and virtual collaboration could examine how technology-mediated interaction affects the balance between knowledge sharing and protection.

The relationship between openness and appropriability remains an area requiring further investigation. Some studies frame this as a dilemma requiring trade-offs, while others suggest it may be possible to transcend the apparent tension through creative organizational approaches. Understanding the conditions under which openness and appropriability reinforce rather than undermine each other could significantly advance both theoretical understanding and practical guidance for cluster development.

## 6. Conclusion

The tension between competitive knowledge spillovers and cooperative resource integration represents a defining characteristic of creative clusters. This tension is not a dysfunction requiring correction but rather a productive dynamic that, when properly managed, drives innovation performance and cluster-level competitiveness. Successful creative clusters employ sophisticated mechanisms for balancing the competing demands of knowledge sharing and competitive advantage, including organizational diversity, network embeddedness, trust-based relationships, modular collaboration approaches, and institutional intermediaries.

The evidence suggests that the most effective approach to managing this tension is not to eliminate either knowledge spillovers or competition but rather to create institutional structures and organizational practices that enable productive navigation of both. By supporting diverse, well-connected clusters with strong institutional infrastructure, policymakers can enhance the innovation benefits that creative clusters provide while addressing the tensions that can undermine cluster effectiveness. For firms, strategic approaches to knowledge management—selective disclosure, diverse relationships, and modular collaboration—can enable capture of cluster benefits while preserving competitive advantage.

As creative clusters continue to evolve and as technology transforms the possibilities for knowledge exchange, the mechanisms through which the spillover-integration tension is managed will continue to develop. Understanding these mechanisms and their implications for cluster performance remains an important area for both scholarly research and practical policy development. The goal should not be to resolve the tension between cooperation and competition but to create the conditions under which this tension generates innovation rather than conflict.
