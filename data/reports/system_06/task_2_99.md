# What Distinguishes New Physics Signals from Neutrino Backgrounds in WIMP Scattering Measurements?

## Abstract

The search for weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) through direct detection represents one of the most compelling approaches to identifying the nature of dark matter. However, as experimental sensitivity continues to improve, coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CEvNS) from various astrophysical neutrino sources emerges as an irreducible background that closely mimics the expected WIMP signal. This report examines the fundamental challenges posed by neutrino backgrounds in WIMP scattering measurements and reviews the sophisticated discrimination techniques that enable the distinction between genuine new physics signals and neutrino-induced recoils. The analysis encompasses the physics of signal-background similarity, the concept of the neutrino floor, directional detection strategies, annual modulation signatures, multi-target complementarity, and emerging technologies that promise to extend the reach of direct detection experiments beyond the neutrino fog.

## 1. Introduction

Cosmological observations and the dynamics of the Milky Way provide compelling evidence for an invisible and dominant mass component constituting approximately 27% of the universe's energy content [1]. This so-called dark matter could be composed of new, color and charge neutral particles that were non-relativistic when they decoupled from ordinary matter in the early universe. Weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) represent a particularly well-motivated dark matter candidate, predicted to have non-zero couplings to baryons that could enable detection via their collisions with atomic nuclei in ultra-low background, deep underground detectors [1].

The direct detection paradigm relies on observing the nuclear recoils induced by WIMP-nucleus elastic scattering in sensitive detectors positioned deep underground to shield against cosmic ray-induced backgrounds. Among the various detector technologies developed for this purpose, those based on liquefied noble gases—particularly argon and xenon—have demonstrated tremendous discovery potential over the last decade [1]. These detectors exploit the scintillation and ionization signals produced when a particle interacts within the liquid target, enabling discrimination between electron recoils (from gamma rays and beta decays) and nuclear recoils (from neutron scattering or WIMP interactions).

However, a fundamental challenge has emerged as experimental sensitivity has improved: neutrinos from various astrophysical sources produce nuclear recoils through coherent elastic scattering that are nearly indistinguishable from those expected from WIMPs [2,3]. This similarity arises because both processes generate low-energy nuclear recoils in the keV energy range, with nearly identical spectral shapes. The resulting "neutrino floor" or "neutrino fog" represents a theoretical lower limit on discoverable WIMP parameter space, necessitating sophisticated discrimination strategies to distinguish genuine dark matter signals from this irreducible background [4,5].

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the physics underlying the signal-background similarity, the various neutrino sources contributing to the background, and the experimental and analytical techniques developed to overcome this challenge. The goal is to elucidate what distinguishes new physics signals from neutrino backgrounds and how current and future experiments can push beyond the neutrino fog to explore deeper into WIMP parameter space.

## 2. The Physics of WIMP Signals and Neutrino Backgrounds

### 2.1 WIMP-Nucleus Scattering Phenomenology

The direct detection of WIMPs relies on the elastic scattering of dark matter particles off atomic nuclei in a detector target. The expected event rate depends on several factors including the local dark matter density, the WIMP velocity distribution, the WIMP-nucleus cross section, and the nuclear response function [6]. For a WIMP of mass $m_\chi$ scattering off a nucleus of mass $m_N$, the maximum recoil energy transfer is given by:

$$E_{\text{max}} = \frac{2\mu^2 v_{\text{max}}^2}{m_N}$$

where $\mu = m_\chi m_N/(m_\chi + m_N)$ is the reduced mass and $v_{\text{max}}$ is the maximum WIMP velocity in the lab frame, typically taken as approximately 544 km/s for the Galactic escape velocity [6].

The differential event rate per unit recoil energy is typically expressed as:

$$\frac{dR}{dE_R} = \frac{\rho_\chi m_N}{2 m_\chi \mu^2} \sigma_0 F^2(E_R) \int_{v_{\text{min}}}^{v_{\text{esc}}} \frac{f(v)}{v} dv$$

where $\rho_\chi \approx 0.3$ GeV/cm³ is the local dark matter density, $\sigma_0$ is the zero-momentum transfer cross section, $F(E_R)$ is the nuclear form factor accounting for momentum-dependent scattering, and $f(v)$ is the WIMP velocity distribution [6].

The characteristic spectral shape of WIMP-induced nuclear recoils depends sensitively on the WIMP mass. For low-mass WIMPs ($m_\chi \sim 6$ GeV/c²), the recoil spectrum is relatively soft, peaking at low energies, while for high-mass WIMPs ($m_\chi \sim 100$ GeV/c²), the spectrum extends to higher recoil energies [3]. This mass-dependent spectral shape is crucial for distinguishing WIMP signals from backgrounds, as different neutrino sources produce characteristic recoil spectra that may overlap with specific WIMP mass hypotheses.

### 2.2 Coherent Elastic Neutrino-Nucleus Scattering

Neutrinos interact with nuclei through both charged-current and neutral-current processes. Of particular relevance for direct detection is coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CEvNS), a neutral-current process where the neutrino scatters off the entire nucleus rather than individual nucleons [7]. This coherence condition requires that the momentum transfer $q$ satisfies $qR \lesssim 1$, where $R$ is the nuclear radius, which is satisfied for neutrino energies below approximately 50 MeV and nuclear recoil energies in the 1-100 keV range [7].

The CEvNS cross section for a neutrino of energy $E_\nu$ scattering off a nucleus with atomic number $A$ and neutron number $N$ is given by:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dE_R} = \frac{G_F^2}{4\pi} Q_W^2 m_N \left(1 - \frac{m_N E_R}{2E_\nu^2}\right) F^2(E_R)$$

where $G_F$ is the Fermi coupling constant, $Q_W = N - (1-4\sin^2\theta_W)Z$ is the weak charge with $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.23$, and $F(E_R)$ is the nuclear form factor [7]. The coherence enhancement scales as $A^2$, making heavy target nuclei like xenon ($A \approx 131$) particularly sensitive to CEvNS.

The recent observation of CEvNS by the COHERENT collaboration has confirmed this Standard Model prediction and provided important constraints on neutrino non-standard interactions [8]. For dark matter direct detection, this measurement has validated the expected rate of neutrino-induced nuclear recoils, confirming that CEvNS will indeed become a relevant background for next-generation experiments.

### 2.3 Sources of Neutrino Backgrounds

Several astrophysical neutrino sources contribute to the background in direct detection experiments, each with distinct energy spectra, fluxes, and directional characteristics [3,9].

**Solar Neutrinos:** The Sun produces neutrinos through various nuclear fusion reactions in its core. The relevant components for direct detection include:

- **pp neutrinos:** With energies below 0.42 MeV, these produce negligible nuclear recoils but contribute to electron recoil backgrounds.
- **⁷Be neutrinos:** The monoenergetic 0.862 MeV line produces a characteristic nuclear recoil spectrum.
- **pep neutrinos:** The monoenergetic 1.44 MeV neutrinos.
- **⁸B neutrinos:** With a continuous spectrum extending up to approximately 15 MeV, these produce the most significant nuclear recoil background among solar neutrinos, with an event rate equivalent to a WIMP of 6 GeV/c² with a spin-independent cross section of approximately $5 \times 10^{-45}$ cm² [3].
- **CNO neutrinos:** Produced by carbon-nitrogen-oxygen cycle reactions.

The solar neutrino flux is relatively well-understood but still carries uncertainties of order 5-10%, which propagate to uncertainties in the expected background rate [10].

**Atmospheric Neutrinos:** Produced by cosmic ray interactions in the Earth's atmosphere, atmospheric neutrinos have a broad energy spectrum extending from MeV to GeV scales. The CEvNS component from atmospheric neutrinos produces nuclear recoils equivalent to a $\sim 100$ GeV WIMP with a spin-independent cross section of approximately $10^{-48}$ cm² [3]. The atmospheric neutrino flux has larger uncertainties than solar neutrinos, particularly at higher energies.

**Diffuse Supernova Neutrino Background (DSNB):** Accumulated neutrinos from all core-collapse supernova explosions throughout the history of the universe constitute a diffuse background with a spectrum extending to approximately 30-50 MeV [3]. The DSNB flux is poorly constrained, with uncertainties of order unity, making it a particularly challenging background source.

**Geo-neutrinos:** Radioactive decays within the Earth produce electron anti-neutrinos with energies up to approximately 3 MeV. While these are primarily detectable via inverse beta decay rather than CEvNS, they contribute to the overall neutrino background budget.

### 2.4 The Signal-Background Similarity Problem

The fundamental challenge posed by neutrino backgrounds stems from the remarkable similarity between neutrino-induced and WIMP-induced nuclear recoil spectra [3,4]. Several factors contribute to this similarity:

**Kinematic Similarity:** Both WIMPs and neutrinos produce nuclear recoils through elastic scattering, resulting in similar recoil energy distributions for comparable event rates. The maximum recoil energy for CEvNS is:

$$E_R^{\text{max}} = \frac{2E_\nu^2}{m_N + 2E_\nu}$$

For typical direct detection targets and solar neutrino energies, this yields maximum recoil energies in the 1-100 keV range, precisely where WIMP searches are most sensitive [7].

**Spectral Shape Convergence:** Detailed calculations have shown that for certain WIMP masses, the neutrino-induced recoil spectrum can almost perfectly mimic the expected WIMP signal [3]. This is particularly true for the case of ⁸B solar neutrinos, which produce a recoil spectrum remarkably similar to that expected from a low-mass WIMP.

**Limited Discrimination Power:** Standard background discrimination techniques based on the ratio of ionization to scintillation signals (for liquid xenon and argon detectors) or pulse shape analysis (for liquid argon) are effective against electron recoil backgrounds but provide no discrimination between nuclear recoils from WIMPs and those from neutrinos, since both produce nuclear recoils with similar ionization and excitation characteristics [1,4].

The combination of spectral similarity and limited discrimination power means that without additional distinguishing features, neutrino backgrounds set a fundamental floor on the achievable sensitivity for WIMP direct detection.

## 3. The Neutrino Floor and Fog

### 3.1 Conceptual Framework

The "neutrino floor" represents a theoretical lower limit on the WIMP-nucleon cross section that can be probed in direct detection experiments, arising from the irreducible background of neutrino-induced nuclear recoils [4,5]. The concept is typically visualized as a curve in the WIMP mass versus cross section plane, below which the neutrino background rate exceeds the expected WIMP signal rate, making discovery challenging.

The original formulation of the neutrino floor was derived by requiring that the expected number of neutrino background events equals the expected number of WIMP signal events at a given WIMP mass and cross section [4]. However, this simple criterion does not account for the statistical significance required for discovery or the systematic uncertainties in the neutrino background prediction.

More sophisticated treatments consider the discovery potential as a function of exposure, incorporating both statistical fluctuations and systematic uncertainties on the neutrino background [4,5]. The discovery potential $\sigma_{\text{disco}}$ can be expressed as:

$$\sigma_{\text{disco}} \propto \sqrt{\frac{1 + \xi^2 N_\nu}{N_\nu}}$$

where $N_\nu$ is the expected number of neutrino events and $\xi$ is the relative uncertainty on the neutrino background [4]. This expression reveals that the discovery potential flattens out at large exposures when the neutrino background uncertainty dominates, setting a fundamental limit on the achievable sensitivity.

### 3.2 From Floor to Fog

Recent work has emphasized that the neutrino "floor" is not a hard limit but rather a "fog" that can be penetrated with sufficient statistics and sophisticated analysis techniques [5]. The term "neutrino fog" more accurately reflects the gradual degradation of sensitivity as experiments approach the neutrino background regime, rather than an abrupt cutoff.

The fog interpretation acknowledges several important considerations:

1. **Statistical Penetration:** With sufficiently large exposures, statistical fluctuations can allow WIMP signals to be distinguished from neutrino backgrounds even when the background rate exceeds the signal rate.

2. **Spectral Information:** Detailed spectral analysis can exploit subtle differences between WIMP and neutrino recoil spectra, particularly when considering multiple target materials.

3. **Directional Signatures:** As discussed in Section 4.2, the anisotropy of the dark matter signal provides a powerful discriminant against isotropic neutrino backgrounds.

4. **Temporal Signatures:** The annual modulation of the dark matter signal (Section 4.3) provides an additional handle for distinguishing WIMPs from steady neutrino backgrounds.

### 3.3 Exposure Dependence and Systematic Uncertainties

The impact of neutrino backgrounds on discovery potential depends critically on the experimental exposure and the systematic uncertainties in the neutrino flux predictions [4,10]. For low WIMP masses (around 6 GeV/c²), the ⁸B solar neutrino background dominates, while for high WIMP masses (around 100 GeV/c²), atmospheric and diffuse supernova neutrino backgrounds become more relevant [3].

The level and exposure at which the discovery potential flattens out is directly related to the systematic uncertainty on the relevant neutrino background [4]. Current uncertainties on solar neutrino fluxes are approximately 5-10%, while atmospheric and DSNB uncertainties are substantially larger (tens of percent to factors of two) [10]. Reducing these uncertainties through improved neutrino physics measurements and theoretical calculations will be essential for pushing the sensitivity beyond the neutrino fog.

## 4. Discrimination Techniques

### 4.1 Energy Threshold Optimization

One approach to mitigating neutrino backgrounds involves optimizing the energy threshold of the experiment [3,9]. The neutrino background most effectively mimics the WIMP signal when there is one dominant neutrino component or a smooth superposition of different neutrino sources. By adjusting the energy threshold, one can preferentially select energy regions where the neutrino background is subdominant or has a different spectral shape than the expected WIMP signal.

However, this approach has limitations. For very low WIMP masses, the signal spectrum is concentrated at low energies, making it difficult to raise the threshold without sacrificing signal efficiency. Additionally, different neutrino components dominate at different energies, and the optimal threshold depends on the WIMP mass hypothesis being tested.

Detailed studies have shown that varying the energy threshold can shift the effective neutrino floor, but cannot eliminate it entirely [9]. The optimal strategy involves using multiple analysis thresholds simultaneously, with dedicated searches optimized for different WIMP mass ranges.

### 4.2 Directional Detection

The most promising approach for distinguishing WIMP signals from neutrino backgrounds exploits the directional signature of dark matter interactions [2,11,12]. The Milky Way's dark matter halo has a characteristic velocity distribution, and the Earth's motion through this halo produces a direction-dependent event rate that modulates throughout the year.

**The Galactic Dark Matter Wind:** The Solar System moves through the Galactic dark matter halo with a velocity of approximately 220 km/s toward the constellation Cygnus. This motion creates an anisotropic flux of WIMPs, with recoils preferentially occurring in the direction of Galactic motion [2,11].

**Neutrino Background Isotropy:** In contrast, most neutrino backgrounds are isotropic or have different directional characteristics. Solar neutrinos originate from a point source (the Sun), producing a recoil direction correlated with the Sun's position, but this correlation is distinct from the Galactic dark matter wind [2]. Atmospheric neutrinos have a different angular distribution related to the Earth's atmosphere, while diffuse supernova neutrinos are truly isotropic.

**Directional Detector Capabilities:** Directional detectors can measure the vector direction of nuclear recoils, enabling discrimination between the anisotropic WIMP signal and isotropic backgrounds [2,11,12]. Studies have shown that directional experiments can probe WIMP-nucleon cross sections below the neutrino floor with little loss in sensitivity due to the neutrino background [2].

For low WIMP masses (around 6 GeV/c²), directional detectors can penetrate below the non-directional limit by several orders of magnitude, while for high WIMP masses (around 100 GeV/c²), improvements of a factor of a few are achievable [2]. Even directional detectors with limited angular resolution or one- or two-dimensional track reconstruction can maintain most of this discovery potential, with at most a factor of three reduction compared to an ideal directional detector [2].

**Implementation Challenges:** Directional detection presents significant experimental challenges. Current approaches include:

- **Gas-based time projection chambers:** Using low-pressure gases to achieve long recoil tracks that can be imaged with micro-pattern detectors [2].
- **Solid-state detectors:** Emerging technologies using crystals that can detect directional signals through phonon or charge propagation [2].
- **Emulsion-based detectors:** Nuclear emulsions can record recoil tracks with sub-micron resolution [2].

The development of meter-scale solid-state directional detectors is particularly attractive for probing WIMP cross-sections below the neutrino floor, as the high target density contrasts well with existing gaseous and emulsion targets [2].

### 4.3 Annual Modulation

The Earth's orbital motion around the Sun produces an annual modulation in the WIMP event rate with a period of one year and a peak around June 2nd [6,13]. This modulation arises from the component of the Earth's velocity along the direction of the Galactic dark matter wind, which varies as:

$$v_{\text{Earth}}(t) = v_{\odot} + v_{\oplus} \cos\gamma \cos\left[2\pi\frac{t - t_0}{T}\right]$$

where $v_{\odot} \approx 220$ km/s is the Solar System velocity, $v_{\oplus} \approx 30$ km/s is the Earth's orbital velocity, $\gamma$ is the angle between the ecliptic pole and the Galactic center direction, and $t_0$ corresponds to the modulation peak [6].

The modulation amplitude is typically a few percent of the total rate for standard halo models, but this distinctive temporal signature provides a powerful discriminant against steady backgrounds, including neutrino backgrounds which do not exhibit annual modulation on the relevant timescale [13].

**Detection Strategy:** Detecting annual modulation requires long exposure times (typically multiple years) and careful control of systematic effects that could mimic or obscure the modulation signal. The DAMA/LIBRA experiment has reported an annual modulation signal consistent with dark matter interpretations, though this interpretation remains controversial due to the lack of confirmation from other experiments [1].

**Neutrino Background Considerations:** While neutrino backgrounds are generally steady, there are potential complications. Solar neutrino fluxes vary slightly over the year due to the Earth's orbital eccentricity, but this effect is much smaller than the expected WIMP modulation. Atmospheric neutrino rates have small seasonal variations related to atmospheric conditions, but these are also subdominant to the expected dark matter signal.

### 4.4 Multi-Target Complementarity

The use of multiple target materials provides a powerful approach for distinguishing WIMP signals from neutrino backgrounds [1,4,9]. Different target nuclei have different responses to WIMP-nucleus scattering due to differences in mass, spin, and nuclear form factors. Similarly, neutrino-induced recoil spectra depend on the target nucleus through the coherence factor $A^2$ and the kinematic relations.

**WIMP Parameter Reconstruction:** If a signal is observed in multiple targets with different nuclear properties, the reconstructed WIMP parameters (mass and cross section) will be consistent only if the signal is truly from WIMP-nucleus scattering [9]. Neutrino backgrounds, being independent of the WIMP model, will produce inconsistent parameter reconstructions across different targets.

**Neutrino Background Validation:** Conversely, if the same signal is observed in multiple targets with characteristics consistent with the expected neutrino background, this can validate the background model and constrain systematic uncertainties [4].

**Target Complementarity in Practice:** Current and planned experiments exploit complementarity between different targets:

- **Liquid xenon experiments (XENONnT, LZ, PandaX-4t):** Provide excellent sensitivity for WIMP masses above approximately 10 GeV/c² [1].
- **Liquid argon experiments (DarkSide-20k, ARGO):** Offer superior background discrimination through pulse shape analysis and are particularly sensitive to low-mass WIMPs [1].
- **Semiconductor detectors (SuperCDMS):** With low energy thresholds and excellent energy resolution, these are optimized for low-mass WIMP searches [1].
- **Fluor-based detectors (PICO):** Sensitive to spin-dependent WIMP-proton interactions [1].

The Global Argon Dark Matter Collaboration exemplifies this approach, combining DarkSide-20k and the future 300-tonne ARGO detector to explore the solar and atmospheric neutrino floors [1].

### 4.5 Statistical Separation Methods

Beyond the physical discrimination techniques described above, sophisticated statistical methods can enhance the separation between WIMP signals and neutrino backgrounds [4,5]. These methods leverage the full event distribution in the space of observable quantities, including recoil energy, position, timing, and detector-specific parameters.

**Profile Likelihood Analysis:** This approach constructs a likelihood function that simultaneously fits the signal and background hypotheses to the observed data, allowing for the extraction of both the signal strength and background parameters [4]. By marginalizing over background uncertainties, profile likelihood methods can properly account for systematic errors in the neutrino background prediction.

**Machine Learning Techniques:** Modern machine learning approaches, including deep neural networks and gradient boosting methods, can be trained to distinguish signal-like events from background events based on multiple input features [5]. These techniques can identify subtle correlations that are not apparent with traditional cut-based analyses.

**Bayesian Model Selection:** Bayesian methods can compare the relative probabilities of different hypotheses (WIMP signal versus neutrino-only background) given the observed data, naturally incorporating prior uncertainties on model parameters [5].

## 5. Experimental Strategies and Future Prospects

### 5.1 Current Generation Experiments

Current direct detection experiments are approaching or have already reached the solar neutrino floor in certain WIMP mass regions [1,14]. The LZ experiment, representing the state of the art in liquid xenon technology, has set the world's most sensitive limits on WIMP-nucleon scattering and has begun to observe solar neutrino interactions that closely resemble expected dark matter signals [14].

LZ employs a dual-phase time projection chamber with 7 tonnes of liquid xenon in the active target. The detector achieves excellent background discrimination through the separation of scintillation (S1) and ionization (S2) signals, enabling rejection of electron recoil backgrounds at the level of $10^{-4}$ or better [14]. Calibration campaigns measure the detector response to both signal-like nuclear recoils and background-like electron recoils across a range of energies, providing the foundation for event classification.

The observation of solar neutrino interactions in LZ demonstrates the detector's capability to identify neutrino-like events and provides important validation of the background model [14]. As LZ accumulates more data (scheduled to collect more than 1000 days of live search data by 2028), it will become increasingly sensitive to dark matter at higher masses while also probing lower masses through threshold improvements.

### 5.2 Next Generation Facilities

The next generation of direct detection experiments is designed to operate at or beyond the neutrino floor, requiring both increased exposure and enhanced discrimination capabilities [1,15].

**DARWIN Observatory:** The DARWIN observatory represents the ultimate realization of the dual-phase xenon TPC concept, targeting a 50-tonne liquid xenon target with exposures of order 200 tonne-years [1]. DARWIN is designed to reach the atmospheric neutrino floor, requiring background levels approximately 50 times lower than current detectors. Key requirements include ultra-low radioactive backgrounds, very low energy thresholds (down to single electrons in S2-only mode), and stable multi-year operation.

**ARGO and Global Argon Program:** The Global Argon Dark Matter Collaboration is pursuing a staged approach, beginning with DarkSide-20k (50 tonnes of liquid argon) and progressing to ARGO (300 tonnes) [1]. Liquid argon offers advantages including excellent pulse shape discrimination between nuclear and electron recoils, intrinsic purity, and scalability to very large masses. The argon program targets the solar neutrino floor with DarkSide-20k and the atmospheric neutrino floor with ARGO.

**Solid-State Directional Detectors:** Emerging directional detection technologies using semiconductor materials promise to revolutionize WIMP searches below the neutrino floor [2]. These detectors can achieve meter-scale target masses while maintaining directional sensitivity through the detection of phonon or charge propagation patterns associated with nuclear recoil tracks. The ability to reject the solar neutrino background through directional discrimination makes these detectors particularly attractive for probing WIMP cross-sections below the neutrino fog.

### 5.3 Theoretical Framework Development

The interpretation of potential signals in the complex landscape near the neutrino floor requires comprehensive theoretical frameworks that systematically explore models with similar phenomenological predictions [15]. Recent developments have highlighted the critical importance of loop-induced effects and radiative corrections in determining direct detection prospects.

Many theoretically attractive dark matter scenarios feature suppressed tree-level interactions with nucleons, either through spin or momentum dependencies or accidental cancellations [15]. In these cases, loop contributions become the dominant source of detectable signals, making the understanding of quantum corrections essential for reliable predictions about the ultimate reach of direct detection experiments.

**Effective Field Theory Approaches:** Effective field theory (EFT) frameworks provide a systematic way to classify possible WIMP-nucleus interactions beyond the standard spin-independent and spin-dependent operators [15]. These frameworks identify a complete set of operators that can arise from integrating out heavy degrees of freedom, enabling model-independent searches for new physics.

**Multi-Messenger Complementarity:** The combination of direct detection with indirect detection (gamma-ray observations), collider searches, and gravitational wave astronomy provides powerful cross-checks and can help distinguish between different dark matter scenarios [15]. This multi-messenger approach becomes increasingly crucial as individual techniques approach their fundamental limits.

## 6. The Path Forward: Overcoming the Neutrino Fog

### 6.1 Reducing Systematic Uncertainties

A critical component of penetrating the neutrino fog is reducing the systematic uncertainties on neutrino fluxes and cross sections [4,10]. Current uncertainties on solar neutrino fluxes (5-10%) already significantly impact the interpretation of results near the neutrino floor, while atmospheric and DSNB uncertainties are substantially larger.

**Neutrino Physics Improvements:** Precision measurements of neutrino-nucleus scattering, including the CEvNS process measured by COHERENT, provide important constraints on neutrino interaction models [8]. Future measurements with improved statistics and systematics will reduce uncertainties on the relevant nuclear response functions.

**Theoretical Calculations:** Improved calculations of nuclear form factors and shell effects can reduce theoretical uncertainties in the expected neutrino-induced recoil spectra [10]. Lattice QCD calculations of nuclear matrix elements and advances in many-body perturbation theory are contributing to this effort.

**Background Characterization:** Direct detection experiments themselves can contribute to reducing neutrino background uncertainties by measuring the neutrino-induced event rates [14]. The observation of solar neutrinos in XENONnT and PandaX-4T represents the first evidence of CEvNS from solar neutrinos in a dark matter detector, providing important validation of the expected rates.

### 6.2 Enhanced Discrimination Techniques

Beyond the techniques described in Section 4, several emerging approaches promise enhanced discrimination between WIMP signals and neutrino backgrounds.

**Migdal Effect:** When a WIMP scatters off an atomic nucleus, the resulting excitation can lead to the emission of electrons (the Migdal effect) or photons [16]. This produces a distinctive signature with both nuclear and electronic components that can provide additional discrimination power. The Migdal effect is particularly relevant for low-energy recoils where the primary nuclear recoil signal is difficult to detect.

**Recoil Imaging:** Advanced detector concepts can image the detailed topology of nuclear recoil tracks, enabling particle identification based on the track morphology [16]. This approach can distinguish between different types of nuclear recoils based on their range, branching ratio, and energy deposition profile.

**Coherent Magnon Excitation:** In crystal targets, nuclear recoils can excite collective spin waves (magnons) with distinctive signatures that differ from neutrino-induced excitations [16]. This approach is being explored in solid-state directional detectors.

### 6.3 The Role of Discovery and Characterization

Once a potential WIMP signal is identified, the characterization of its properties becomes essential for distinguishing it from neutrino backgrounds [14]. A genuine WIMP signal should exhibit:

1. **Consistency with directional signature:** The recoil directions should be correlated with the Galactic dark matter wind.
2. **Annual modulation:** The event rate should exhibit the characteristic yearly variation.
3. **Multi-target consistency:** The signal strength across different target materials should be consistent with WIMP-nucleus scattering predictions.
4. **Spectral consistency:** The recoil energy spectrum should match the expected WIMP shape.

Conversely, a neutrino background would not exhibit these features, providing a robust framework for confirmation or refutation of potential signals.

## 7. Conclusion

The distinction between new physics signals from WIMP scattering and neutrino backgrounds represents one of the most significant challenges in modern dark matter direct detection. The fundamental similarity between WIMP-induced and neutrino-induced nuclear recoils—both in energy spectrum and event topology—means that standard discrimination techniques are insufficient to separate these signals in the most sensitive parameter regions.

However, the neutrino "floor" is not an insurmountable barrier. The development of sophisticated discrimination techniques—directional detection, annual modulation analysis, multi-target complementarity, and advanced statistical methods—provides powerful tools for distinguishing genuine dark matter signals from neutrino backgrounds. Directional detectors, in particular, offer a path to probe WIMP cross-sections below the neutrino floor by exploiting the anisotropy of the Galactic dark matter wind against the isotropy of neutrino backgrounds.

The experimental program moving forward combines increased exposure with enhanced discrimination capabilities. Next-generation facilities like DARWIN and ARGO will operate at the neutrino floor, requiring unprecedented control of backgrounds and systematic uncertainties. Emerging directional detection technologies using solid-state targets promise to revolutionize the field by providing the angular resolution needed to reject neutrino backgrounds through their distinct directional signatures.

The ultimate success of direct detection searches will depend on the synergy between experimental advances and theoretical developments. Comprehensive theoretical frameworks that systematically explore the landscape of dark matter models, combined with precise calculations of neutrino physics and nuclear responses, will be essential for interpreting potential signals in the complex environment near the neutrino fog.

As experiments continue to push the boundaries of sensitivity, the distinction between WIMP signals and neutrino backgrounds will become increasingly nuanced, requiring the full arsenal of discrimination techniques developed by the community. The coming decade promises to be transformative for dark matter direct detection, with the potential to either discover the elusive WIMP or to establish that new approaches are needed to probe the remaining parameter space.
