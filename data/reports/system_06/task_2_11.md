# Balancing On-Resistance and Reliability in GaN Power Devices: Design Approaches for Synergistic Effects of Doping Gradients and Interface Trap States

## Abstract

Gallium nitride (GaN) high electron mobility transistors (HEMTs) represent a transformative technology for power electronics, offering unprecedented performance through their unique polarization-induced two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) channel. However, the simultaneous optimization of on-resistance ($R_{on}$) and long-term reliability remains a critical challenge, particularly when considering the synergistic effects arising from doping concentration gradients and interface trap state density. This report presents a comprehensive analysis of design approaches that balance these competing requirements, examining the fundamental physics of 2DEG formation, the mechanisms of trap-assisted degradation, and the strategic implementation of epitaxial engineering solutions. Through systematic evaluation of step-doped channel architectures, graded AlGaN back barriers, and advanced surface passivation techniques, this analysis establishes a framework for achieving optimal device performance while mitigating reliability concerns associated with electric field crowding and dynamic $R_{on}$ degradation.

## 1. Introduction

The continued advancement of power electronic systems demands semiconductor devices capable of delivering higher power density, improved efficiency, and enhanced reliability at reduced system costs. GaN-based HEMTs have emerged as a leading candidate technology to address these requirements, leveraging the exceptional material properties of III-nitride semiconductors—including wide bandgap energy (3.4 eV for GaN), high critical electric field (exceeding 3 MV/cm), and high electron saturation velocity—to achieve performance metrics that surpass traditional silicon and silicon carbide counterparts [1]. The distinctive feature of AlGaN/GaN heterostructures is the formation of a high-density 2DEG at the heterojunction interface without the need for extrinsic doping, resulting from the combined effects of spontaneous and piezoelectric polarization charges [2].

Despite these advantages, the commercialization of GaN power devices has been constrained by reliability challenges that are intimately linked to the very mechanisms enabling their superior electrical performance. The polarization-induced 2DEG, while providing high carrier density and electron mobility (exceeding 2000 cm²/V·s), also creates a system highly sensitive to charge trapping at surface states and buffer interfaces [3]. These trapping phenomena manifest as current collapse, dynamic $R_{on}$ increase, and threshold voltage instabilities—all of which degrade device reliability under actual operating conditions.

The design of GaN power devices must therefore navigate a complex trade-off space where attempts to reduce on-resistance through increased carrier density or modified epitaxial structures may inadvertently exacerbate trapping effects, while aggressive approaches to trap mitigation may compromise current-carrying capability. This challenge is further complicated by the synergistic interactions between doping concentration gradients in the epitaxial layers and the density of interface trap states, which collectively determine the electric field distribution, carrier confinement, and long-term stability of the device [4].

## 2. Fundamental Mechanisms of On-Resistance in GaN HEMTs

### 2.1 Polarization-Induced 2DEG Formation

The on-resistance of GaN transistors is fundamentally linked to the density of electrons in the 2DEG channel, which arises from the intrinsic polarization properties of III-nitride materials. In wurtzite-structured AlGaN/GaN heterostructures, the total polarization ($\mathbf{P}$) comprises contributions from spontaneous polarization ($P_{sp}$) and piezoelectric polarization ($P_{pz}$), with the latter arising from strain-induced lattice mismatch between the AlGaN barrier and GaN channel layers [5]. The net polarization charge density at the AlGaN/GaN interface can be expressed as:

$$\rho_p = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{P} = \frac{\partial P_{sp}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial P_{pz}}{\partial x}$$

where $x$ represents the aluminum mole fraction in the AlGaN layer. This polarization gradient induces a fixed positive sheet charge at the heterojunction interface, which attracts free electrons to form the 2DEG. The sheet electron concentration ($N_{2DEG}$) is directly proportional to the net polarization charge and can reach values on the order of $10^{13}$ cm⁻² without any intentional doping of the barrier layer [6].

The thickness and aluminum content of the AlGaN barrier layer serve as primary design parameters for controlling the 2DEG density. Increasing the aluminum mole fraction enhances the piezoelectric polarization due to greater lattice mismatch, while simultaneously increasing the spontaneous polarization difference between the layers. However, this relationship is not strictly linear, as the piezoelectric coefficients and elastic constants vary with composition. The Ambacher model provides a comprehensive framework for calculating the 2DEG carrier density by applying Gauss's theorem across the heterostructure stack [7]:

$$\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_{AlGaN} E_{AlGaN} - \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_{GaN} E_{GaN} = \sigma(x) - q N_{2DEG}$$

where $\varepsilon_{AlGaN}$ and $\varepsilon_{GaN}$ are the relative permittivities of the respective materials, $E_{AlGaN}$ and $E_{GaN}$ represent the electric fields in each layer, $\sigma(x)$ is the net polarization charge at the interface, and $q$ is the elementary charge.

### 2.2 Components of On-Resistance

The total on-resistance of a GaN HEMT comprises multiple contributions that must be considered in any comprehensive optimization strategy. In a first-order approximation, $R_{on}$ can be expressed as the sum of the channel resistance ($R_{channel}$), access region resistances, and contact resistances [8]:

$$R_{on} = R_{channel} + R_{source} + R_{drain} + R_{contact}$$

The channel resistance itself depends on the 2DEG carrier density and electron mobility according to:

$$R_{channel} = \frac{L_{ch}}{q \mu_n N_{2DEG} W}$$

where $L_{ch}$ is the channel length, $\mu_n$ is the electron mobility, and $W$ is the device width. This relationship highlights the fundamental trade-off inherent in GaN HEMT design: increasing $N_{2DEG}$ through higher aluminum content or thicker barriers reduces $R_{channel}$ but may simultaneously increase electric field peaks and exacerbate trapping effects under high-voltage operation.

The access region resistances, comprising the source-to-gate and gate-to-drain access regions, become increasingly significant as device dimensions are scaled for higher frequency operation or higher current capability. These regions are characterized by lateral electron transport through the 2DEG channel, with the resistance strongly dependent on the specific contact resistance at the metal-semiconductor interface and the sheet resistance of the access regions [9].

## 3. Interface Trap States: Mechanisms and Impact on Reliability

### 3.1 Origins and Distribution of Interface Traps

The reliability of GaN power devices is profoundly influenced by the presence of charge trapping states at multiple interfaces within the device structure. These trap states arise from several distinct sources, each contributing to the overall trap density and energy distribution within the bandgap [10]. Surface states at the AlGaN/air interface or AlGaN/dielectric interface can capture and emit electrons on time scales ranging from microseconds to seconds, depending on the trap energy level and temperature. Buffer states within the GaN channel layer or at the GaN/substrate interface may arise from point defects, dislocations, or impurity incorporation during epitaxial growth.

The density and energy distribution of interface traps are strongly influenced by the specific passivation scheme employed and the quality of the dielectric/semiconductor interface. High-quality dielectric passivation layers can reduce surface leakage and suppress surface-related conduction paths, but may introduce fixed charges or interface states that modulate the 2DEG density [11]. The interface between the passivation layer and the AlGaN barrier represents a particularly critical region, as charges trapped at this interface can modulate the electric field distribution in the access regions and contribute to current collapse phenomena.

### 3.2 Dynamic $R_{on}$ Degradation and Current Collapse

One of the most significant reliability concerns in GaN power devices is the dynamic increase in on-resistance observed when the device transitions from off-state to on-state following high-voltage stress. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as current collapse or dynamic $R_{on}$ degradation, results from the trapping of electrons at surface states or buffer states during off-state high-field operation [12]. The trapped charges create a virtual gate effect that depletes the 2DEG channel in the access regions, effectively increasing the channel resistance until the trapped charges are emitted or the device is subjected to forward bias stress that facilitates detrapping.

The severity of current collapse is strongly correlated with the breakdown voltage rating of the device, with higher-voltage devices exhibiting more pronounced effects due to the larger electric field magnitudes and extended depletion regions in the access regions [13]. This correlation arises from the fundamental relationship between breakdown voltage and device geometry: achieving higher blocking capability requires increased gate-to-drain spacing ($L_{GD}$), which results in more gradual electric field distribution but also exposes a larger surface area to high-field conditions that promote electron trapping.

The time-dependent nature of current collapse is characterized by capture and emission time constants that span a wide range, reflecting the distributed nature of trap states with varying energy levels. Fast traps with capture times in the nanosecond to microsecond range may cause frequency dispersion in RF applications, while slow traps with capture times extending to milliseconds or seconds can cause significant dynamic $R_{on}$ effects in power switching applications [14]. Understanding and mitigating this multi-timescale trapping behavior requires comprehensive characterization using techniques such as pulsed I-V measurements, frequency-dependent capacitance-voltage (C-V) and conductance-voltage (G-V) spectroscopy, and deep level transient spectroscopy (DLTS).

### 3.3 Threshold Voltage Instabilities

In addition to dynamic $R_{on}$ effects, interface trap states can cause threshold voltage ($V_{TH}$) instabilities that compromise the reliable operation of GaN power devices. Trapping at the gate-stack interface or within the gate dielectric can result in positive or negative bias temperature instability (BTI) effects, depending on the dominant trap type and the polarity of the applied stress [15]. In p-GaN gate HEMTs, which represent the primary architecture for enhancement-mode (normally-off) operation, hole trapping at the p-GaN/AlGaN interface can cause positive $V_{TH}$ shifts, while electron trapping in the gate dielectric can cause negative shifts.

The physical mechanisms underlying threshold voltage instabilities involve charge transfer between the semiconductor and the trap states, with the rate of trapping and detrapping determined by the trap energy level relative to the Fermi energy and the availability of carriers for capture. In MIS-gate structures, the quality of the semiconductor/dielectric interface becomes particularly critical, as high interface trap density can lead to significant $V_{TH}$ hysteresis and long-term drift during device operation [16].

## 4. Doping Concentration Gradients: Design Considerations and Effects

### 4.1 Epitaxial Doping Strategies for Reliability Enhancement

The strategic introduction of dopants into the epitaxial layers of GaN HEMTs represents a powerful approach for improving device reliability while maintaining acceptable on-resistance performance. Carbon and iron doping of the buffer layers has been widely employed to increase the resistivity of the GaN buffer, thereby suppressing parasitic carrier injection from the substrate and reducing buffer-related leakage currents [17]. The deep acceptor nature of carbon in GaN creates deep levels that compensate shallow donors, effectively increasing the resistivity of the unintentionally doped GaN layer and preventing punch-through effects that would otherwise limit the breakdown voltage.

The concentration and distribution of dopants in the buffer layers must be carefully optimized to balance the competing requirements of high breakdown voltage and low buffer-related trapping. Excessive carbon doping can introduce deep levels that act as trapping centers, potentially exacerbating current collapse effects, while insufficient doping may fail to adequately suppress buffer leakage [18]. The vertical structure of the buffer stack, including any step-graded or superlattice-based buffer layers, must be designed to provide effective carrier confinement while minimizing the incorporation of trapping centers.

### 4.2 Step-Doped Channel Architectures

A particularly innovative approach to balancing on-resistance and reliability involves the implementation of step-doped channel (SDC) architectures in AlGaN/GaN HEMTs. By introducing controlled doping concentration gradients into the GaN channel layer, the electric field distribution along the heterojunction interface can be reshaped to reduce peak field magnitudes while maintaining low on-resistance [19]. This approach represents a departure from conventional uniform doping strategies, instead leveraging the spatial variation of dopant concentration to achieve more favorable field profiles.

Numerical and analytical studies have demonstrated that an optimized doping concentration gradient of approximately $2 \times 10^{16}$ cm⁻³ per step can achieve significant improvements in breakdown voltage while simultaneously reducing the specific on-resistance ($R_{on,sp}$) [20]. The mechanism underlying this improvement involves the modulation of the potential distribution in the channel layer, which reduces the electric field crowding at the gate edge on the drain side—a region that typically experiences the highest field magnitudes and is most susceptible to reliability degradation.

The thickness of the channel layer in SDC-HEMTs also plays a critical role in determining the trade-off between on-resistance and breakdown voltage. Thicker channel layers provide lower $R_{on,sp}$ due to reduced vertical electric field and improved carrier confinement, with studies showing improvements from 0.80 mΩ·cm² to 0.70 mΩ·cm² as channel thickness increases from sub-micron values to approximately 1 μm [21]. However, excessively thick channels may introduce background carrier-related leakage that degrades breakdown voltage, establishing an optimal thickness range that must be determined based on the specific device requirements and epitaxial growth capabilities.

### 4.3 Graded AlGaN Back Barriers

The implementation of graded AlGaN or AlN back barriers represents another effective strategy for improving carrier confinement and reducing short-channel effects in GaN HEMTs. By introducing a graded composition profile between the GaN channel and the underlying buffer layers, the back barrier creates a potential step that impedes electron spillover into the buffer during high-field operation [22]. This enhanced confinement reduces buffer-related trapping and improves the breakdown voltage by preventing premature punch-through breakdown.

The design of graded back barriers must consider the trade-off between carrier confinement and on-resistance, as the back barrier potential step may increase the effective barrier height for electron injection from the source. The composition gradient profile—whether linear, exponential, or stepped—affects both the electric field distribution and the carrier transport characteristics, requiring careful optimization through TCAD simulation and experimental validation [23]. The thickness of the graded region and the final aluminum mole fraction determine the magnitude of the potential step and the effectiveness of carrier confinement.

## 5. Synergistic Effects: Integrated Design Approach

### 5.1 Coupling Between Doping Gradients and Interface Traps

The simultaneous optimization of doping concentration gradients and interface trap density requires an integrated design approach that considers the synergistic interactions between these factors. The electric field distribution established by the doping profile directly influences the capture and emission dynamics of interface traps, while the presence of trapped charge modulates the effective potential distribution in the device [24]. This bidirectional coupling creates a complex optimization space where improvements in one aspect may have unintended consequences for the other.

In the gate-drain access region, for example, the peak electric field is determined by the combined effects of the applied bias, the doping profile in the channel and buffer layers, and the charge state of surface traps. A doping concentration gradient that reduces the peak field magnitude may simultaneously reduce the capture rate of interface traps, thereby mitigating current collapse effects [25]. Conversely, aggressive surface passivation that reduces interface trap density may allow for more aggressive doping profiles that further optimize the field distribution.

The time-dependent behavior of trapped charge adds another dimension to this optimization problem, as the capture and emission time constants depend on both the trap energy distribution and the instantaneous electric field. Devices operating under switching conditions experience time-varying fields that modulate the trapping dynamics, potentially leading to complex transient behaviors that are difficult to predict from static characterization alone [26]. Comprehensive device simulation incorporating both carrier transport and trapping physics is essential for understanding these dynamic effects and guiding design optimization.

### 5.2 Electric Field Management Through Integrated Design

The management of electric field distribution represents a unifying principle for balancing on-resistance and reliability in GaN power devices. Peak electric fields that exceed the critical value for impact ionization or dielectric breakdown can initiate degradation mechanisms that ultimately limit device lifetime, while non-uniform field distributions can create localized regions of enhanced trapping [27]. The goal of integrated design is therefore to achieve a field distribution that minimizes both peak magnitudes and spatial gradients while maintaining low on-resistance.

Field plate structures represent a well-established approach for reshaping the electric field distribution in GaN HEMTs, using conductive extensions of the gate metal or dedicated field plate electrodes to spread the field more uniformly across the gate-drain region [28]. The design of field plates involves optimization of the plate length, height, and termination to achieve the desired field profile without introducing excessive parasitic capacitance that would degrade switching performance. The interaction between field plate geometry and the underlying doping profile must be considered, as the field plate-induced field modification may interact constructively or destructively with the field shaping provided by doping gradients.

Lateral device termination structures, including junction termination extensions (JTE) and variation of lateral doping (VLD) approaches, provide additional mechanisms for electric field management in high-voltage GaN devices [29]. These structures use controlled doping variations to create a gradual transition from the high-field region near the active device to the low-field region at the device periphery, reducing the peak field at the termination edge and improving the breakdown voltage uniformity. The optimization of termination structures through TCAD simulation enables the achievement of breakdown voltages approaching the ideal parallel-plane limit while minimizing the device area penalty.

## 6. Optimized Design Strategies

### 6.1 Epitaxial Structure Optimization

The optimization of the epitaxial structure provides the foundation for achieving balanced on-resistance and reliability in GaN power devices. The AlGaN barrier thickness and aluminum content must be selected to provide adequate 2DEG density for low on-resistance while avoiding excessive piezoelectric strain that could introduce reliability concerns [30]. The typical barrier thickness range of 10-30 nm represents a trade-off between 2DEG density and the electric field distribution under high-voltage operation, with thicker barriers providing higher 2DEG density but concentrating more of the applied voltage drop across the barrier layer.

The GaN channel thickness must be optimized to achieve high electron density in the 2DEG, good electron confinement, and low trapping effects simultaneously. For SDC-HEMT structures, the channel thickness of approximately 1 μm has been identified as optimal for balancing these requirements, providing sufficient thickness for effective carrier confinement while avoiding the background carrier leakage that degrades breakdown voltage in thicker layers [31]. The doping concentration gradient within the channel should be designed to reshape the electric field distribution, with the optimized gradient of $2 \times 10^{16}$ cm⁻³ per step providing significant improvements in both breakdown voltage and on-resistance.

Buffer layer design, including the incorporation of carbon or iron doping, step-graded buffers, and superlattice structures, plays a critical role in determining the breakdown voltage and buffer-related leakage. The doping concentration in the buffer must be sufficient to provide adequate compensation of shallow donors and impurities, but not so high as to introduce excessive deep level trapping [32]. The transition between the channel and buffer regions should be designed to maintain sharp heterojunction interfaces while providing effective carrier confinement through back barrier structures.

### 6.2 Surface Passivation and Gate Stack Engineering

The surface passivation strategy directly impacts both the interface trap density and the surface leakage current, making it a critical element of the overall device design. High-quality dielectric layers, deposited using techniques such as plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) or atomic layer deposition (ALD), can provide effective surface passivation while minimizing the introduction of fixed charges or interface states [33]. The choice of dielectric material—whether silicon nitride, silicon oxide, aluminum oxide, or combinations thereof—affects both the electrical characteristics and the thermal stability of the passivation layer.

The interface between the passivation dielectric and the AlGaN barrier requires particular attention, as charges trapped at this interface can modulate the 2DEG density and contribute to current collapse. In-situ surface treatment prior to dielectric deposition, including plasma exposure or chemical cleaning, can reduce the interface trap density by removing surface contaminants and terminating surface states [34]. The post-deposition annealing conditions also affect the interface quality, with optimal annealing temperature and ambient determined by the specific dielectric material and the desired electrical characteristics.

For p-GaN gate HEMTs and MIS-gate structures, the gate stack design encompasses additional considerations related to the gate dielectric formation, the p-GaN/AlGaN interface, and the metal/p-GaN contact resistance. The gate dielectric must provide adequate gate capacitance while minimizing interface trap density and preventing excessive gate leakage [35]. The p-GaN layer thickness and magnesium doping concentration must be optimized to achieve the desired threshold voltage while maintaining good gate forward characteristics.

### 6.3 Device Geometry Optimization

The device geometry, including the gate length, gate-to-drain spacing, and field plate design, must be optimized in conjunction with the epitaxial structure to achieve the desired performance and reliability. The gate length primarily affects the channel resistance and the transit frequency, with shorter gates providing higher frequency performance but potentially increased short-channel effects [36]. For power switching applications, the gate length is typically in the range of 0.5-2 μm, balancing the requirements for low on-resistance and adequate control of the channel.

The gate-to-drain spacing ($L_{GD}$) is the primary geometric parameter determining the breakdown voltage, with longer spacings providing higher breakdown voltage but increased on-resistance due to the extended access region. The relationship between $L_{GD}$ and breakdown voltage is approximately linear in the sub-micron to few-micron range, but saturates at larger spacings due to the dominance of buffer-related breakdown mechanisms [37]. The optimization of $L_{GD}$ must therefore consider both the target breakdown voltage and the acceptable on-resistance penalty.

Field plate design provides an additional mechanism for breakdown voltage optimization, allowing for shorter $L_{GD}$ spacings while maintaining high breakdown voltage through electric field spreading. The field plate length and height determine the degree of field spreading, with longer and higher plates providing more effective field reduction but also increasing the gate-to-drain capacitance [38]. The termination of the field plate at the drain edge must be designed to avoid field crowding at the plate termination while maintaining the desired breakdown voltage.

## 7. Conclusion

The design of GaN power devices that balance on-resistance and reliability in the presence of synergistic effects from doping concentration gradients and interface trap state density requires a holistic approach that considers the fundamental physics of polarization-induced 2DEG formation, the mechanisms of trap-assisted degradation, and the interactions between epitaxial structure, surface passivation, and device geometry. The step-doped channel architecture represents a particularly promising innovation, achieving breakdown voltage improvements of up to 60% while simultaneously reducing specific on-resistance through optimized electric field distribution.

The optimized doping concentration gradient of $2 \times 10^{16}$ cm⁻³ per step, combined with a channel thickness of approximately 1 μm, provides a design point that balances carrier confinement, breakdown voltage, and dynamic $R_{on}$ performance. The implementation of graded AlGaN back barriers and high-quality surface passivation further enhances device reliability by reducing buffer leakage and suppressing surface trapping effects. The integration of these epitaxial and surface engineering approaches with optimized device geometry—including appropriate field plate design and lateral termination structures—enables the achievement of high-performance GaN power devices suitable for demanding applications in power conversion, RF amplification, and electric vehicle systems.

Future research directions should focus on the development of in-situ characterization techniques for monitoring epitaxial growth dynamics and trap formation, as well as novel dopant engineering strategies that can further improve the trade-off between on-resistance and reliability. The continued advancement of TCAD simulation capabilities, incorporating comprehensive physics models for polarization, trapping, and hot carrier effects, will be essential for guiding the next generation of GaN power device designs.
