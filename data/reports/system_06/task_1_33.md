# Balancing Medication Adjustments and Lifestyle Modifications in Coronary Heart Disease Secondary Prevention: A Comprehensive Analysis of LDL Target Management and Recurrence Risk Reduction

## Executive Summary

Coronary heart disease (CHD) remains the leading cause of mortality globally, with approximately 805,000 myocardial infarctions (MIs) occurring annually in the United States alone, and nearly one-quarter of these events happening in individuals with a prior MI history [1]. This persistent burden of recurrent cardiovascular events underscores the critical importance of effective secondary prevention strategies. Contemporary approaches to secondary prevention require a delicate balance between guideline-directed pharmacotherapy—particularly lipid-lowering therapy targeting low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) reduction—and comprehensive lifestyle modification programs. The integration of these two pillars of secondary prevention represents the cornerstone of modern cardiovascular risk management, yet significant challenges persist in optimizing this balance to minimize recurrence risk.

This report examines the current landscape of CHD secondary prevention, focusing specifically on how clinicians navigate the complex interplay between medication titration for LDL targets and the promotion of sustained lifestyle modification adherence. Drawing upon recent clinical evidence and guideline recommendations, this analysis explores the pharmacological foundations of secondary prevention, the evidence supporting lifestyle interventions, barriers to adherence across both domains, and emerging strategies for integrated care delivery that may improve patient outcomes.

## Introduction: The Imperative of Secondary Prevention in Coronary Heart Disease

### Global Burden and Recurrence Risk

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) have emerged as the predominant cause of disease burden worldwide, with coronary artery disease representing the single largest contributor to this burden [2]. The epidemiological data reveal a sobering reality: despite significant advances in acute coronary care, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and optimized medical therapy, recurrent coronary events continue to pose a substantial clinical challenge. Approximately one in twenty adults over the age of 20 years in the United States is affected by coronary artery disease, making it not only the leading cause of death within the country but also the primary cause of mortality globally [1].

The significance of secondary prevention cannot be overstated when considering that nearly 25% of MIs occur in individuals who have experienced a prior MI [1]. While post-MI survival rates and early recurrent MI rates (within the first year) have improved markedly since the 1970s due to advances in both interventional cardiology and pharmacological management, the specter of recurrent cardiovascular events remains a persistent concern. This reality has catalyzed a renewed focus on comprehensive secondary prevention strategies that address the full spectrum of modifiable risk factors contributing to cardiovascular recurrence.

### The Dual Pillars of Secondary Prevention

Contemporary guidelines from the American Heart Association (AHA) and the American College of Cardiology Foundation (ACCF) establish that secondary prevention of CHD rests upon two fundamental pillars: guideline-directed pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modification [3]. These approaches are not mutually exclusive but rather complementary, with each reinforcing the efficacy of the other in reducing residual cardiovascular risk. The pharmacological pillar encompasses evidence-based medications including beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-I) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), statins and other lipid-lowering agents, and antithrombotic therapy [3]. The lifestyle pillar addresses behavioral risk factors including smoking, physical inactivity, poor nutrition, and psychosocial stressors.

The concept of "residual risk" has gained increasing recognition in cardiovascular medicine, referring to the persistent risk of cardiovascular events observed among patients receiving optimal medical therapy [2]. This residual risk may be partially attributable to variability in cardiovascular risk factors over time, including visit-to-visit variability in blood pressure, blood glucose, and lipid levels [2]. Understanding how to address this residual risk through the optimal integration of pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modification represents one of the central challenges in contemporary cardiovascular prevention.

## Pharmacological Foundations of LDL Management in Secondary Prevention

### The Evidence Base for Lipid-Lowering Therapy

Lipid-lowering therapy (LLT), particularly statin therapy, forms the pharmacological cornerstone of LDL management in CHD secondary prevention. The rationale for aggressive LDL lowering in patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is grounded in extensive randomized clinical trial evidence demonstrating that LDL-C reduction translates directly into cardiovascular event reduction. Contemporary guidelines recommend high-intensity statin therapy for all patients with established ASCVD, with the goal of achieving at least a 50% reduction in LDL-C levels and ideally reaching LDL-C levels below 70 mg/dL or even below 55 mg/dL in very high-risk patients.

The importance of medication adherence in realizing the benefits of lipid-lowering therapy cannot be overstated. Large cohort studies across diverse healthcare settings have consistently documented high rates of discontinuation of lipid-lowering therapies and poor adherence to prescribed drug regimens [4]. These adherence challenges have significant clinical consequences, as failure to achieve target lipid levels translates directly into suboptimal cardiovascular protection. Furthermore, nonadherence has been shown to impact subsequent clinical outcomes substantially, with patients who do not take their medication as prescribed being more likely to require hospitalization for cardiovascular events [4].

### Adherence Trajectories and Clinical Outcomes

Recent research has illuminated the heterogeneity in medication adherence patterns among patients prescribed lipid-lowering therapy. Using group-based trajectory modeling, investigators have identified distinct adherence trajectories that persist over time. Among adults with or at risk for ASCVD, approximately 64.1% of patients demonstrate a high adherence trajectory, while 24.3% follow an intermediate trajectory, and 11.6% exhibit a low adherence pattern [5]. These adherence trajectories have profound implications for clinical outcomes, with low adherence associated with a hazard ratio of 1.20 (95% CI 1.16, 1.23) for composite ASCVD outcomes compared to high adherence [5].

The threshold of adherence necessary to achieve clinical benefit has been informed by landmark clinical trials. In the West of Scotland Coronary Prevention Study (WOSCOPS), patients taking at least 75% of their prescribed dose of pravastatin demonstrated significantly lower rates of non-fatal MI, revascularization procedures, death from any cause, and cardiovascular death compared to those taking less than 75% of their prescribed dose [4]. More striking evidence comes from studies examining statin adherence after initial MI, where patients with 80% or better adherence to statin treatment had an adjusted relative risk of recurrent MI of 0.19 compared to those not taking statins, with no significant risk reduction observed among patients with adherence below 80% [4].

### The Challenge of Treatment Discontinuation

Despite the well-documented safety and effectiveness of lipid-lowering therapy, significant gaps persist in both prescriber-guideline concordance and patient adherence. Current data indicate that approximately 21% of patients with established ASCVD are not taking lipid-lowering therapy, representing a substantial gap in secondary prevention delivery [5]. Even among patients who initiate therapy, discontinuation rates remain alarmingly high, with 32.4% of lipid-lowering therapy users discontinuing treatment within 12 months [5].

The clinical consequences of treatment discontinuation extend beyond simple loss of therapeutic benefit. Studies have demonstrated that withdrawal of statins or sudden reduction of dose can increase the rate of thrombotic events, suggesting that nonadherence and poor persistence may contribute to incremental risk through mechanisms beyond simple loss of LDL lowering [4]. This finding underscores the importance of sustained adherence rather than episodic use and highlights the need for strategies that support long-term persistence with therapy.

## Lifestyle Modification as a Complementary Strategy

### Evidence Supporting Lifestyle Interventions

While pharmacotherapy addresses the pathophysiological consequences of atherosclerosis through specific mechanisms, lifestyle modification targets the behavioral and environmental factors that contribute to cardiovascular risk. Lifestyle interventions including physical activity and healthy dietary patterns play a pivotal role in both primary and secondary prevention of CVD, demonstrating efficacy in managing multiple cardiovascular risk factors including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity [6]. The AHA has identified key lifestyle factors necessary to achieve "ideal" cardiovascular health, yet data suggest that only approximately 5% of Americans have successfully adopted these lifestyle factors [6].

The evidence supporting lifestyle interventions in secondary prevention is substantial. Post-MI patients who reported adherence to healthy lifestyle habits—including smoking cessation, regular exercise, and healthy dietary patterns—at 30 days post-hospital discharge demonstrated improved outcomes [3]. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) programs, which incorporate comprehensive lifestyle modification strategies alongside structured exercise training, have been shown to be effective in improving cardiovascular health outcomes in patients with established CHD [1].

### Components of Comprehensive Lifestyle Modification

The lifestyle modification component of secondary prevention encompasses multiple domains, each contributing to overall cardiovascular risk reduction. Smoking cessation represents perhaps the most impactful single lifestyle modification, with prior studies demonstrating that a combination of pharmacotherapy and behavioral therapy can improve the success rate of smoking cessation by 70–100% compared to minimal intervention [1]. Effective smoking cessation strategies include behavioral counseling (individual or group, telephone or text message, mobile applications, and self-help approaches), nicotine replacement therapy, and pharmacotherapy with agents such as varenicline or bupropion [1].

Dietary modification represents another cornerstone of lifestyle intervention, with recommendations emphasizing the adoption of a healthy, balanced diet focused on whole grains, fruits, and vegetables to support weight management and reduce cholesterol and blood pressure [1]. The Mediterranean dietary pattern, rich in plant-based foods, olive oil, and moderate in fish and poultry, has demonstrated particular benefit in cardiovascular populations. Physical activity promotion is equally critical, with regular exercise contributing to weight management, improved lipid profiles, enhanced endothelial function, and better psychological well-being.

Psychological interventions, including cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), have emerged as important components of comprehensive lifestyle modification. CBT has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety in patients with CAD, with benefits extending to improved quality of life and potentially better cardiovascular outcomes [1]. Given that depression and anxiety can influence lifestyle decisions and medication adherence, screening for and treating mental health disorders represents an important element of holistic secondary prevention [1].

### The Relationship Between Lifestyle and Risk Factor Variability

An important consideration in balancing pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modification is the relationship between lifestyle interventions and variability in cardiovascular risk factors. Recent evidence has raised the possibility that variability of cardiovascular risk factors may represent a phenomenon contributing to residual risk among those with high cardiovascular risk already receiving optimal medical therapy [2]. Visit-to-visit variability in blood pressure, blood glucose, and lipids has been identified as a potential independent risk factor for cardiovascular events.

Interestingly, some evidence suggests that lifestyle interventions may not be effective in reducing visit-to-visit variability of cardiovascular risk factors. A secondary analysis of data from the Trials of Hypertension Prevention II, which randomized participants to weight loss, sodium reduction, combination interventions, or usual care, found that lifestyle intervention including weight loss and sodium reduction may not be effective in reducing visit-to-visit variability of blood pressure in overweight adults with high-normal diastolic blood pressure [2]. This finding has important implications for understanding the complementary roles of pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modification, suggesting that while lifestyle changes address underlying cardiovascular risk through multiple pathways, they may not fully address the variability component of residual risk.

## The Critical Role of Cardiac Rehabilitation

### Structure and Components of Cardiac Rehabilitation

Cardiac rehabilitation represents the most comprehensive integration of pharmacotherapy optimization and lifestyle modification in a single secondary prevention platform. CR is described as an effective, comprehensive intervention that incorporates most secondary prevention strategies tailored to the individual patient [1]. The vulnerable period post-MI or after PCI represents a critical window during which clinicians must work closely with patients to optimize secondary prevention, involving starting appropriate pharmacotherapy, emphasizing lifestyle changes, and promoting participation in CR [1].

The structured nature of CR programs provides several advantages over unstructured lifestyle advice. CR programs typically include supervised exercise training, structured education on heart-healthy living, counseling on risk factor modification, and support for medication adherence. The multidisciplinary nature of CR, involving physicians, nurses, exercise physiologists, dietitians, and mental health professionals, allows for comprehensive addressing of the multiple domains contributing to cardiovascular risk.

### Evidence for Cardiac Rehabilitation Outcomes

The benefits of CR participation extend across multiple outcome domains. Patients who engage in CR demonstrate improvements in functional capacity, lipid profiles, blood pressure control, weight management, and psychological well-being. Beyond these intermediate endpoints, CR participation is associated with reduced mortality and fewer recurrent cardiovascular events. The mechanism of benefit likely involves both the direct effects of exercise training on cardiovascular function and the indirect effects of improved risk factor control and medication adherence facilitated by the structured CR environment.

## Barriers to Adherence: Understanding the Challenges

### Multifactorial Nature of Nonadherence

Patient nonadherence to guideline-recommended long-term pharmacologic secondary preventative therapies after acute coronary syndrome is multifactorial, encompassing patient-related, therapy-related, healthcare system-related, and socioeconomic factors [7]. Understanding these barriers is essential for developing effective interventions to improve adherence in both the pharmacological and lifestyle domains.

Nonadherence can be categorized as intentional or unintentional. Intentional nonadherence involves deliberate decisions by patients to not follow prescribed treatments, often related to beliefs about the necessity of treatment, concerns about side effects, or competing priorities. Unintentional nonadherence occurs when patients fail to follow prescribed treatments despite willingness to do so, arising from causes such as forgetfulness, cognitive impairment, busy lifestyles, logistical barriers (cost, refilling prescriptions, geographical access), or simply being overwhelmed by complex medication regimens [8].

### Patient-Related Barriers

Patient-related barriers to adherence span a wide spectrum. Health literacy limitations can impair patients' understanding of their condition and the importance of treatment adherence. Age-related factors, including cognitive impairment in older patients, can affect the ability to manage complex medication regimens. Depression and anxiety, which are common among patients with CHD, can significantly impact both motivation for lifestyle modification and consistency with medication taking [1]. The patient's perception of the adherence process has been shown to be significantly related to therapeutic adherence outcomes [9].

### Therapy-Related Barriers

Complex medication regimens present significant challenges for sustained adherence. The pill burden associated with comprehensive secondary prevention, which may include multiple agents for lipid lowering, blood pressure control, antiplatelet therapy, and other indications, can overwhelm patients' ability to consistently take all medications as prescribed. Side effect concerns, whether real or perceived, can lead patients to discontinue or reduce medications without consulting their healthcare providers. The route of administration may affect both intentional and unintentional adherence, with injectable therapies presenting particular challenges for some patients [8].

### Healthcare System and Socioeconomic Barriers

System-level factors also contribute to nonadherence. Limited access to healthcare, particularly specialist care and cardiac rehabilitation services, can impede optimal secondary prevention delivery. Cost of medications remains a significant barrier for many patients, particularly those without adequate insurance coverage. Geographic barriers can limit access to CR programs, especially in rural areas. The quality of patient-provider communication has been identified as a critical determinant of adherence, with meta-analyses demonstrating that physician communication significantly impacts patient adherence to treatment [8].

## Strategies for Optimizing the Balance

### Integrated Care Approaches

Given the multifactorial nature of nonadherence, effective strategies to improve secondary prevention outcomes must be multifaceted and integrated. A comprehensive approach should include key interventions such as early follow-up visits, high medication adherence at 90 days, patient engagement and education, and development of novel interventions that support the broad categories influencing patient adherence [7]. The integration of multiple intervention strategies appears necessary to address the diverse barriers patients face in maintaining adherence to both pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modification.

Successful strategies for improving medication adherence among patients diagnosed with CHD have been identified through systematic review of the literature. Consistent intervention strategies include facilitating patient-provider communication, using mobile health (mHealth) technologies with emphasis on two-way communication, providing patient education in tandem with lifestyle and behavioral counseling, and providing psychosocial support [10]. These strategies address different barriers to adherence and, when combined, can produce synergistic effects on adherence behavior.

### The Role of Healthcare Professionals

Different healthcare professionals can contribute distinct expertise to the secondary prevention effort. Pharmacists have demonstrated effectiveness in providing in-depth management of medications including medication reconciliation and review, patient education for medication regimens, and lifestyle management with regular follow-up [10]. Nurses have proven effective at delivering ongoing monitoring and follow-up and developing rapport with patients that supports adherence [10]. Clinical providers engage in goal setting with patients to manage and reduce behavioral or physical risk factors [10].

The selection of interventionists should be guided by the specific strategies being employed, matching the interventionists' clinical scope of practice to the needs of the intervention. This multidisciplinary approach allows for comprehensive coverage of the multiple domains requiring attention in effective secondary prevention.

### Technology-Enabled Interventions

Mobile health technologies offer promising opportunities for supporting both medication adherence and lifestyle modification. mHealth interventions can provide medication reminders, facilitate communication between patients and providers, deliver educational content, and support behavioral change through self-monitoring and feedback. The emphasis on two-way communication in effective mHealth interventions is particularly important, as单向 messaging alone may be insufficient to support sustained behavior change.

Digital health innovations can facilitate self-management by supporting lifestyle behavior change and medication adherence [11]. The scalability of technology-enabled interventions makes them particularly attractive for reaching large populations and addressing barriers related to access to traditional healthcare services. However, the digital divide may limit the reach of these interventions among populations with limited access to technology or lower digital literacy.

### Fixed-Dose Combination Therapy

Fixed-dose combination (FDC) therapy, often referred to as the "polypill" approach, represents a pharmacological strategy for improving medication adherence. By combining multiple secondary prevention medications into a single pill, the polypill approach reduces pill burden and simplifies medication regimens. Evidence suggests that FDC therapy can improve medication adherence and clinical outcomes, with demonstrated success in hypertension treatment leading to the listing of multiple anti-hypertensive combinations on the World Health Organization Essential Medicines List [11]. The adoption of polypill strategies for secondary CVD prevention could be particularly impactful, though meaningful health impact would require simultaneous investments in primary care infrastructure to increase uptake [11].

### Health Literacy and Patient Education

Addressing health literacy represents an important but often overlooked component of improving adherence. Effective interventions have focused on building social support for health and using the "teach-back" method to provide patient education [11]. The teach-back method, in which patients are asked to explain in their own words what they have been told about their condition and treatment, helps ensure comprehension and can identify gaps in understanding that need to be addressed.

Patient education should extend beyond simple information provision to include lifestyle and behavioral management counseling [10]. This integrated approach to education recognizes that knowledge alone is insufficient to produce behavior change; patients also need practical strategies for implementing lifestyle modifications and managing the challenges they encounter in their daily lives.

## Balancing Medication Titration with Lifestyle Adherence

### The Complementary Relationship

The relationship between medication optimization and lifestyle modification in secondary prevention is fundamentally complementary rather than competitive. Pharmacotherapy provides reliable, dose-dependent reduction in cardiovascular risk factors, while lifestyle modification addresses the underlying behavioral contributors to cardiovascular risk and provides additional benefits beyond what can be achieved through medication alone. The optimal approach to secondary prevention integrates both elements, recognizing that each contributes uniquely to risk reduction.

The concept of residual risk is important in understanding this balance. Even patients receiving optimal pharmacotherapy may continue to experience elevated cardiovascular risk due to factors not fully addressed by medication, including lifestyle factors and variability in risk factors over time [2]. Lifestyle modification can help address this residual risk, while pharmacotherapy provides consistent risk factor control that may be less susceptible to the variability that can limit the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions alone.

### Individualized Target Setting

The process of balancing medication adjustments for LDL targets with lifestyle modification adherence should be individualized to each patient's circumstances, preferences, and risk profile. LDL target setting has become increasingly sophisticated, with contemporary guidelines recommending more aggressive targets for patients at higher absolute cardiovascular risk. The decision regarding how aggressively to titrate medications versus how much to emphasize lifestyle modification should consider patient preferences, ability to implement lifestyle changes, medication tolerance, and the presence of comorbidities.

For some patients, particularly those with strong motivation and ability to implement comprehensive lifestyle changes, a greater emphasis on lifestyle modification may be appropriate, with medication serving as a foundation on which lifestyle benefits can be built. For other patients, particularly those with limited ability or motivation for lifestyle change, more aggressive pharmacotherapy may be necessary to achieve adequate risk reduction. Most patients will benefit from a balanced approach that emphasizes both elements while recognizing the practical constraints patients face in implementing lifestyle changes.

### Monitoring and Feedback

Effective balancing of medication and lifestyle interventions requires ongoing monitoring and feedback. Regular assessment of both medication adherence and lifestyle behaviors allows clinicians to identify areas where patients are struggling and provide targeted support. The use of clinical dashboards and performance feedback can help identify patients who are not receiving optimal preventive care based on defined algorithms, enabling proactive outreach to address gaps in care [12].

Biomarker monitoring, including regular lipid panels, provides objective feedback on the effectiveness of both pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modification in achieving risk factor targets. When LDL targets are not achieved, clinicians can systematically evaluate whether the issue relates to medication underdosing, poor medication adherence, inadequate lifestyle modification, or some combination of factors, and adjust the management plan accordingly.

## Implementation Frameworks and Checklists

### Comprehensive Care Checklists

To address the complexity of secondary prevention and reduce gaps in care, frameworks have been proposed for comprehensive guideline-directed management. A proposed framework includes a 13-point checklist of pharmacological and nonpharmacological management based on clinical evidence and published guidelines [12]. This comprehensive tool can be used in outpatient settings by primary care providers and cardiologists who provide direct medical care for patients with established ASCVD.

The checklist approach addresses the challenge of therapeutic inertia, whereby clinicians may fail to intensify therapy when patients are not at goal. By providing a systematic reminder of all the elements of comprehensive secondary prevention, checklists can help ensure that no important component of care is overlooked. The supporting tools accompanying such checklists can facilitate timely feedback, identify gaps in care, and efficiently streamline prescription of effective therapies [12].

### Addressing Implementation Gaps

Existing gaps in the management of patients with established ASCVD are related to barriers to guideline implementation [12]. These barriers include time constraints during clinical encounters, complexity of guideline recommendations, inadequate reimbursement for preventive care services, and fragmented care delivery across multiple providers and settings. Addressing these system-level barriers is essential for optimizing the balance between medication and lifestyle interventions in routine clinical practice.

The integration of pharmacists into care delivery can help facilitate appropriate prescriptions with appropriate physician supervision [12]. Pharmacists can provide medication management services, patient education, and follow-up monitoring that complement the care provided by physicians, helping to ensure that patients receive comprehensive secondary prevention services even when physician time is limited.

## Future Directions and Emerging Opportunities

### Precision Approaches to Secondary Prevention

The future of secondary prevention lies in more personalized approaches that account for individual patient characteristics, preferences, and risk profiles. Advances in biomarkers, imaging, and genetic testing may allow for more precise risk stratification and more targeted therapeutic approaches. The identification of patients who are most likely to benefit from intensive lifestyle intervention versus those who may require more aggressive pharmacotherapy could help optimize the balance between these approaches.

### Novel Therapeutic Agents

The development of novel therapeutic agents, including PCSK9 inhibitors, bempedoic acid, and inclisiran, provides additional pharmacological options for patients who cannot achieve LDL targets with statin therapy alone or who cannot tolerate statins. These agents offer the potential for more aggressive LDL lowering in high-risk patients, potentially reducing the reliance on lifestyle modification alone in patients who struggle to implement behavioral changes. However, the high cost of these newer agents and the need for injection administration present challenges for widespread adoption.

### Digital Health Integration

The continued evolution of digital health technologies offers opportunities for more seamless integration of medication and lifestyle support. Wearable devices can monitor physical activity and sleep quality, while smartphone applications can provide medication reminders, dietary tracking, and educational content. The integration of these technologies with electronic health records could enable more comprehensive monitoring of both medication adherence and lifestyle behaviors, allowing clinicians to provide more timely and targeted support.

### Health System Innovations

Health system-level innovations, including value-based payment models that reward prevention and population health management, could create incentives for more comprehensive secondary prevention programs. The barbershop-based pharmacist-led intervention model for blood pressure control represents an innovative approach to delivering preventive care in community settings [11]. Similar models could be developed for secondary prevention of CHD, potentially improving access to care for underserved populations.

## Conclusion

The management of coronary heart disease secondary prevention requires careful balancing of medication adjustments for LDL targets with lifestyle modification adherence to minimize recurrence risk. Contemporary evidence supports the complementary roles of guideline-directed pharmacotherapy, particularly lipid-lowering therapy, and comprehensive lifestyle modification including smoking cessation, heart-healthy nutrition, regular physical activity, and psychological support. Cardiac rehabilitation programs provide an ideal platform for integrating these approaches, though access to such programs remains limited for many patients.

Significant barriers to adherence exist across both the pharmacological and lifestyle domains, necessitating multifaceted intervention strategies that address patient-related, therapy-related, healthcare system-related, and socioeconomic factors. Successful strategies include facilitating patient-provider communication, leveraging mobile health technologies, providing integrated patient education and behavioral counseling, and ensuring psychosocial support. The involvement of multidisciplinary care teams, including pharmacists, nurses, and physicians working in coordinated fashion, can help address the diverse needs of patients engaged in secondary prevention.

The future of secondary prevention will likely involve more personalized approaches that account for individual patient characteristics, preferences, and risk profiles, supported by digital health technologies and innovative care delivery models. By optimizing the balance between medication optimization and lifestyle modification, clinicians can more effectively reduce the substantial burden of recurrent cardiovascular events in patients with established coronary heart disease.
