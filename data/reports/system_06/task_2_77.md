# In What Way Does the Value Translation Mechanism Adjust the Tension Between Local Encoding and International Aesthetics?

## Abstract

This research report examines the complex mechanisms through which value translation operates to reconcile the inherent tensions between locally encoded cultural meanings and internationally recognized aesthetic frameworks. Drawing upon interdisciplinary scholarship spanning cultural studies, translation theory, indigenous aesthetics, and cross-cultural design, this analysis reveals that value translation functions not as a neutral conduit of meaning transfer but as an active site of negotiation, reconstruction, and cultural mediation. The investigation demonstrates that effective value translation mechanisms operate through multiple simultaneous strategies: the preservation of cultural specificity while enabling cross-cultural intelligibility, the recognition of fundamental ontological differences between Western and non-Western aesthetic paradigms, and the development of adaptive frameworks that maintain brand or cultural core values while allowing for localized expression. The findings suggest that successful value translation requires what scholars have termed "adding context to context"—a methodology that acknowledges linguistic, conceptual, and material plurality rather than imposing homogenizing frameworks. This report contributes to understanding how organizations, designers, and cultural practitioners navigate the persistent tension between globalization imperatives and localization needs, ultimately proposing that the most effective approach involves treating translation as a creative act of cultural negotiation rather than mere linguistic conversion.

## 1. Introduction

The question of how value translation mechanisms adjust the tension between local encoding and international aesthetics represents one of the most consequential challenges facing contemporary cultural production, design practice, and cross-cultural communication. As globalization accelerates the circulation of cultural products across borders, the need to translate values, meanings, and aesthetic sensibilities from their original cultural contexts into forms that resonate with international audiences has become increasingly urgent. Yet this translation process is far from straightforward, as it must navigate the complex terrain where deeply embedded cultural meanings encounter divergent aesthetic frameworks, often rooted in fundamentally different philosophical and ontological assumptions.

The tension between local and global aesthetic paradigms manifests across multiple domains. In the realm of indigenous cultural expression, Western analytical methodologies have historically imposed philosophical frameworks that fundamentally misalign with indigenous ways of understanding the relationship between the physical and spiritual worlds [1]. In commercial design and branding, organizations must balance the consistency of global brand identity with the cultural resonance required for local market success [2]. In artistic practice, curators and critics working within international exhibition contexts must grapple with questions of how to preserve cultural difference even as translation inherently seeks to overcome it [3]. These diverse contexts reveal that the challenge of value translation is not merely technical but deeply philosophical, touching upon fundamental questions about the nature of meaning, the possibility of cross-cultural understanding, and the ethics of representation.

This research report synthesizes scholarship from multiple disciplines to develop a comprehensive understanding of how value translation mechanisms operate to adjust these tensions. The analysis proceeds through several interconnected dimensions: the philosophical foundations of aesthetic difference, the methodological approaches to cultural mediation, the practical strategies employed in cross-cultural design and communication, and the theoretical frameworks that illuminate the dynamics of translation as a creative and political act. By examining these dimensions in relation to one another, this report aims to provide both theoretical insight and practical guidance for those engaged in the work of cultural translation across aesthetic domains.

## 2. Philosophical Foundations of Aesthetic Difference

### 2.1 The Western Philosophical Binary and Its Limitations

Understanding the tension between local encoding and international aesthetics requires first examining the philosophical foundations that shape how Western analytical frameworks have historically approached non-Western cultural materials. The separation of spirit from the physical, a philosophical concept posited by Augustine of Hippo in *City of God*, has profoundly influenced Western understanding of the relationship between heaven and earth, ultimately manifesting in the institutional separation of church and state [1]. This philosophical binary was further advanced during the Protestant Reformation through the work of Martin Luther and incorporated into Enlightenment thinking through John Locke's foundational contributions to democratic theory [1]. The separation of physical and spiritual thus became central to Western intellectual traditions, shaping not only theological and political discourse but also the frameworks through which Western scholars have analyzed and evaluated aesthetic expressions from other cultures.

The implications of this philosophical inheritance for cross-cultural aesthetic analysis are significant. When Western methodologies of analysis are applied to indigenous cultures or their expressive cultural materials, this binary imposes what might be termed a "philosophical blinder"—a systematic limitation that prevents full appreciation of aesthetic systems organized around fundamentally different ontological assumptions [1]. For many indigenous cultures, the separation of physical and spiritual that structures Western thought does not hold in the same way; rather, aesthetic practice may be understood as inherently spiritual, with the creation of beautiful objects using traditional materials and the adornment of these objects with culturally specific symbols constituting acts of cultural sovereignty and spiritual practice [1]. The claiming of identity, language, designs, and related stories and philosophies through aesthetic practice represents, for these communities, the heart of their capacity to remain culturally distinct peoples [1].

### 2.2 Indigenous Aesthetic Paradigms and Their Distinctiveness

The distinctiveness of indigenous aesthetic paradigms challenges the universal applicability of Western aesthetic categories and evaluation frameworks. For Norwegian Sámi theorist Harald Gaski, aesthetics as an indigenous methodology affords opportunities to apply a holistic approach to indigenous research and activate the full potential of indigenous research paradigms [4]. This holistic approach stands in contrast to the analytical fragmentation that often characterizes Western academic disciplines, where aesthetic experience is separated from spiritual practice, political organization, and social life. Gaski's concept of "adding context to context," borrowed from Arnold Krupat, signals linguistic, conceptual, and material plurality as it regards indigenous aesthetics—where several concepts in the Sámi dialect are linked to notions and practices pertaining to what is regarded as aesthetic [4]. This plurality of aesthetic association accounts for the Western institutionalization of Sámi material practices that has bilaterally categorized Sámi ideas and values embedded in Sámi aesthetic practices in ways that remain invisible to the colonial gaze [4].

The Inuit concept of Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, also known as "IQ," provides another example of how indigenous paradigms offer alternative frameworks for understanding aesthetic production and curation [4]. Operating as an ethical paradigm and value system, IQ approaches art and aesthetic production through relational frameworks that differ fundamentally from Western aesthetic theories focused on individual genius, formal innovation, or autonomous aesthetic value. A non-indigenous approach to indigenous aesthetic materials, by contrast, often subverts the colonialist museumification of objects that are typically exhibited divorced from their social life and context [4]. The objects are thus stripped of the relational meanings and cultural practices that constitute their aesthetic significance within indigenous frameworks.

### 2.3 The Problem of Ontological Incommensurability

The philosophical differences between Western and non-Western aesthetic paradigms raise the question of whether translation between such fundamentally different systems is even possible. This is not merely a question of linguistic translation, though that itself presents significant challenges, but of ontological translation—the attempt to convey ways of being and understanding that may not have equivalents across cultural boundaries. The modernist ethno-aesthetics that institutionalized Arctic Indigenous visual art according to Western tastes, aesthetics, and values has resulted in what scholars describe as a profoundly entangled, limited, and disruptive obfuscation of Arctic Indigenous historical experiences, intangible cultural practices, and aesthetic expressions [4]. This inheritance continues to shape contemporary moments related to Arctic geopolitics, demonstrating how historical translation practices continue to exert influence long after their initial occurrence.

The challenge of translation between ontologically distinct systems has been addressed by various scholars working at the intersection of cultural studies, translation theory, and postcolonial critique. For Judith Butler, directly mobilizing Homi Bhabha's notion of cultural translation, cultural articulations can convey universal claims while simultaneously being subject to particular cultural constraints [5]. The translational process, in order to overcome these constraints, is marked by what Butler calls a "performative contradiction," bringing into play the "excluded outside" of established articulations [5]. This formulation suggests that translation always involves a certain violence—the imposition of frameworks that may not fully capture the meaning being translated—while also opening possibilities for new forms of understanding to emerge through the encounter between different cultural logics.

## 3. Theoretical Frameworks for Understanding Value Translation

### 3.1 Translation as Cultural Negotiation

Contemporary translation theory has moved decisively away from understanding translation as a neutral or transparent process of transferring meaning from one language or cultural context to another. Instead, translation is increasingly understood as an active site of cultural negotiation, where meanings are not simply reproduced but reconstructed, adapted, and sometimes transformed beyond recognition. In the context of cross-cultural research, translations are used to express meaning in other languages through the construction of multilingual versions of existing or new surveys and questionnaires [6]. However, although translations may invoke thoughts of simply moving words between source and target languages, translations are more complex than rote word replacement [6]. High-quality translations must keep the content of the original while adapting it to the cultural and linguistic expectations of the target audience, a process that requires deep cultural knowledge and sophisticated judgment.

The Cultural Turn in translation theory, associated with scholars like Bassnett and Lefevere, has emphasized that culture cannot be refined and analyzed based only on small linguistic units, nor can translation be driven only by the micro-level of morphemes, vocabulary, sentences, and stylistic characteristics [7]. Rather, the best approach to translation involves reconstructing traditional strategies and finding relatively scientific frameworks that account for the cultural dimensions of meaning [7]. This cultural turn has profound implications for understanding how value translation operates to adjust tensions between local encoding and international aesthetics, as it directs attention away from purely linguistic concerns toward the broader cultural, social, and political contexts within which translation takes place.

### 3.2 The Tension Between Globalization and Localization

The tension between globalization and localization remains a critical challenge in cross-cultural design and communication. Research findings clearly demonstrate that brand consistency and cultural adaptation are not irreconcilable dichotomies but rather constitute what has been termed a "tension negotiation" mechanism [2]. This mechanism enables organizations to realize what might be described as the communication fusion of "global skeleton + local skin"—maintaining core brand values and standards while allowing for cultural differentiation in visual style, language, and experiential elements [2]. For example, in the case of IKEA, BILLY bookcases maintain uniform modular interfaces and environmentally friendly materials across different national markets, yet the visual presentation of the Chinese version pays more attention to spatial adaptation and parent-child collaboration, while the Swedish version focuses on individual scalability and DIY freedom [2]. This difference does not undermine the global brand core but rather enhances local resonance while maintaining essential brand identity.

The tension between globalization and localization extends beyond commercial contexts to encompass broader questions about cultural production and exchange in an interconnected world. As Dilip Gaonkar and Elizabeth Povinelli have argued, it is no longer viable to look at circulation as a singular or empty space in which things move [5]. Rather, a form can be said to move intelligibly from one cultural space to another only in a state of translation [5]. This formulation emphasizes that the movement of cultural forms across boundaries is always mediated by translation processes that shape, transform, and sometimes distort the meanings being conveyed. Focus thus shifts to the effects of mediation, to the ways practices of communication shape the knowledge that we receive, to the ways in which it is developed, organized, and passed on [5].

### 3.3 Preserving Difference in Translation

A particularly important theoretical question concerns whether and how translation can preserve cultural difference rather than homogenizing it in the service of global circulation. Sarat Maharaj has viewed international exhibition culture as a communicative space where language plays a key role in the formation of cultural and social relations [3]. Yet this communicative space simultaneously masks a tendency toward global cultural homogenization, as Nicolas Bourriaud has suggested [3]. The question thus becomes: what would it mean to preserve difference in translation, even though the very activity of translating is, paradoxically, concerned with overcoming it? What could be a translation methodology that can counteract the centripetal forces of the global centers [3]?

These questions are not merely theoretical but have profound practical implications for how cultural institutions, museums, galleries, and other sites of cultural exchange operate. If visual art is subject to the forces of globalization as much as it actively produces and advances globalization, then the figure of the translator—whether artist, curator, critic, or scholar—becomes crucial in determining whether translation will serve to homogenize cultural difference or to create new forms of cross-cultural understanding that respect and preserve distinctiveness [3]. The challenge is to develop translation methodologies that enable communication across cultural boundaries without erasing the differences that make such communication necessary and valuable in the first place.

## 4. Mechanisms of Value Translation in Practice

### 4.1 Cultural Mediation and Cross-Cultural Design

The practical mechanisms through which value translation operates to adjust tensions between local encoding and international aesthetics can be observed across multiple domains of cultural production and exchange. In the field of cultural mediation, standards at national, international, and global levels would support the creation of effective training units and materials that combine universal principles with culturally specific elements, helping to standardize practices in cultural mediation training [8]. This combination of universal and particular elements reflects the fundamental challenge of value translation: how to establish frameworks and practices that can be consistently applied while remaining flexible enough to accommodate cultural specificity.

In product design and development, the integration of culture and aesthetics relies on various conceptual frameworks that guide the application of cultural elements while maintaining aesthetic appeal [9]. Different design models emphasize distinct aspects of this integration. The Symbolic Design Model embeds cultural symbols directly into product forms and motifs, whereas the Functional-Aesthetic Model prioritizes usability while ensuring visual harmony [9]. Experience-centered approaches focus on user interaction and emotional engagement, integrating cultural cues in the context of user experience [9]. Cross-cultural hybrid methods combine local cultural characteristics with universal aesthetic principles, and narrative-based design uses storytelling to link cultural identity with visual design [9]. These diverse approaches demonstrate the range of strategies available for negotiating the tension between local and global aesthetic considerations.

### 4.2 Technological Mediation and Adaptive Design

Emerging technologies offer new possibilities for mediating the translation of values across cultural and aesthetic boundaries. Advanced design tools enable designers to simulate, visualize, and iterate complex forms, textures, and patterns that align with both functional and cultural requirements [9]. Augmented reality and virtual reality platforms allow users to experience culturally adapted designs in virtual environments before production, providing real-time feedback on aesthetic perception and usability [9]. Artificial intelligence design tools can generate multiple design variations quickly, incorporating cultural motifs, color schemes, and stylistic preferences tailored to diverse user groups [9]. These technologies accelerate the design process, reduce trial-and-error costs, and facilitate a deeper understanding of how cultural elements interact with aesthetic principles [9].

The role of technology in value translation extends beyond mere efficiency gains to encompass fundamental changes in how translation is conceptualized and practiced. Speculative storytelling provides a design logic for rebuilding translation practices, offering a structured way to preserve the experiential dimensions of meaning that traditional localization practices often overlook [10]. This reframes translation within human-computer interaction and user experience design, positioning interface design as an active site of meaning negotiation [10]. The key insight is that meaning is not alone shaped by translation but by design itself [10], suggesting that the mechanisms of value translation must be integrated into the broader processes through which cultural products are conceived, developed, and delivered to audiences.

### 4.3 Organizational Structures for Cultural Adaptation

The mechanisms of value translation operate not only at the level of individual designers or translators but also through organizational structures and collaborative processes. Research on global brands has found that cultural adaptation is supported by organizational structures and cross-cultural collaboration mechanisms [2]. For example, the "Agile Team" mechanism enables fortnightly iterative design through mixed teams of employees from different cultural backgrounds, operationalizing and streamlining cultural adaptation [2]. This organizational approach allows for continuous refinement of cultural adaptation strategies based on feedback and emerging insights, rather than treating cultural adaptation as a one-time translation task completed at the outset of a project.

The organizational dimension of value translation is particularly important because it addresses the systemic and institutional factors that shape how cultural products move across boundaries. As one research participant noted, cultural adaptation is not in conflict with brand consistency, and the key lies in "basing the adjustments at the expression level on uniform standards" [2]. This means that the underlying standards of safety, environmental protection, structure, and materials are not changed, while the visual, linguistic, and experiential elements are adapted to local contexts [2]. The distinction between core values and expressive elements provides a framework for understanding how organizations can maintain consistency while enabling meaningful cultural adaptation.

## 5. Strategies for Navigating the Local-Global Tension

### 5.1 The Global Localization Approach

The tension between globalization and localization has given rise to what scholars and practitioners have termed the Global Localization (GL) approach—a strategic framework that seeks to transcend the apparent opposition between these two imperatives [2]. Localization emphasizes that in the communication of different countries, adjustments should be made according to local culture, language, values, and aesthetic preferences [2]. For example, in the Chinese market, advertisements often adopt a more indirect, emotional style of expression, focusing on cultural connotations such as family, community, and harmony, in contrast to Western advertisements that emphasize individuality, ego, and a direct style of communication [2]. From the contradiction between these two strategies, a more operational theoretical path has been extended—one that recognizes the value of both global consistency and local resonance while providing practical guidance for achieving both.

The Global Localization approach does not mean abandoning the global brand core or cultural identity; rather, it involves developing strategies for expressing core values in ways that resonate with diverse cultural contexts [2]. This requires deep understanding of both the universal elements that give a brand or cultural product its distinctive identity and the particular elements that shape meaning and reception in specific cultural contexts. The challenge is to identify which elements can be standardized without sacrificing cultural relevance and which elements must be adapted to local contexts to achieve effective communication and resonance.

### 5.2 Re-Semanticization and Creative Reconstruction

Beyond mere adaptation or localization, some scholars and practitioners have advocated for approaches to value translation that involve creative reconstruction rather than simple transfer. In the context of graphic design and cultural symbol systems, the fundamental task of design should be translation and reconstruction [11]. In a visual environment with multiple cultures, how to present the symbolic language in traditional Chinese culture in a contemporary expression form is an important proposition that graphic design should consider [11]. This not only involves the renewal of aesthetic language but also concerns the embodiment of cultural continuity and innovation ability [11].

The re-semanticization strategy involves taking cultural symbols and reinterpreting them in ways that preserve their cultural significance while making them accessible and meaningful to contemporary and international audiences. For example, traditional Chinese window lattice patterns can be transformed into modern background graphic systems, retaining cultural texture without destroying the overall visual simplicity [11]. Characters such as "Fu," "Shou," and "Xi" can be used as the basis for font design that draws upon traditional forms while creating new aesthetic possibilities [11]. This approach recognizes that cultural symbols are not static but dynamic, capable of being reinterpreted and recontextualized while maintaining their connection to cultural traditions and meanings.

### 5.3 Managing Semantic Loss and Emotional Resonance

One of the most significant challenges in value translation is the inevitable loss of meaning that occurs when cultural expressions are transferred across boundaries. In translation between languages that belong to totally different cultures, such as Arabic and English, the challenges of translation do not stem from universal notions but from culture-specific terms [12]. Cultural terms are phrases and words conditioned by cultural variety that usually exist in translation [12]. These terms represent concepts that cannot be translated without comprehensive knowledge of the cultures between which the meaning transfer takes place [12]. This issue can only be addressed through knowledge of the general cultural context of these concepts and through subject matter expansion [12].

The challenge of semantic loss is particularly acute in the translation of literary and artistic works, where aesthetic function aspects must be comprehended in addition to literal meaning [12]. Translation is regarded as the process of conveying a message from source text to target text, but it must also account for the differences in culture between the source and target language, especially when translating cultural-specific terms [12]. The study recommends that translators should employ many translation strategies simultaneously and have sufficient knowledge in both the source and target languages to convey the meanings of cultural terms appropriately [12]. This multi-strategy approach recognizes that no single translation technique can adequately address the complexity of cross-cultural meaning transfer.

## 6. Case Studies and Applications

### 6.1 Indigenous Art and Decolonizing Aesthetic Theory

The application of value translation mechanisms to indigenous art and aesthetic practice provides important insights into both the possibilities and limitations of cross-cultural aesthetic exchange. Contemporary art created by indigenous artists may be conceived in a simultaneous movement of un-rootedness and confirmation of tradition [4]. This is evident in the work of Sámi artists Sunna and Nilsson Skum, whose artistic practice navigates between engagement with international art worlds and deep connection to Sámi cultural traditions and values [4]. The challenge for these artists is to translate their cultural values and aesthetic sensibilities into forms that can be understood and appreciated by international audiences without losing the distinctiveness that gives their work its meaning and power.

The broader project of decolonizing aesthetic theory, concepts, and contexts involves not simply adding indigenous perspectives to existing frameworks but fundamentally rethinking the categories and assumptions through which aesthetic value has been defined and evaluated [4]. For indigenous communities, the willful learning of customary cultural practices, making beautiful objects using traditional materials and adorning them with the symbols of each distinct cultural group, remains an important act of cultural sovereignty [1]. The claiming of identity, language, designs, and related stories and philosophies is at the heart of the capacity to remain culturally distinct peoples [1]. Value translation in this context must serve not merely to make indigenous aesthetic expressions accessible to international audiences but to challenge and transform the frameworks through which aesthetic value is recognized and evaluated.

### 6.2 Cross-Cultural Wedding Visual Culture

Contemporary wedding visual culture provides another illuminating case study for understanding how value translation operates in practice. Research on graphic design in contemporary weddings has revealed how images reflect the influence and reshaping of Western culture on traditional Chinese rituals [11]. The study found that graphic design not only records cultural changes but participates in the process of cultural negotiation, becoming an important means of modern identity construction, value translation, and emotional identification [11]. The juxtaposition of Chinese and Western visual symbols in contemporary weddings—such as the combination of Western wedding marches with Chinese firecrackers and the integration of Chinese tea ceremonies with Western vows—constitutes an intertwined network of cultural meanings and visual languages [11].

This visual scene of juxtaposition of Chinese and Western cultures not only reflects the openness and inclusiveness of culture in the context of globalization but also reveals deep cultural tensions and structural tensions [11]. On one hand, it responds to the needs of the new generation of individuals to seek identity and multiple self-expression in globalization [11]. On the other hand, it also exposes the imbalance in cultural mixing, that is, the hollowing out of visual symbols and the absence of local cultural context [11]. The case of wedding visual culture demonstrates both the creative possibilities of value translation and its potential pitfalls, particularly when translation serves to reinforce existing power imbalances rather than enabling genuine cross-cultural dialogue.

### 6.3 International Exhibition Culture and Curatorial Practice

The context of international exhibition culture provides yet another lens through which to examine the mechanisms of value translation. Curators, art critics, and scholars working in international visual art contexts face particular challenges in translating cultural meanings across boundaries [3]. It is difficult, for example, for a non-Lebanese and non-Arabic-speaking individual attending a presentation of The Atlas Group by Walid Raad to fully grasp the work's relation to Lebanese history precisely because they are likely outsiders to the very collective historical experience and knowledge that Raad's work speaks from [3]. This epistemological gap may be precisely what permits the artist as translator to take creative license and build a new discourse through which these collective experiences can become relatable to outsiders [3].

The role of the artist as translator is particularly significant in international exhibition contexts, where the movement of artworks across cultural boundaries involves complex processes of interpretation, contextualization, and recontextualization. Writers and curators like Sarat Maharaj, Rey Chow, Hito Steyerl, Suzanne Cotter, Boris Buden, Nicolas Bourriaud, Marcus Verhagen, Stefan Nowotny, and others have previously used the concept of translation in various ways to designate processes of transcultural exchange, circulation, and transformation in art contexts [3]. Moreover, some of these writers have explored the political and ethical implications of translation acts [3]. The figure of the translator—whether artist, curator, or scholar—becomes crucial in determining how value translation will adjust the tension between local encoding and international aesthetics.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### 7.1 Theoretical Implications

The analysis presented in this report has significant implications for theoretical understanding of value translation and its role in adjusting tensions between local encoding and international aesthetics. First, the findings support a reconceptualization of translation as a creative act of cultural negotiation rather than a technical process of linguistic or visual conversion. This reconceptualization has important consequences for how translation is taught, practiced, and evaluated across professional and academic contexts. Second, the analysis highlights the importance of recognizing and accounting for ontological differences between cultural systems rather than assuming that meanings can be straightforwardly transferred across boundaries. Third, the findings suggest that effective value translation requires what might be termed "translation competence"—the capacity to navigate between cultural systems, recognize the limitations of one's own frameworks, and develop creative strategies for enabling cross-cultural understanding.

The theoretical frameworks examined in this report—from Butler's concept of performative contradiction to Gaski's notion of "adding context to context"—provide conceptual resources for understanding the complex dynamics of value translation. These frameworks share a recognition that translation is never neutral or transparent but always involves interpretation, selection, and transformation. The challenge for future theoretical development is to move beyond mere critique of translation's limitations toward the development of more robust frameworks for guiding translation practice in ways that respect cultural difference while enabling meaningful cross-cultural exchange.

### 7.2 Practical Implications

The practical implications of this analysis extend across multiple professional domains. For designers working on cross-cultural projects, the findings suggest the importance of developing cultural knowledge and sensitivity that goes beyond surface-level awareness of cultural symbols and conventions. Effective cultural adaptation requires understanding the deeper values and assumptions that shape how aesthetic meanings are constructed and interpreted in different cultural contexts. For organizations operating across national and cultural boundaries, the findings support the development of organizational structures and processes that enable meaningful cultural adaptation while maintaining core brand or institutional values. The "Agile Team" approach and similar collaborative mechanisms provide models for how this can be achieved in practice.

For cultural institutions such as museums, galleries, and cultural centers, the findings have implications for how exhibitions and programs are conceived and implemented. The challenge is to create spaces where cultural difference can be preserved and respected even as translation enables communication across boundaries. This requires attention to curatorial practices, interpretive frameworks, and the physical and social contexts within which cultural products are presented to audiences. For translators and interpreters working across cultural and linguistic boundaries, the findings underscore the importance of recognizing the creative and political dimensions of translation practice and developing strategies that address the inherent tensions between fidelity to source meaning and accessibility to target audiences.

### 7.3 Future Research Directions

This analysis suggests several directions for future research on value translation mechanisms and their role in adjusting tensions between local encoding and international aesthetics. First, there is a need for more empirical research examining how value translation actually operates in specific contexts and domains. While this report has drawn upon scholarship from multiple disciplines, much of the existing research remains theoretical or case-study based. More systematic investigation of translation practices across different domains could provide a more robust empirical foundation for theoretical claims about how translation works.

Second, future research should examine the perspectives of those who receive translated cultural products, not only those who produce them. How do audiences from different cultural backgrounds interpret and respond to culturally adapted products? What factors shape whether adaptation is perceived as respectful engagement or cultural appropriation? Understanding reception processes is essential for developing translation strategies that achieve their intended effects.

Third, there is a need for research on the ethical dimensions of value translation. Questions about who has the authority to translate cultural meanings, what obligations translators have to source cultures, and how power relations shape translation practices deserve more sustained attention. These questions are particularly urgent in contexts where translation involves indigenous cultures or other communities that have historically been subject to colonial extraction and misrepresentation.

## 8. Conclusion

The value translation mechanism through which tensions between local encoding and international aesthetics are adjusted operates as a complex, dynamic process of cultural negotiation rather than a simple technical procedure. This mechanism must navigate fundamental philosophical differences between Western and non-Western aesthetic paradigms, organizational pressures toward global consistency, and the inherent limitations of cross-cultural meaning transfer. The analysis presented in this report has demonstrated that effective value translation requires recognition of cultural specificity, creative approaches to reconstruction and re-semanticization, and organizational structures that enable meaningful cultural adaptation while maintaining core values.

The key insight emerging from this investigation is that value translation is fundamentally a creative act that involves not merely reproducing existing meanings but constructing new forms of understanding through the encounter between different cultural logics. This creative dimension of translation is evident across the diverse domains examined—from indigenous aesthetic practice to international exhibition culture, from commercial branding to cross-cultural design. In each case, the challenge is not to eliminate difference but to create conditions under which difference can be communicated, understood, and respected across cultural boundaries.

The mechanisms through which this creative translation occurs include both formal frameworks and informal practices, both technological tools and human judgment, both organizational structures and individual initiative. The most effective approaches to value translation appear to combine multiple strategies—employing universal principles while accommodating culturally specific elements, maintaining core values while allowing for localized expression, preserving semantic content while adapting to the expectations and frameworks of target audiences. This multi-strategy approach recognizes that no single technique or framework can adequately address the complexity of cross-cultural meaning transfer.

As globalization continues to accelerate the circulation of cultural products across boundaries, the importance of effective value translation will only increase. The frameworks and strategies examined in this report provide resources for navigating this complex terrain, but they also highlight how much remains to be understood about how translation works and how it might be done better. Future research and practice must continue to develop more sophisticated theoretical frameworks, more effective practical strategies, and more ethical approaches to the work of translating values across the boundaries that both separate and connect the world's diverse cultures.
