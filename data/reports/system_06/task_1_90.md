# How Does Public Leisure Space Usage Contribute to Community Identity During Commercialization?

## Abstract

This research report examines the complex relationship between public leisure space usage and community identity formation within the context of increasing commercialization. Drawing from interdisciplinary scholarship in urban planning, leisure studies, and community psychology, this analysis investigates how public spaces serve as crucibles for collective identity while simultaneously facing pressures from market-driven development models. The report synthesizes evidence demonstrating that public leisure spaces function as essential sites for social interaction, place attachment, and community cohesion, yet their commercialization through mechanisms such as Business Improvement Districts and public-private partnerships introduces both opportunities and challenges for maintaining authentic community identity. Key findings indicate that successful integration of commercial elements requires careful attention to inclusivity, resident agency, and the preservation of spaces that allow for spontaneous social encounters. The report concludes with policy recommendations for balancing commercial sustainability with the fundamental social functions that underpin community identity formation in urban environments.

---

## 1. Introduction

The relationship between built environments and human social behavior has long occupied a central position in urban studies, environmental psychology, and community development research. Public leisure spaces—encompassing parks, plazas, community centers, and other shared environments—represent particularly significant sites where this relationship unfolds. These spaces serve not merely as physical locations for recreational activities but as fundamental infrastructure for the reproduction of social bonds, the cultivation of collective memory, and the formation of community identity. As cities worldwide experience intensifying commercial pressures, understanding how public leisure spaces contribute to community identity becomes both academically compelling and practically urgent.

Contemporary urban landscapes are characterized by an accelerating intersection of public and private interests in the management and development of leisure spaces. Business Improvement Districts, public-private partnerships, and various value-capture mechanisms have proliferated as municipalities seek innovative funding solutions for parks and public amenities [1]. These arrangements reflect broader neoliberal trends in urban governance, wherein market-based solutions are applied to spaces traditionally understood as public goods. The implications of this transformation for community identity formation remain contested, with some scholars arguing that commercial involvement enhances space quality and sustainability while others caution that market logics may undermine the egalitarian and spontaneous qualities essential to genuine public life.

This report addresses the central question of how public leisure space usage contributes to community identity during periods of commercialization. The analysis proceeds through several interconnected dimensions. First, it examines the theoretical foundations linking leisure environments to community identity, with particular attention to concepts of place attachment, leisure satisfaction, and social cohesion. Second, it investigates the mechanisms through which commercialization reshapes public leisure spaces, including governance structures, funding mechanisms, and the introduction of commercial amenities. Third, it explores the tensions that emerge between commercial imperatives and community identity formation, considering issues of inclusivity, gentrification, and access. Finally, it synthesizes these analyses to offer insights for policy and practice that might reconcile commercial sustainability with the fundamental social functions of public leisure spaces.

The significance of this inquiry extends beyond academic interest. As cities grapple with fiscal constraints, environmental challenges, and social fragmentation, the fate of public leisure spaces carries profound implications for urban livability, democratic participation, and community well-being. Understanding how these spaces can maintain their identity-forming functions while adapting to commercial pressures represents one of the central challenges facing contemporary urban planning and community development.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: Community Identity and Place Attachment

### 2.1 Conceptualizing Community Identity

Community identity represents a multidimensional construct encompassing residents' recognition of community functions and the intensity of their emotional connection to their locality [2]. This identity manifests through a sense of belonging, pride, and responsibility that residents develop toward their geographic community. Unlike individual personality or personal identity, community identity operates at the collective level, emerging through shared experiences, common spaces, and collective memory. It reflects not merely cognitive awareness of community boundaries but affective investment in the community as a meaningful social entity.

The formation of community identity occurs through ongoing processes of social interaction, spatial practice, and symbolic interpretation. Residents come to identify with their community through repeated encounters with fellow residents, participation in local activities, and the accumulation of meaningful experiences within community spaces. These processes are fundamentally spatial in nature, occurring in and through the physical environments that communities inhabit. Public leisure spaces serve as particularly important sites for identity formation because they bring residents together in contexts that facilitate interaction, recreation, and the shared experience of community life.

Identity theory provides a useful framework for understanding these dynamics. This perspective explains individual social behavior through the interactions between individual cognition and social mechanisms, connecting personal characteristics with local social structures [2]. When residents engage in leisure activities within public spaces, they simultaneously perform their individual identities and participate in the reproduction of community identity. The spaces themselves become arenas where individual and collective identities are negotiated, contested, and consolidated.

### 2.2 Place Attachment and Environmental Psychology

Place attachment theory offers additional insight into the relationship between physical environments and community identity. This concept describes the emotional bonds that form between individuals and specific physical locations, bonds that develop through repeated interaction and the accumulation of meaningful experiences. Research has confirmed that place attachment plays a mediating role in the relationship between outdoor leisure environments and well-being [2]. When residents develop strong attachments to public leisure spaces, these attachments contribute to their broader sense of community belonging and identity.

The development of place attachment in public leisure spaces involves multiple dimensions. Physical attachment emerges through appreciation of environmental features—the shade of a particular tree, the view from a favorite bench, the sound of a fountain. Social attachment develops through encounters with familiar faces, friendships formed in the space, and memories of shared experiences with other community members. Functional attachment arises from the space's capacity to meet leisure needs, providing opportunities for desired activities and experiences. Together, these dimensions create a layered relationship between residents and their public leisure environments that contributes significantly to community identity.

The perception of environment and physical activity during leisure time also contributes to the formation of community identity [2]. When residents engage in physical activities within public leisure spaces—exercising, walking, playing—they develop embodied memories and routines that anchor their identity to place. These physical engagements create kinesthetic connections to space that complement cognitive and emotional attachments, grounding community identity in bodily experience as well as social interaction.

### 2.3 Leisure Satisfaction and Community Belonging

The relationship between leisure satisfaction and community identity has been the subject of substantial empirical research. Studies have demonstrated a positive relationship between leisure satisfaction and community identity, suggesting that residents who derive fulfillment from their leisure experiences tend to exhibit stronger identification with their communities [2]. This relationship operates through several mechanisms.

First, positive leisure experiences generate favorable associations with the locations where they occur. When residents enjoy satisfying experiences in public leisure spaces, they come to associate those spaces with pleasure and well-being. These positive associations contribute to place attachment and, by extension, community identity. Second, leisure satisfaction reflects the successful fulfillment of needs for recreation, social interaction, and personal enrichment. When public leisure spaces effectively meet these needs, residents develop gratitude and loyalty toward their communities, strengthening their sense of belonging.

The leisure environment holds particular significance for the development of community leisure activities. A positive leisure environment provides good and comfortable experiences for community residents, affording them a strong sense of responsibility as community owners [2]. This sense of ownership proves crucial for community identity formation. When residents feel that public spaces belong to them—that they have legitimate claims to use and care for these spaces—they develop the psychological investment that underlies community identity. Conversely, when public spaces feel alien, unwelcoming, or controlled by external forces, residents may experience diminished sense of ownership and weakened community identification.

## 3. Public Leisure Spaces as Sites of Community Identity Formation

### 3.1 Social Interaction and Chance Encounters

Public spaces such as parks and plazas foster sense of community by facilitating chance encounters between neighbors [3]. These encounters, though seemingly incidental, play a fundamental role in community identity formation. When residents repeatedly encounter familiar faces in public leisure spaces, they develop awareness of community membership that underlies collective identity. These encounters also provide opportunities for the exchange of information, the development of trust, and the formation of social ties that bind communities together.

The significance of chance encounters reflects the importance of proximity and exposure in social bond formation. Social psychological research demonstrates that repeated exposure to others tends to increase liking and familiarity, foundational elements of social cohesion. Public leisure spaces create conditions for this exposure by bringing diverse community members into shared physical proximity. Unlike commercial spaces designed for transaction or private spaces reserved for family, public leisure spaces allow for the kind of spontaneous, unstructured interaction that builds community bonds.

The quality of public space significantly influences the frequency and quality of these encounters. Research on urban design has identified features that facilitate social interaction, including seating arrangements that encourage lingering, food and beverage options that create gathering points, shelter from elements that extend usability, and programming that draws diverse users [3]. Spaces lacking these features may fail to generate the critical mass of users and interactions necessary for community identity formation. Conversely, well-designed public spaces become crucibles where community identity is forged through accumulated social experience.

### 3.2 Multi-Use Destinations and Civic Pride

Great urban parks function as safety valves for cities, providing relief for residents in dense urban environments [4]. A well-designed and maintained park can generate civic pride and help citizens feel connected to their cultural and political institutions. This connection between public leisure spaces and civic identity reflects the symbolic as well as functional significance of these environments. Public spaces often commemorate community history, express collective values, and provide stages for civic ritual. Through these symbolic functions, they contribute to community identity at the level of civic culture rather than merely interpersonal association.

The multi-use character of successful public spaces contributes to their identity-forming potential. When parks and plazas accommodate diverse activities—exercise, recreation, socializing, cultural events, political assembly—they become integrated into the daily routines of varied community segments. This integration creates multiple points of contact between residents and space, deepening the attachments that underlie community identity. A park used only by joggers on weekend mornings develops different significance than one used throughout the day by seniors, children, workers, and families engaged in diverse activities.

The transformation of underperforming public spaces into vibrant community assets demonstrates the malleability of space and its relationship to identity. Case studies reveal that spaces once perceived as dangerous or unwelcoming can be reimagined and redesigned to become sources of civic pride [4]. These transformations often involve both physical changes—improved maintenance, enhanced amenities, better lighting—and programmatic innovations—events, activities, and management practices that draw diverse users. The success of such transformations indicates that community identity is not simply given by physical environment but actively constructed through the interaction of space, use, and meaning.

### 3.3 Intergenerational and Intercultural Encounters

Public leisure spaces serve as sites for encounters between different demographic and cultural groups, with implications for community identity that extend beyond immediate social interaction. Research on inter-ethnic interaction in urban public spaces has examined how diverse populations share and negotiate these environments [5]. These encounters can foster appreciation of diversity and contribute to more inclusive forms of community identity, though the impacts may be limited to positive feelings about diversity rather than deeper social integration.

The conditioning factors affecting public space and social cohesion include sociodemographic characteristics that shape how individuals experience and use public space [5]. Age, in particular, influences patterns of public space use and the types of social encounters that occur. Older adults may gravitate toward spaces conducive to sedentary activities like sitting and socializing, while younger users may prefer spaces supporting physical activity. These differentiated use patterns can create temporal and spatial segregation within public spaces, potentially limiting the intergenerational encounters that might otherwise contribute to unified community identity.

Social grouping patterns in public spaces reveal how users of different characteristics experience collective social cohesion [5]. Analysis of patterns—such as couples, families, and friend groups—demonstrates that public spaces accommodate various configurations of social interaction. These patterns both reflect and reinforce community identity by providing visible evidence of community composition and social structure. When public spaces host diverse social groupings, they communicate inclusivity and belonging; when they predominantly serve homogeneous populations, they may inadvertently signal exclusion.

## 4. Commercialization of Public Leisure Spaces

### 4.1 Business Improvement Districts and Governance Transformation

Business Improvement Districts or Zones represent the most common model for capturing future value created by present-day investment in public spaces [1]. These governance structures involve the collection of additional assessments from property owners within designated areas to fund enhanced services, improvements, and programming. The proliferation of BIDs reflects broader trends toward market-based governance of urban space, wherein private actors assume responsibilities traditionally associated with municipal government.

The emergence of BIDs represents a significant transformation in public space governance. Under traditional models, public spaces were maintained through general tax revenues and managed by public agencies accountable to democratic constituencies. BIDs introduce private actors with commercial interests into governance structures, creating hybrid arrangements that blend public and private authority. These arrangements can generate resources that municipalities cannot or will not provide, enabling improvements that enhance space quality and attractiveness. However, they also introduce commercial logics into space management, potentially prioritizing profitability over equity and access.

The expertise developed in cities like Detroit and others around the country regarding BID creation and governance reflects accumulated learning about the pros and cons of these structures [1]. Proponents argue that BIDs provide focused management, specialized expertise, and reliable funding that public agencies cannot match. Critics caution that BIDs may prioritize commercial interests over community needs, create islands of privilege in otherwise underserved areas, and undermine democratic accountability in public space governance. The balance between these considerations varies across contexts, depending on BID structure, community engagement, and oversight mechanisms.

### 4.2 Value-Capture Mechanisms and Financial Sustainability

Beyond BIDs, numerous value-capture mechanisms have been developed to finance public space improvements and ensure long-term sustainability [1]. These mechanisms share the common feature of linking public space investment to future revenue streams, capturing increased property values or economic activity generated by improvements. The variety of approaches reflects ongoing experimentation with public-private finance and the search for sustainable models that reduce dependence on general tax revenues.

Specific value-capture mechanisms include agreements for portions of future rent increases to go to public space steward organizations, monthly service charges included in condo association fees for purchase units, corporate memberships in "Friends" organizations with member benefits, and deferred fees triggered by market growth and increased values [1]. These mechanisms acknowledge the quality of life premium added by public space and seek to recapture some of this value for ongoing space management and improvement. They represent sophisticated financial engineering that treats public space as an appreciating asset generating returns that can be reinvested.

Municipal bonds for capital improvements in redevelopment areas represent another value-capture approach, with repayment from new tax revenues resulting from improvements [1]. This approach requires predicting future revenue growth, securing support from stakeholders, and managing the legal and financial complexity of bond issuance. The multi-step process—defining mechanisms through research, securing stakeholder support, identifying revenue assumptions, and selling bonds—reflects the substantial institutional capacity required for value-capture finance. Public-private partnership arrangements may define municipal entities to own spaces and nonprofits to manage them, further hybridizing governance and finance.

### 4.3 Public-Private Partnerships in Park Management

Public-private partnerships (PPPs) have emerged as significant arrangements for park management, particularly in the context of deferred maintenance challenges facing public park systems [6]. Under PPPs, private sector operators assume responsibility for park maintenance and operations under contracts that specify performance standards. This approach contrasts with traditional governmental management, where maintenance is routinely deferred amid budget pressures and accountability mechanisms may be lacking.

The private sector's accountability to maintenance standards under PPPs ensures proper park upkeep in ways that public management often cannot achieve [6]. When private operators fail to meet contractual standards, remedies are available through the partnership agreement. This accountability structure can address the problem of deferred maintenance that afflicts many public park systems, where political pressures and competing budget priorities lead to systematic underinvestment in park care. PPPs insulate park maintenance from these pressures by creating contractual obligations that persist across budget cycles and political transitions.

The application of PPPs to state parks and other public recreation assets reflects broader trends toward private involvement in public infrastructure [6]. While public lands serve various purposes—ecological preservation, resource extraction, recreation—the recreation enterprise generates the bulk of individual park revenues in states that charge user fees. This revenue potential makes recreation assets attractive candidates for PPP arrangements, as private operators can generate returns through user fees while public agencies benefit from improved maintenance and reduced operational burdens. However, the commercial orientation of PPPs raises questions about access, equity, and the preservation of public character in spaces traditionally understood as common resources.

## 5. Tensions Between Commercialization and Community Identity

### 5.1 Gentrification and Displacement Pressures

The relationship between public space investment and gentrification represents one of the most contentious issues in contemporary urban planning. New green spaces can increase property values and, without protective measures, price out marginalized communities [7]. This dynamic reflects the fundamental tension between public space improvements that benefit communities and the market pressures those improvements may generate. When public investment enhances neighborhood desirability, it simultaneously increases property values and rents, potentially displacing the residents who stood to benefit from improvements.

Decades of redlining and urban renewal, rooted in racist planning and design policies, created conditions for gentrification to occur in American cities [7]. The primary concern with gentrification today is displacement, which primarily impacts marginalized communities shaped by historical denial of access to mortgages and other resources. When new green spaces are developed in these historically marginalized areas, they risk repeating patterns wherein public investment benefits external actors more than existing residents. The communities that most need access to quality public leisure spaces may be those most vulnerable to displacement when such access is provided.

Community-led approaches offer potential for reconciling green space investment with anti-displacement goals. If projects are led by marginalized communities, they can be embraced rather than resisted [7]. This approach requires genuine community ownership of planning and development processes, ensuring that residents have meaningful influence over how spaces are designed, programmed, and managed. When communities lead development rather than merely participating in it, they can ensure that spaces reflect local needs and priorities while capturing benefits for existing residents rather than outside investors.

### 5.2 Inclusivity Challenges in Commercialized Spaces

Commercialization of public spaces raises significant concerns about inclusivity and whose interests are served by market-oriented governance. When public spaces are constantly programmed with the same types of events—outdoor concerts, public markets, parties involving alcohol—they may attract only users comfortable with these activities, excluding others who might otherwise use the space [8]. This dynamic reflects how commercial imperatives can narrow the range of uses and users that public spaces accommodate, undermining their function as inclusive commons.

The significance of each public space differs for every person, and inclusivity varies depending on where spaces are located and how people interact with them [8]. For many, public space is a common area designed for leisure, recreation, social gathering, and social interactions—a public asset accessed daily or periodically. However, personal experience and sense of inclusiveness can differ substantially even when two people use the exact same public space. These variations reflect the intersectionality of personal identities and lived experiences, including factors of race, ethnicity, age, disability, income, and cultural background.

Conventional approaches to inclusivity often overlook people marginalized from mainstream public space discourse [8]. When dominant users take over the narrative of inclusiveness in a given space, the experiences and needs of marginalized populations may be systematically ignored. This dynamic can be exacerbated by commercialization, as market-oriented management may prioritize the preferences of higher-spending users over those with fewer economic resources. Ensuring genuine inclusivity in commercialized public spaces requires deliberate attention to diverse user needs and ongoing engagement with marginalized communities.

### 5.3 Resistance and Community Contestation

Communities are not passive recipients of commercialization's effects but active agents who contest and resist problematic developments. In Valencia, residents from surrounding low-income neighborhoods organized to protect existing trees with significant symbolic value from demolition in a public-private partnership development [9]. Organized under the social platform "Salvem el Parc Central," these residents rejected construction that would benefit developers while threatening community heritage and potentially accelerating gentrification.

Protests and direct denunciations represent one articulation of community resistance to problematic commercialization [9]. These collective actions demonstrate that community identity can motivate opposition to developments that threaten shared resources and collective memory. The symbolic value of trees that provided refuge during civil conflict illustrates how public spaces carry historical meanings that residents seek to protect. When commercialization threatens these meanings, communities may mobilize to assert their identity and interests against market pressures.

The dynamics of community resistance reflect the contested nature of public space in commercialized urban environments. Public spaces are not neutral containers but arenas of social struggle where different interests and visions compete. The outcomes of these struggles depend on the relative power of actors—developers, governments, community organizations, residents—and the resources they can mobilize. Understanding commercialization's effects on community identity requires attention not only to structural conditions but to the agency of communities in shaping how commercialization unfolds.

## 6. Social Media and Contemporary Leisure Behavior

### 6.1 Digital Mediation of Space and Identity

Social media have emerged as significant influences on leisure behavior and urban space usage, creating new relationships between digital and physical environments [10]. As new means of information dissemination, social media have impacted social communication and the uses of urban space in ways that reshape how communities form and identity is expressed. The influence mechanism operates through two stages: the influence of social media on residents' leisure behavior and urban space, and the influence of time on residents' leisure behavior, leisure patterns, and spatial requirements.

Spatially, social media creates and strengthens communication experiences while interacting bodies remain separate, located in different spaces [10]. This capacity for connection across distance has transformed how communities form and maintain themselves. Residents can sustain relationships with geographically distant others while simultaneously connecting with local community members through digital networks. These dual processes of connection—simultaneously global and local—reshape the spatiality of community identity, potentially expanding communities beyond neighborhood boundaries while also creating new possibilities for local connection.

The documentation of specific examples at the local level remains an important area for further research [10]. Understanding how social media influences leisure behavior patterns and spatial structure in mid-sized urban destinations requires empirical investigation of particular contexts. The mechanisms through which digital and physical spaces interact in shaping community identity likely vary across settings, depending on local culture, infrastructure, and social dynamics. Case study research can illuminate these context-specific dynamics while contributing to broader theoretical understanding.

### 6.2 Identity Construction Through Digital Expression

Advertising and media provide repertoires of images, symbols, and narratives that individuals draw upon to construct and express identities [11]. This construction process extends to community identity, as residents draw on available cultural resources to make sense of their belonging and express it to others. In contemporary contexts, digital media increasingly provide these resources, offering templates for identity expression that circulate through social networks and influence how communities understand themselves.

Consumers often purchase products not only for functional value but also for symbolic meaning, using them to signal affiliation with particular social groups and adherence to certain cultural values [11]. This dynamic extends to the consumption of experiences in public leisure spaces, where the choice of where to spend leisure time communicates identity to others. When residents choose particular parks, plazas, or community spaces, they signal their community affiliations and social positions. These choices both express and constitute community identity, as the aggregation of individual choices creates patterns of usage that define community boundaries and character.

The authenticity of representation in media significantly influences its effects on identity [11]. When advertising actively celebrates diversity and portrays cultures authentically, it can affirm cultural identities and promote inclusivity. Conversely, tokenistic representation can do more harm than good, reinforcing stereotypes and marginalizing those it purports to represent. These dynamics have implications for how public spaces are represented in digital media and how community identity is constructed through digital circulation of images and narratives about place.

## 7. Policy Implications and Recommendations

### 7.1 Balancing Commercial Sustainability and Social Function

The evidence reviewed in this report suggests that commercial mechanisms can contribute to public space sustainability but require careful design to preserve identity-forming functions. Value-capture mechanisms, BIDs, and PPPs can generate resources that public agencies cannot provide, enabling improvements that enhance space quality and community benefit [1]. However, these mechanisms must be structured to ensure that commercial interests do not override community needs and that spaces remain accessible and welcoming to diverse populations.

Successful integration of commercial elements requires attention to governance structures that include community representation and accountability mechanisms. When BID boards or PPP partnerships include resident representatives and community organizations, commercial decision-making can be informed by community perspectives and needs. Similarly, when value-capture mechanisms include provisions for affordable housing, anti-displacement measures, or community benefit agreements, the benefits of public space investment can be shared more equitably.

The design of commercial amenities within public spaces also requires careful consideration. When spaces include commercial elements—cafés, retail, event programming—these should complement rather than dominate the public character of the space [4]. The goal should be enhancing the public's ability to use and enjoy the space, not maximizing commercial revenue at the expense of public access and comfort. Successful examples like Bryant Park demonstrate that commercial elements can be integrated thoughtfully, generating revenue while maintaining space quality and public character.

### 7.2 Ensuring Inclusivity and Access

Policy interventions are needed to ensure that commercialized public spaces remain inclusive and accessible to diverse community members. This requires going beyond formal access—ensuring that spaces are physically open to all—to address substantive inclusion—ensuring that diverse populations feel welcome and can use spaces comfortably [8]. Programming should accommodate diverse activities and user groups, avoiding the dominance of any single use or user type.

Engagement with marginalized communities is essential for understanding their needs and ensuring that spaces serve them effectively [8]. When conventional approaches to inclusivity overlook marginalized populations, deliberate efforts are needed to center their experiences and perspectives. This engagement should occur not only during planning processes but on an ongoing basis, as community needs evolve and spaces are continuously programmed and managed.

Anti-displacement measures should accompany public space investments in vulnerable communities [7]. These might include community land trusts, rent control or stabilization policies, right-to-remain provisions, or community ownership models that ensure residents benefit from neighborhood improvements. Without such measures, public space investment may contribute to displacement that undermines the community identity it was intended to strengthen.

### 7.3 Supporting Community Agency and Resistance

Policy should support rather than constrain community agency in shaping public space development and management. This includes ensuring meaningful community participation in planning processes, respecting community knowledge and priorities, and providing resources for community organizations to engage effectively [7]. When communities lead development rather than merely consulting on it, outcomes are more likely to reflect community needs and generate authentic community identity.

Legal and institutional frameworks should protect community rights to contest problematic developments and resist displacement [9]. When communities organize to protect public spaces from commercialization that threatens their interests, their efforts should be supported rather than suppressed. This may involve recognizing community standing in planning decisions, providing legal resources for community organizations, and creating avenues for community appeal of decisions that threaten community interests.

The recognition of public spaces as sites of community identity formation should inform policy at multiple scales. At the site level, design and management should prioritize the social functions that underlie identity formation—opportunities for interaction, comfort, safety, and belonging. At the neighborhood level, public space networks should connect diverse populations and facilitate encounters across difference. At the citywide level, public space systems should ensure equitable distribution of quality spaces across communities, recognizing that access to good public space is a matter of social justice.

## 8. Conclusion

This report has examined the complex relationship between public leisure space usage and community identity formation within the context of commercialization. The evidence demonstrates that public leisure spaces serve as essential infrastructure for community identity, providing sites for social interaction, place attachment, and the accumulation of shared experiences that bind residents together. The physical and social environments of these spaces contribute significantly to residents' sense of belonging, pride, and ownership—key dimensions of community identity.

Commercialization introduces both opportunities and challenges for this identity-forming function. Value-capture mechanisms, Business Improvement Districts, and public-private partnerships can generate resources that enhance space quality and sustainability [1]. However, commercial logics may prioritize profitability over equity, marketability over authenticity, and narrow user preferences over inclusive access. The outcomes depend on governance structures, policy frameworks, and community agency in shaping how commercialization unfolds.

The tensions between commercialization and community identity are most acute in contexts of inequality and displacement pressure. When public space investment increases property values and attracts affluent newcomers, existing communities may face displacement that threatens the very identity they sought to strengthen through improved spaces [7]. Ensuring that public space benefits existing communities requires deliberate anti-displacement measures, community leadership in development processes, and ongoing attention to inclusivity and access.

Social media and digital media have added new dimensions to the relationship between public space and community identity, creating new possibilities for connection across distance while also providing resources for identity construction and expression [10][11]. These dynamics warrant continued research as digital mediation of everyday life intensifies and reshapes how communities form and maintain themselves.

The fundamental insight from this analysis is that public leisure spaces are not merely physical infrastructure but social infrastructure—essential resources for the reproduction of community life and identity. Their commercialization should be approached with awareness of these social functions and commitment to preserving them. When commercial mechanisms are designed thoughtfully—with community representation, anti-displacement provisions, and inclusivity commitments—they can enhance rather than undermine the identity-forming potential of public spaces. The challenge for policy and practice is to realize this possibility while guarding against the risks that market logics pose to the public character and inclusive functions of these vital community resources.
