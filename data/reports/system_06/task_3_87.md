## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Context

The global proliferation of cultural heritage tourism has created unprecedented challenges for destination managers seeking to balance visitor access with preservation imperatives. Heritage sites, whether individual monuments or extensive historic districts, increasingly compete for visitor attention within broader destination ecosystems. The traditional approach of treating heritage sites as isolated points of interest has given way to more sophisticated understandings of how spatial connections between sites can enhance both visitor experience and conservation outcomes.

Heritage corridors have emerged as a foundational concept for addressing these challenges. Originally developed in the United States as a mechanism for regional heritage protection and management, heritage corridors are now defined as "linear or spatially connected areas that preserve and integrate historical, cultural, and natural heritage assets while promoting sustainable urban development" [1]. This approach emphasizes the significance of cultural and natural resource values and typically requires coordination between cultural and environmental management authorities. The corridor concept transforms heritage from a collection of discrete points into a networked system, enabling visitors to experience interconnected narratives while reducing the cognitive fragmentation that often accompanies site-by-site visitation.

The theoretical shift toward connected heritage systems aligns with broader developments in visitor psychology research. Contemporary understanding of heritage tourism recognizes that "cognition and, hence, motivation imbues the ability for the tourist to evaluate tourism impacts" [2]. This cognitive dimension is particularly significant because it connects directly to knowledge retention, a primary objective of heritage tourism. When visitors engage with heritage sites, they undergo complex cognitive processes involving "emotional, reflective, and cognitive processes during heritage site experiences" that "not only influence immediate behavioral decisions but also exert a sustaining effect through memory recall" [3].

### 1.2 Research Problem

Despite the recognized benefits of heritage corridor approaches, significant gaps remain in understanding how to optimize the balance between knowledge acquisition and visitor fatigue within connected multi-site systems. This balance is critical because excessive cognitive load can undermine learning outcomes, while insufficient engagement fails to justify the investment of visitor time and resources. The challenge is compounded by the heterogeneous nature of heritage visitors, whose physical capacities, cognitive preferences, and knowledge backgrounds vary substantially.

Route planning for heritage tourism must therefore address multiple, sometimes competing objectives. Physical fatigue considerations demand attention to walking distances, elevation changes, and rest opportunities. Cognitive fatigue concerns require careful management of information density, narrative complexity, and sensory stimulation levels. Simultaneously, route optimization must account for site significance, temporal constraints, and the logical sequencing of historical narratives. The integration of these considerations into coherent planning frameworks represents a significant technical and theoretical challenge.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of Heritage Corridor Development

### 2.1 Evolution of Heritage Corridor Concepts

The concept of heritage corridors has undergone substantial evolution since its origins in the American conservation movement of the 1980s. Initially conceived as extensions of greenway approaches, heritage corridors were understood primarily as linear geographic features—valleys, canals, historical roads, or railway networks—that could serve as carriers for integrated conservation strategies [4]. The early theoretical framework emphasized connecting "heritage sites, villages, and landmarks of cultural, historical, or ecological value" to form coherent regional conservation and development systems.

Contemporary heritage corridor theory has expanded considerably beyond these initial formulations. Research indicates that "heritage corridor research is evolving from experience-driven methods to technology-driven and interdisciplinary approaches, gradually achieving spatial analysis, data quantification, and multidimensional optimization" [4]. This evolution reflects both the increasing availability of computational tools for spatial analysis and growing recognition that effective heritage management requires integration across multiple domains, including urban planning, environmental science, visitor psychology, and cultural economics.

The shift toward quantitative and technology-driven approaches has enabled more sophisticated analysis of corridor networks. Researchers have developed network analysis metrics—including the α index (network closure), β index (path complexity), and γ index (connectivity efficiency)—to "quantitatively assess the accessibility, redundancy, and structural robustness of the corridor network" [4]. These metrics provide objective measures for evaluating corridor designs and identifying opportunities for improvement. The integrated methodological framework that has emerged covers the full process from node identification and path construction to system validation, offering a logically coherent and technically rigorous approach to heritage corridor planning.

### 2.2 Spatial Integration and Urban Morphology

The integration of heritage corridors with urban built environments represents a critical dimension of contemporary heritage planning. Research demonstrates that "heritage can be better recognized through urban morphology analysis as part of the urban components" [1]. This recognition has important implications for route planning, as it suggests that heritage experiences must be understood within broader urban contexts rather than as isolated interventions.

The spatial distribution of heritage sites significantly influences corridor design possibilities. Urban core sites, such as Fengyue Pass, Xiangtang Mountain Grottoes, and Zhifang Yuhuang Pavilion, demonstrate how heritage locations "can extend heritage revitalization to surrounding areas, creating interconnected corridors and enhancing accessibility and commercial experience" [1]. These urban heritage nodes benefit from existing infrastructure, higher population densities, and established commercial ecosystems that can support heritage tourism. In contrast, dispersed peripheral sites, like the Yangjiaopu Site and Yijing Dragon King Temple, require different integration strategies, potentially combining "with rural cultural facilities to create public cultural spaces" [1].

The environmental spatial evaluation framework provides useful guidance for understanding these differential potentials. A-Class heritage sites, representing "the highest potential for revitalizing surrounding regions through heritage management," share common characteristics including "improved accessibility and environmental quality" that "facilitate the integration of these sites into diverse urban contexts" [1]. These findings suggest that corridor planning should prioritize connections to high-potential heritage nodes while developing appropriate strategies for peripheral sites that may require additional infrastructure investment.

### 2.3 Holistic Heritage Consideration

The corridor approach fundamentally transforms heritage management by enabling "holistic consideration of heritage and the surrounding urban built environment elements" [1]. This holistic perspective is essential for optimizing the knowledge-to-fatigue balance because it allows planners to consider heritage experiences as integrated systems rather than collections of independent sites. When heritage elements are considered in isolation, there is a risk of either overloading visitors with redundant information or creating gaps in narrative coherence that undermine knowledge acquisition.

The holistic approach also enables more effective management of visitor flows and experience pacing. By understanding heritage sites as nodes within interconnected systems, managers can design routes that distribute visitors appropriately across the network, preventing overcrowding at popular sites while ensuring that lesser-known sites receive adequate attention. Research on intelligent route planning demonstrates that optimized routes "improve visit efficiency and balance attraction utilization, preventing overcrowding in popular areas" [5]. This balancing function is particularly important for managing cognitive load, as overcrowded sites create sensory and attentional demands that can accelerate fatigue.

## 3. Cognitive Dimensions of Heritage Experience

### 3.1 Embodied Cognition in Heritage Settings

The cognitive processes underlying heritage experience have received increasing research attention in recent years. The concept of embodied cognition provides a useful framework for understanding how visitors construct knowledge and meaning during heritage encounters. Research conceptualizes "the embodied cognition process in heritage settings across three dimensions: multisensory perception, physical engagement, and cognitive processing" [3]. These dimensions interact dynamically to shape both immediate experience and long-term knowledge retention.

Multisensory perception encompasses the visual, auditory, tactile, and olfactory dimensions of heritage experience. When visitors engage with heritage sites, they process information through multiple sensory channels simultaneously, creating rich experiential records that support subsequent memory recall. Physical engagement refers to the bodily dimensions of heritage experience, including movement through space, physical interaction with artifacts and environments, and the embodied sensations associated with heritage encounters. Cognitive processing involves the interpretive and meaning-making activities that transform sensory inputs into lasting knowledge structures.

Research demonstrates that each of these dimensions positively influences place attachment and destination-supportive behavior intention [3]. Specifically, "multisensory perception positively influences place attachment," "physical engagement positively influences place attachment," and "cognitive processing positively influences place attachment" [3]. Similarly, all three dimensions contribute to "destination-supportive behavior intention" [3]. These findings have important implications for route planning, suggesting that optimal routes should incorporate opportunities for multisensory engagement, physical activity appropriate to visitor capacity, and cognitive processing through well-designed interpretive experiences.

### 3.2 Cognitive Load Management

The management of cognitive load represents a critical consideration for heritage route planning. Cognitive load theory, originally developed in educational psychology, distinguishes between intrinsic load (inherent to the material being learned), extraneous load (imposed by the learning environment), and germane load (contributing to schema construction). Heritage experiences must balance these load types to optimize learning outcomes while preventing cognitive overload that leads to fatigue and reduced retention.

Recent research on heritage games provides valuable insights into cognitive load management strategies. The development of "cognitive load-based multi-level annotation model[s] for knowledge acquisition in heritage games" demonstrates that "precise cognitive load regulation prevents information overload, dynamic information presentation ensures seamless and comprehensive learning, and tiered task complexity enhances user motivation and interaction quality" [6]. These principles can be directly applied to heritage route design, suggesting that information delivery should be regulated, progressive, and adapted to visitor capacity.

The challenge of cognitive load management is particularly acute in multi-site heritage experiences. Visitors traversing connected heritage corridors must integrate information across multiple sites while maintaining coherent understanding of broader historical and cultural narratives. Without careful planning, the accumulation of site-specific information can create cognitive overload that undermines rather than enhances knowledge acquisition. Route planning must therefore incorporate not only physical logistics but also information management strategies that distribute cognitive demands appropriately across the visitor experience.

### 3.3 Knowledge Retention and Learning Outcomes

The ultimate objective of heritage tourism, from an educational perspective, is knowledge retention that extends beyond the immediate visit experience. Research on learning retention suggests that "the awareness of being at a place where historic events took place is of great importance" for psychological engagement with heritage [7]. This awareness creates cognitive and emotional connections that support long-term memory formation and knowledge integration.

Studies of on-site learning demonstrate that knowledge gained at heritage sites can be retained for extended periods when appropriate conditions are met. Research comparing on-site quiz performance with follow-up assessments conducted weeks later shows that "knowledge gained at the Visitor Center was retained for at least the 3-6 weeks between" assessments [8]. These findings suggest that well-designed heritage experiences can create lasting learning outcomes, supporting the value proposition of heritage tourism investment.

The relationship between physical experience and knowledge retention has particular relevance for route planning. Research indicates that outdoor learning experiences may enhance retention compared to indoor settings, with the "cultural and historical context of a local outdoor site" potentially enhancing learning retention [9]. This finding supports the value of heritage corridor approaches that encourage visitors to engage with heritage in its physical context rather than through mediated representations. However, the physical demands of extended outdoor heritage experiences must be carefully managed to prevent fatigue from undermining the cognitive benefits of contextual learning.

## 4. Route Optimization Technologies and Methods

### 4.1 Algorithmic Approaches to Heritage Route Planning

The application of computational algorithms to heritage route planning has advanced significantly in recent years, enabling more sophisticated optimization of visitor experiences. The traveling salesman problem (TSP) and its variants provide foundational models for heritage route optimization, with researchers employing various algorithms to generate optimal or near-optimal solutions. The ant colony algorithm, which "mimics the mechanisms of natural selection and genetics" by "simulating the biological evolution processes of selection, crossover, and mutation," has proven particularly effective for cultural heritage tourism route optimization [10].

Route optimization algorithms evaluate potential routes against fitness functions that incorporate multiple factors. Research indicates that effective fitness functions consider "route length, scenic spot popularity, and tourist satisfaction" [10]. By systematically evaluating alternative route configurations, these algorithms can identify solutions that balance competing objectives such as total distance, visit duration, and site coverage. The resulting optimized routes can "swiftly adapt to diverse tourist demands, offering steadfast and proficient tourism services" while "precisely recommend[ing] tourist routes tailored to individual preferences" [10].

The Q-Learning algorithm represents another powerful approach to heritage route optimization. Research demonstrates that "the Q-Learning algorithm creates the best route that includes all the attractions while shortening the whole path and achieving rapid convergence" [5]. This algorithm is particularly effective for managing the complex spatial relationships characteristic of heritage districts, where sites may be irregularly distributed and subject to varying accessibility constraints. The optimized routes generated by Q-Learning "improve visit efficiency and balance attraction utilization, preventing overcrowding in popular areas" [5], addressing both efficiency and experience quality objectives.

### 4.2 Geographic Information Systems and Network Analysis

Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have become essential tools for heritage route planning, enabling sophisticated spatial analysis and visualization. Research on heritage site routing in Faro demonstrates the application of "ArcGIS, utilizing the Network Analyst extension and vector data obtained from OpenStreetMap" to plan routes connecting 54 historical heritage sites [11]. This approach enables planners to incorporate real-world constraints including street networks, elevation changes, and accessibility features.

The GIS-based approach generates quantitative assessments of route characteristics that inform planning decisions. For the Faro heritage sites, researchers found that "the optimal route to visit the 54 historical heritage sites forms a closed circuit spanning 17.35 km" [11]. When routes are divided into segments, the system can generate "one covering 31 monuments in the old city and another 24 monuments in the outer area of the urban center," measuring "6.16 km and 11.31 km, respectively" [11]. These precise measurements enable planners to design routes appropriate for different visitor capacities and time constraints.

The temporal dimensions of heritage routes are as important as spatial characteristics. Research demonstrates that transportation mode significantly affects route completion times: "If the tourist uses an electric vehicle at an average speed of 15 km/h, with stops of 5 min at each monument, it takes about 5 h and 40 min. If opting to travel on foot at an average speed of 3 km/h, the same route with 5 min stops at each monument will take approximately 10 h and 17 min" [11]. These findings highlight the importance of offering multiple route options accommodating different physical capacities and time availability.

### 4.3 Big Data and Intelligent Systems

The integration of big data technologies with heritage route planning represents an emerging frontier in tourism management. Traditional planning approaches "rely on a large amount of real-time data" that is often "limited and difficult to integrate" [12]. Big data approaches address these limitations by enabling the aggregation and analysis of diverse data streams including visitor movement patterns, site popularity metrics, environmental conditions, and social media feedback.

Intelligent systems combining big data with advanced algorithms offer significant advantages for heritage route planning. Research demonstrates that "merging BD and intelligent algorithms" enables "personalized recommendations and route optimization, optimize[s] traffic management of scenic spots, flexibly respond[s] to emergencies, promote[s] the intelligent and refined management of historical district tourism" [12]. These capabilities are particularly valuable for heritage sites facing variable visitor loads and dynamic environmental conditions.

The application of big data to heritage route planning supports more nuanced understanding of visitor behavior and preferences. By analyzing patterns in visitor movement and停留时间, planners can identify optimal site sequencing, predict congestion points, and develop adaptive routing strategies. Future developments are likely to incorporate "visitor preferences and real-time traffic conditions for greater personalization and applicability" [5], enabling dynamic route optimization that responds to changing conditions during visitor trips.

## 5. Balancing Knowledge and Fatigue in Multi-Site Heritage Connections

### 5.1 Physical Fatigue Considerations

Physical fatigue represents a fundamental constraint on heritage experience quality and knowledge acquisition. Extended walking through heritage districts imposes cumulative physiological demands that can undermine both physical comfort and cognitive capacity. Research on heritage routing demonstrates that walking routes covering multiple sites require substantial time investments: visiting 31 monuments on foot "will take 4 h and 45 min" [11], while a comprehensive 54-site route requires "approximately 10 h and 17 min" when walking at 3 km/h with 5-minute stops per site [11].

These findings have significant implications for route planning strategy. The substantial time requirements for walking routes suggest that multi-site heritage experiences must be designed with realistic expectations about visitor capacity. Route planning should incorporate rest opportunities, provide clear information about expected durations, and offer alternatives for visitors with different physical capacities. The availability of alternative transportation modes, such as electric vehicles that reduce the 54-site route to "about 5 h and 40 min" [11], can extend the range of visitors who can complete comprehensive heritage circuits.

The spatial distribution of heritage sites within corridors significantly influences physical fatigue outcomes. Urban core sites benefit from existing infrastructure and shorter distances between locations, while peripheral sites may require longer transfers that contribute to cumulative fatigue. Route planning should consider the sequencing of sites to manage physical demands, potentially reserving more demanding segments for periods of peak visitor energy or providing transportation alternatives for challenging segments.

### 5.2 Cognitive Fatigue Management

Beyond physical demands, heritage experiences impose cognitive burdens that can lead to mental fatigue and reduced knowledge acquisition. The processing of historical information, interpretation of cultural meanings, and integration of experiences across multiple sites all consume cognitive resources. Without appropriate management, cognitive fatigue can undermine the educational objectives that justify heritage tourism investment.

Cognitive load theory provides guidance for managing cognitive fatigue in heritage contexts. Research suggests that effective cognitive load management requires "precise cognitive load regulation" to prevent information overload, "dynamic information presentation" that adapts to visitor processing capacity, and "tiered task complexity" that progressively builds understanding [6]. These principles suggest that heritage routes should incorporate graduated information density, with more complex interpretive content positioned at appropriate points in the visitor journey.

The sequencing of heritage sites within routes can significantly influence cognitive fatigue patterns. Research on embodied cognition indicates that "positive cognition also enhances the consistency between emotion and action, making behavior more executable through physical engagement" [3]. This finding suggests that routes incorporating varied engagement modalities—active exploration, contemplative reflection, interactive interpretation—may sustain cognitive engagement more effectively than routes with uniform experience types. Route planning should therefore consider the cognitive rhythm of heritage experiences, alternating between high-stimulation and low-stimulation activities.

### 5.3 Optimizing the Knowledge-to-Fatigue Ratio

The central challenge of heritage route planning is optimizing the ratio between knowledge acquisition and fatigue outcomes. This optimization requires balancing multiple factors including physical demands, cognitive load, information density, and experiential variety. The goal is to maximize learning outcomes and visitor satisfaction while remaining within acceptable fatigue thresholds.

Research on heritage corridor development suggests that connected systems can achieve synergies that improve the knowledge-to-fatigue ratio compared to isolated site visits. When heritage sites are connected within coherent corridors, visitors can construct integrated understanding that enhances knowledge retention beyond what site-specific learning could achieve. The "holistic consideration of heritage" enabled by corridor approaches [1] supports this integrative learning while potentially reducing redundant information processing that contributes to cognitive fatigue.

The optimization of knowledge-to-fatigue ratios benefits from quantitative assessment approaches. Network analysis metrics including accessibility, redundancy, and structural robustness provide objective measures for evaluating corridor designs [4]. When combined with visitor studies assessing knowledge outcomes and fatigue indicators, these metrics enable evidence-based optimization of heritage route systems. The integration of algorithmic optimization with empirical validation creates iterative improvement cycles that progressively enhance heritage experience quality.

## 6. Practical Implementation Strategies

### 6.1 Multi-Modal Transportation Integration

Effective heritage route planning requires integration of multiple transportation modes to accommodate diverse visitor capacities and preferences. Research demonstrates that offering transportation alternatives significantly extends the accessibility of heritage routes. Electric vehicle options reduce route completion times by approximately 45% compared to walking [11], enabling visitors with limited physical capacity to access comprehensive heritage experiences.

The integration of transportation modes should be seamless and intuitive. Heritage corridors should incorporate clear wayfinding systems, convenient transfer points, and real-time information about transportation options. The physical infrastructure supporting multi-modal transport—including charging stations for electric vehicles, bicycle parking, and accessible pathways—should be integrated into heritage district design rather than treated as afterthoughts.

Transportation planning should also consider the experiential dimensions of movement between heritage sites. The journey between sites is not merely a transition but an opportunity for continued engagement with heritage landscapes. Well-designed transportation systems can enhance rather than interrupt heritage experiences, providing interpretive content during transit and offering perspectives on heritage landscapes that are unavailable to pedestrians.

### 6.2 Tiered Information Delivery Systems

The implementation of tiered information delivery systems enables heritage routes to accommodate visitors with varying knowledge backgrounds and cognitive preferences. Research on cognitive load management demonstrates that "dynamic information presentation ensures seamless and comprehensive learning" [6], suggesting that information systems should adapt to individual visitor needs rather than delivering uniform content.

Tiered systems might offer multiple levels of interpretive content, from introductory overviews suitable for casual visitors to detailed scholarly information for enthusiasts and researchers. Mobile applications and digital guides can enable visitors to select appropriate information density in real-time, adjusting based on remaining energy levels and accumulated knowledge. This flexibility supports optimal cognitive load management across diverse visitor populations.

The physical environment of heritage sites should support tiered information delivery through complementary analog and digital systems. On-site signage and interpretive panels provide baseline information accessible to all visitors, while digital supplements offer depth for those seeking more comprehensive understanding. This layered approach ensures that information is available without creating cognitive overload for visitors preferring lighter engagement.

### 6.3 Experience Pacing and Rest Integration

Thoughtful pacing of heritage experiences is essential for managing fatigue while maintaining engagement. Routes should incorporate rest opportunities at appropriate intervals, with rest points designed as positive experiences rather than mere breaks. Research on embodied cognition suggests that physical engagement enhances place attachment [3], implying that rest areas offering light physical activity may be more restorative than passive seating.

The timing of rest opportunities should consider both physical and cognitive fatigue patterns. Research on visitor behavior suggests that attention and engagement vary across visit duration, with typical decline patterns that can be addressed through well-planned interventions. Rest points positioned at narrative transition points in heritage routes can serve dual functions—providing physical and cognitive recovery while marking meaningful shifts in historical or cultural content.

The design of rest environments should support both recovery and continued engagement. Rest areas might offer interactive elements, refreshment options, and ambient heritage content that maintains visitor connection to the broader corridor experience. These spaces should be equipped with seating appropriate for visitors with varying mobility levels, shade and weather protection, and accessibility features ensuring universal inclusion.
