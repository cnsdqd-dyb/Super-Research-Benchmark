# Do Traditional Mutual Aid Networks Sustain Social Support in Communities with High Floating Populations?

## A Research Report on Network Stability, Social Capital, and Community Resilience in Mobile Populations

---

## Abstract

This research report examines the sustainability of traditional mutual aid networks in communities characterized by high population mobility, often referred to as "floating populations." Drawing on interdisciplinary scholarship spanning disaster sociology, migration studies, network theory, and community development, this analysis investigates whether conventional bonding and bridging social capital structures can maintain their supportive functions when confronted with continuous demographic flux. The evidence suggests that traditional mutual aid networks face significant structural challenges in high-mobility contexts, including weakened trust accumulation, disrupted reciprocity cycles, and diminished network density. However, adaptive mechanisms—including the emergence of hybrid organizational forms, technology-enabled coordination, and faith-based institutional bridges—can partially offset these challenges. The report concludes that while traditional mutual aid networks alone prove insufficient for sustaining social support in highly mobile communities, their integration with formal institutional arrangements and technology platforms offers promising pathways for maintaining community resilience amid demographic instability.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Research Context and Significance

The question of whether traditional mutual aid networks can sustain social support in communities with high floating populations represents one of the pressing challenges facing contemporary urban sociology and community development practice. As globalization accelerates human mobility and as economic forces drive unprecedented levels of internal migration and urban turnover, understanding the resilience of community support structures becomes increasingly vital for policymakers, practitioners, and scholars alike.

Mutual aid—the voluntary reciprocal exchange of resources, services, or knowledge between community members—has historically served as a foundational mechanism for social protection, particularly in times of crisis. As articulated by the anarchist philosopher Pëtr Kropotkin, mutual aid represents a fundamental component of human social organization, functioning as what he termed a "voluntary biological factor" that has driven societal evolution through cooperation rather than competition [1]. This conceptualization positions mutual aid not merely as an emergency response mechanism but as an intrinsic dimension of human social life, woven into the daily rituals and institutional structures that govern community existence.

However, the sustainability of these traditional mutual aid mechanisms faces particular stress when communities experience high levels of population turnover. The theoretical literature on social capital has long recognized that the accumulation of trust, the development of reciprocity norms, and the formation of stable interpersonal networks require temporal continuity and repeated interactions [2]. When community membership changes rapidly, these accumulation processes face disruption, potentially undermining the very foundations upon which mutual aid depends.

### 1.2 Defining Key Concepts

Before proceeding with the analysis, it is essential to establish clear operational definitions for the key concepts under investigation.

**Traditional mutual aid networks** refer to informal, community-based systems of reciprocal support that emerge organically among residents of geographic neighborhoods or social communities. These networks are characterized by face-to-face interactions, the development of interpersonal trust over time, and the establishment of reciprocity norms that govern exchange relationships. Examples include informal childcare arrangements between neighbors, mutual borrowing of household items, informal lending circles, and collective responses to emergencies within immediate residential proximity.

**Floating populations** denote communities experiencing high rates of residential turnover, where a significant proportion of residents have lived in the area for relatively short periods. This phenomenon characterizes many urban areas experiencing rapid gentrification, communities near international borders with high cross-border movement, cities with significant migrant worker populations, and areas with substantial refugee or internally displaced person concentrations.

**Social support** encompasses the various forms of assistance that individuals receive from their social networks, including emotional support (expressions of empathy, caring, and love), instrumental support (tangible assistance such as money, labor, or goods), informational support (advice, guidance, and relevant information), and appraisal support (feedback useful for self-evaluation) [3].

### 1.3 Research Objectives

This report pursues three primary objectives. First, it seeks to examine the theoretical mechanisms through which population mobility affects traditional mutual aid networks. Second, it analyzes empirical evidence regarding mutual aid sustainability across different mobility contexts. Third, it identifies adaptive strategies and institutional arrangements that may enhance the resilience of community support systems in high-turnover environments.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 Social Capital Theory and Network Stability

The sustainability of mutual aid in mobile communities cannot be adequately understood without reference to social capital theory, which provides the conceptual architecture for analyzing how social relationships generate resources and facilitate collective action.

Social capital manifests through multiple dimensions, with Robert Putnam's distinction between bonding and bridging social capital proving particularly relevant for understanding mutual aid dynamics [4]. Bonding social capital describes the strong ties that develop among individuals who share demographic characteristics, geographic proximity, or common identities—precisely the relationships that underpin traditional neighborhood-based mutual aid. Bridging social capital, by contrast, refers to weaker but more extensive ties that connect individuals across social cleavages, potentially linking community members to external resources and institutions.

The theoretical literature suggests that both forms of social capital face distinct challenges in high-mobility contexts. Bonding social capital requires sustained interaction and the development of interpersonal trust, processes that require temporal investment. As research on residential stability has demonstrated, longer periods of residence in a neighborhood promote attachment to the community and engagement in social interactions and activities [5]. Conversely, geographic relocation and frequent migration can compromise the availability of social support by disrupting the accumulation of bonding social capital.

The mechanisms through which residential stability facilitates social capital accumulation operate through multiple pathways. First, stability enables the development of what Granovetter termed the "strength of weak ties"—the gradual transformation of initially distant relationships into more meaningful connections through repeated interaction [6]. Second, stability allows for the emergence of shared community narratives and collective memories that strengthen group identity and solidarity. Third, stable residence enables participation in community institutions and informal social gatherings that serve as venues for relationship formation and maintenance.

### 2.2 Trust, Reciprocity, and Collective Action

Trust represents a foundational element for mutual aid sustainability, functioning as what some scholars have termed the "lubricant" of social cooperation [7]. In the context of mutual aid, trust operates on multiple levels: interpersonal trust between individuals who have developed confidence in each other's reliability, institutional trust in community organizations and leadership, and generalized trust in community norms regarding reciprocity and fair dealing.

Research on disaster response has demonstrated that trust embedded in bonding and bridging networks can mobilize mutual aid, information, and rapid collective action, particularly in response and recovery phases [8]. The Chilean fishing community response to the 2010 tsunami, as analyzed by Moreno and colleagues, illustrates how pre-existing social capital enabled coordinated survival strategies and community recovery. Similarly, organizational support networks in post-earthquake Canterbury, New Zealand, facilitated relational resilience through the maintenance of trust relationships across the disaster and recovery period [9].

However, the relationship between trust and mutual aid sustainability is not uniformly positive. As noted in the research literature, the same trust ties that facilitate cooperation can also normalize risky choices or crowd out adaptive responses [8]. In wildfire contexts, for instance, community trust may reinforce "stay-and-defend" norms even when evacuation would be safer. This finding suggests that the relationship between social capital and community welfare is context-dependent and may produce negative outcomes under certain conditions.

Reciprocity norms represent another critical dimension of mutual aid sustainability. The classical sociological literature, from Marcel Mauss to contemporary scholars, has emphasized that gift exchange and mutual aid are governed by implicit norms of reciprocity that maintain social bonds while ensuring some degree of balance in exchange relationships [10]. These norms develop through repeated interactions over time, creating expectations about appropriate behavior that can be enforced through social approval and disapproval.

In high-mobility contexts, the development and maintenance of reciprocity norms face particular challenges. When community membership changes rapidly, individuals may be less willing to invest in reciprocal relationships, knowing that their partners may soon depart. Moreover, newcomers may be unfamiliar with local reciprocity norms, creating misunderstandings and reducing the efficiency of mutual aid exchanges.

### 2.3 Resilience Theory and Adaptive Capacity

Resilience theory provides an additional lens for understanding mutual aid sustainability in mobile communities. Originally developed in ecological systems research, resilience thinking has been increasingly applied to social systems, with scholars examining how communities can maintain essential functions and recover from disturbances [11].

The distinction between stability and resilience proves particularly illuminating for this analysis. Engineering resilience measures how quickly a system returns to equilibrium after disturbance, emphasizing the speed of recovery. Social-ecological resilience, by contrast, focuses on the capacity of systems to absorb disturbance while maintaining essential functions, even if this involves transformation to a different state [12].

From this perspective, the question of whether traditional mutual aid networks can sustain social support in high-floating-population communities may be reframed: rather than asking whether these networks can maintain their pre-mobility structure and function, we might ask whether communities can develop alternative support arrangements that maintain essential protective functions despite demographic change.

This reframing acknowledges that traditional mutual aid networks, as they have historically existed in stable communities, may be inherently unstable in high-mobility contexts. However, it opens space for considering adaptive mechanisms through which communities might maintain social support functions through different organizational forms.

## 3. Challenges to Traditional Mutual Aid in High-Mobility Contexts

### 3.1 Network Density and Structural holes

The structure of social networks profoundly influences their capacity to generate and sustain mutual aid. Research in social network analysis has established that dense networks—where most community members are connected to most other members—facilitate the emergence and maintenance of cooperation through multiple mechanisms [13]. Dense networks enable effective norm enforcement, as deviant behavior is quickly observed and sanctioned by community members. They facilitate information flow, ensuring that knowledge about needs and resources spreads rapidly throughout the community. They also provide multiple pathways for resource exchange, ensuring that assistance can reach those in need through various routes.

High population mobility threatens network density by continuously introducing new nodes into the network while removing established nodes. Each new resident represents a potential addition to the network, but until that individual develops connections with existing members, they represent what network theorists term a "structural hole"—a gap in the network's connectivity [14]. The accumulation of structural holes as newcomers arrive and established residents depart can progressively fragment network structure, reducing density and impairing mutual aid functions.

The challenge is compounded by the fact that network development requires temporal investment. Research on social network formation demonstrates that tie strength develops through repeated interaction over time, with initial weak ties gradually strengthening through accumulated positive experiences [6]. When community membership turns over rapidly, there may be insufficient time for newcomers to develop the strong ties necessary for effective mutual aid participation.

### 3.2 Trust Accumulation and Decay

Trust, as a social psychological phenomenon, develops through repeated interactions that allow individuals to assess the reliability and benevolence of potential cooperation partners. This accumulation process follows what might be termed a "trust lifecycle": initial trust is fragile and easily damaged, but through successful repeated interactions, trust deepens into what some scholars have termed "thick trust"—a robust confidence in another's reliability that can withstand occasional setbacks [15].

High population mobility disrupts this trust lifecycle at multiple points. For newcomers, the absence of prior relationships means they must begin trust accumulation from a position of vulnerability, not knowing which potential partners are trustworthy. For established residents, the departure of trusted neighbors represents a loss of accumulated social capital that cannot be easily replaced. Moreover, the prospect of future mobility may reduce incentives for deep trust investment, as individuals anticipate that their trust partners may soon depart.

The decay of trust in mobile communities operates not only at the interpersonal level but also at the community level. Generalized trust—confidence that community norms and institutions will function fairly—requires evidence of consistent fair dealing over time. When community composition changes rapidly, individuals may observe fewer instances of fair dealing and more instances of defection or free-riding, leading to declining generalized trust that undermines mutual aid willingness.

### 3.3 Reciprocity Norm Erosion

The sustainability of mutual aid depends critically on the maintenance of reciprocity norms—shared expectations that assistance will be reciprocated and that free-riding will be sanctioned. These norms emerge through repeated interactions and are maintained through social learning and enforcement mechanisms.

In high-mobility communities, reciprocity norms face what might be termed the "newcomer problem." New arrivals may be unfamiliar with local reciprocity expectations, either because such expectations differ from those in their communities of origin or because they have not yet learned the local norms. This norm uncertainty can lead to inappropriate behavior—either excessive withdrawal from mutual aid due to fear of exploitation or excessive giving without expectation of reciprocity that undermines norm maintenance.

The problem is compounded by the fact that norm enforcement becomes more difficult in transient communities. Effective norm enforcement requires that community members can identify defectors and communicate this information to others. When community membership changes rapidly, defectors may depart before being identified or sanctioned, while potential defectors may calculate that they can exploit the community briefly before moving on.

### 3.4 Institutional Memory and Collective Action Capacity

Effective community mutual aid requires not only interpersonal relationships but also institutional memory—shared knowledge about community needs, resources, past experiences, and effective strategies. This institutional memory is embodied in community organizations, informal leadership structures, and collective narratives that guide community action.

High population mobility threatens institutional memory by continuously removing community members who possess this knowledge while introducing newcomers who lack it. The result can be a progressive erosion of community capacity for coordinated action, as the accumulated wisdom about how to respond to challenges is lost with each departure.

Research on community resilience has documented how this institutional memory loss can impair disaster response and recovery. Communities that have experienced major disasters often develop sophisticated response capacities based on their experiences, but these capacities can dissipate if key organizational members depart and knowledge is not transferred to newcomers [16].

## 4. Empirical Evidence on Mutual Aid in Mobile Communities

### 4.1 Disaster Contexts and Emergent Behavior

Research on disaster response provides important evidence regarding mutual aid sustainability in high-mobility contexts, as disasters often spark prosocial behavior among affected community members even when social capital is limited [17]. Studies of disaster response have documented that disasters frequently generate what Rodríguez, Trainor, and Quarantelli term "emergent organizations"—new collective structures that form spontaneously to address needs that established organizations cannot meet [18].

The emergence of mutual aid in disaster contexts suggests that social support capacity can be generated relatively rapidly under conditions of acute need, even in communities without strong pre-existing social capital. This finding has important implications for understanding mutual aid sustainability in mobile communities: it indicates that the complete absence of mutual aid capacity is unlikely even in high-turnover contexts, as disasters can catalyze solidarity among strangers brought together by shared adversity.

However, the disaster research also suggests important limitations on emergent mutual aid. Emergent organizations often face coordination challenges and may struggle to sustain their activities beyond the immediate emergency phase [19]. Moreover, the mutual aid that emerges in disaster contexts often depends on the presence of at least some individuals with organizational experience and community knowledge—resources that may be depleted in high-mobility contexts.

### 4.2 Migration and Refugee Resettlement

Research on migration and refugee resettlement provides additional evidence regarding mutual aid sustainability in mobile populations. Studies of refugee communities have documented the important role that mutual aid plays in supporting newcomers, from the formation of ethnic enclave economies to the provision of cultural orientation and practical assistance [20].

The research literature on refugee resettlement demonstrates that mutual aid networks can form rapidly among immigrant populations, often organized around ethnic, national, or religious identities that transcend geographic community boundaries. These networks provide crucial support for newcomers navigating unfamiliar institutional environments, from assistance with housing and employment to cultural mediation with schools, police, and government agencies [21].

However, the sustainability of these immigrant mutual aid networks faces particular challenges. As refugee communities become more established and integrated into host society institutions, the intensity of mutual aid often diminishes. Moreover, the mutual aid that does persist may be directed primarily toward newly arriving community members rather than toward the broader host community, limiting its contribution to community-wide social cohesion.

Research on emerging immigrant gateways—cities that have more recently become destinations for refugee resettlement—suggests that organizational infrastructure for mutual aid may be less developed in these contexts compared to traditional gateway cities with longer histories of immigrant settlement [22]. This finding indicates that the sustainability of mutual aid in mobile communities depends significantly on the presence of organizational infrastructure that can persist despite individual membership turnover.

### 4.3 COVID-19 Pandemic and Community Response

The COVID-19 pandemic provides a recent, large-scale natural experiment regarding mutual aid sustainability in diverse community contexts. Research on community responses to the pandemic has documented the emergence of extensive mutual aid networks even in contexts where such networks were not previously prominent [23].

Studies of COVID-19 mutual aid have identified several key patterns. First, the pandemic catalyzed the formation of new mutual aid initiatives in many communities, with individuals and organizations rapidly mobilizing to address needs that formal institutions could not meet. Second, existing organizations often pivoted their activities toward pandemic response, demonstrating the adaptability of established mutual aid infrastructure. Third, traditional sharing, borrowing, and other forms of mutual aid served as important coping strategies for households facing economic hardship, particularly in communities where formal financial services were inaccessible [24].

The pandemic experience suggests that mutual aid capacity can be rapidly generated or regenerated in response to acute need, even in communities that had not previously exhibited strong mutual aid traditions. However, the sustainability of these pandemic-era mutual aid networks beyond the immediate crisis remains an open question. Early evidence suggests that many emergent initiatives struggled to maintain momentum as the acute phase of the pandemic passed, indicating that the sustainability of mutual aid in mobile communities may depend on ongoing crisis conditions rather than stable community structures.

## 5. Adaptive Mechanisms and Institutional Responses

### 5.1 Hybrid Organizational Forms

The challenges facing traditional mutual aid in high-mobility contexts have prompted the emergence of hybrid organizational forms that combine elements of informal mutual aid with more formal organizational structures. These hybrid forms seek to capture the flexibility and responsiveness of informal mutual aid while providing the stability and coordination capacity that formal organizations can offer.

Research on community resilience has documented the importance of organizational support networks in facilitating mutual aid across disaster and recovery phases [9]. These networks often include both formal organizations (nonprofits, religious institutions, government agencies) and informal community groups, with the formal organizations providing coordination, resources, and institutional legitimacy while the informal groups provide local knowledge, flexibility, and grassroots energy.

The emergence of hybrid organizational forms represents what might be termed an "institutional fix" to the problem of mutual aid sustainability in mobile communities. By embedding mutual aid within formal organizational structures that can persist despite individual membership turnover, these hybrid forms seek to maintain continuity of mutual aid capacity across demographic changes.

### 5.2 Faith-Based Organizations and Bridging Networks

Faith-based organizations play a particularly important role in sustaining mutual aid in mobile communities, serving as what some scholars have termed "bridging institutions" that connect individuals across social divides [25]. Religious institutions often maintain membership rolls and organizational continuity even when individual members arrive and depart, providing a stable institutional framework for mutual aid activities.

Research on faith-based humanitarian organizations has documented their extensive networks for advocacy and service provision, ranging from local congregational support to global coordination through umbrella organizations such as Caritas Internationalis and World Vision [26]. These networks can mobilize resources across geographic scales, connecting community-level mutual aid initiatives with regional and international support systems.

The role of faith-based organizations in sustaining mutual aid in mobile communities extends beyond resource mobilization. Religious institutions also provide spaces for community building and social connection that can partially compensate for the weakening of neighborhood-based ties in high-turnover contexts. By bringing together individuals who share religious identity rather than geographic proximity, faith-based organizations create alternative bases for social capital formation that may be more resilient to population mobility.

### 5.3 Technology-Enabled Coordination

Digital technologies have increasingly been deployed to support mutual aid coordination, offering potential solutions to the coordination challenges that arise in high-mobility contexts. Online platforms and social media tools can facilitate rapid information sharing about needs and resources, connect individuals across geographic distances, and maintain organizational memory despite individual turnover.

Research on mutual aid during the COVID-19 pandemic documented extensive use of digital tools for coordination, from neighborhood Facebook groups to mutual aid databases mapping local resources [27]. These technology-enabled approaches offered advantages in terms of speed, reach, and flexibility, though they also raised concerns about digital divides that might exclude populations with limited technology access.

The potential of technology to sustain mutual aid in mobile communities extends beyond simple coordination. Digital platforms can also support the formation of what might be termed "persistent networks"—social connections that persist across geographic transitions. Individuals who form connections through online mutual aid platforms may maintain those connections even when they move to different communities, potentially sustaining mutual aid relationships across geographic distance.

### 5.4 Formal-Informal Integration

A promising approach to sustaining mutual aid in high-mobility contexts involves the integration of informal mutual aid with formal institutional arrangements. This integration seeks to leverage the strengths of both sectors: the flexibility and responsiveness of informal mutual aid, and the stability and resources of formal institutions.

Research on refugee resettlement has documented how formal resettlement organizations collaborate with informal community networks to provide comprehensive support to newcomers [21]. The formal organizations provide initial reception services, case management, and connection to public benefits, while informal networks provide cultural orientation, social support, and practical assistance that formal organizations cannot easily deliver.

This formal-informal integration model addresses several of the challenges facing traditional mutual aid in mobile communities. Formal organizations provide institutional continuity that persists despite individual turnover. They also provide resources that informal networks cannot generate independently. Meanwhile, informal networks provide the local knowledge, cultural competence, and flexibility that formal organizations often lack.

## 6. Discussion: Synthesis and Implications

### 6.1 The Limits of Traditional Mutual Aid

The evidence reviewed in this report suggests that traditional mutual aid networks face significant structural challenges in communities with high floating populations. The theoretical mechanisms through which mutual aid operates—trust accumulation, reciprocity norm development, network density formation—all require temporal continuity and repeated interaction that high population mobility disrupts. The empirical evidence from disaster contexts, migration studies, and the COVID-19 pandemic, while documenting substantial mutual aid activity, also reveals the contingent and often transient nature of mutual aid in high-mobility contexts.

These findings suggest that the question posed in this report—whether traditional mutual aid networks can sustain social support in communities with high floating populations—must be answered with qualified skepticism. Traditional mutual aid, as it has historically existed in stable communities characterized by long-term residence and face-to-face interaction, faces inherent sustainability challenges when confronted with rapid demographic turnover.

However, this qualified negative answer should not be interpreted as a complete否定 of mutual aid potential in mobile communities. Rather, it suggests that mutual aid in these contexts must take different forms than the traditional neighborhood-based mutual aid of stable communities.

### 6.2 Toward Adaptive Mutual Aid Systems

The evidence reviewed suggests that sustainable mutual aid in high-mobility contexts requires adaptation along several dimensions. First, mutual aid must be embedded in organizational structures that provide continuity despite individual turnover. This may involve formal organizations, faith-based institutions, or hybrid arrangements that combine elements of formal and informal structure.

Second, mutual aid in mobile communities must leverage bridging as well as bonding social capital. While bonding social capital among similar individuals remains important, the challenges of network fragmentation in high-turnover contexts require connections across community boundaries that can provide access to resources and support when local networks prove insufficient.

Third, technology must be strategically deployed to support mutual aid coordination and maintain connections across geographic transitions. Digital platforms can address some of the coordination challenges that arise in mobile communities, though attention must be paid to ensuring that technology-dependent approaches do not exclude populations with limited digital access.

Fourth, formal institutions must be integrated with informal mutual aid to provide resources, stability, and coordination capacity that informal networks cannot generate independently. This integration requires careful attention to power dynamics and the potential for formal institutions to co-opt or constrain grassroots mutual aid initiatives.

### 6.3 Policy and Practice Implications

For policymakers and practitioners concerned with community resilience in high-mobility contexts, this analysis suggests several implications. Investment in organizational infrastructure that can sustain mutual aid across demographic transitions is essential. This may involve supporting community organizations, faith-based institutions, and other entities that can maintain continuity of function despite individual membership changes.

Attention to bridging social capital is equally important. Programs that connect community members across geographic and social boundaries—through interfaith dialogue, cross-neighborhood initiatives, or technology-enabled platforms—can help compensate for the weakening of local networks in high-turnover contexts.

The integration of formal and informal support systems deserves particular attention. Rather than treating formal services and informal mutual aid as separate sectors, policymakers should seek ways to leverage the complementary strengths of each. This may involve formal organizations providing resources and coordination for grassroots initiatives, while informal networks provide local knowledge and cultural competence that formal organizations lack.

Finally, the analysis suggests the importance of managing expectations regarding mutual aid in mobile communities. Traditional neighborhood-based mutual aid, as it has existed in stable communities, may not be replicable in high-turnover contexts. However, alternative support arrangements—embedded in organizational structures, leveraging bridging connections, enabled by technology, and integrated with formal services—can provide meaningful social protection even in demographically unstable environments.

## 7. Conclusion

This report has examined the sustainability of traditional mutual aid networks in communities characterized by high floating populations, drawing on theoretical frameworks from social capital theory, resilience thinking, and empirical research from disaster sociology, migration studies, and community development.

The analysis leads to a nuanced conclusion. Traditional mutual aid networks, which depend on accumulated trust, established reciprocity norms, and dense interpersonal connections, face inherent structural challenges in high-mobility contexts. The temporal continuity and repeated interactions necessary for these networks to develop and function are disrupted by rapid population turnover, leading to network fragmentation, trust decay, and norm erosion.

However, the evidence also suggests that mutual aid functions can be sustained through adaptive mechanisms that address these structural challenges. Hybrid organizational forms, faith-based bridging institutions, technology-enabled coordination, and formal-informal integration all offer pathways for maintaining social support capacity in demographically unstable environments.

The question of whether traditional mutual aid networks can sustain social support in communities with high floating populations thus admits no simple affirmative or negative answer. Traditional mutual aid, in its conventional form, faces significant sustainability challenges in mobile contexts. But mutual aid itself—understood as the reciprocal exchange of support among community members—remains possible and important, provided it takes forms adapted to the distinctive challenges of high population mobility.

For scholars, policymakers, and practitioners, the implication is that efforts to promote community resilience in mobile populations must look beyond traditional neighborhood-based mutual aid toward more adaptive, institutionalized, and technologically enabled approaches. The challenge is not to preserve mutual aid in its traditional form but to develop support systems that can maintain their protective functions amid the demographic instability that characterizes many contemporary communities.
