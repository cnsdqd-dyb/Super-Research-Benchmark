# Balancing Benefit and Harm in Early Alzheimer's Diagnosis: Amyloid Biomarkers, Cognitive Tests, and the False Positive Dilemma

## Executive Summary

The pursuit of early Alzheimer's disease diagnosis represents one of the most consequential challenges in contemporary neurology and geriatric medicine. As therapeutic interventions increasingly target disease-modifying pathways, the imperative to identify Alzheimer's pathology before significant cognitive decline has intensified. However, this diagnostic ambition confronts a fundamental tension: the benefits of timely treatment must be weighed against the substantial harms associated with false positive results. This report examines how amyloid biomarkers and cognitive tests navigate this delicate balance, analyzing the evidentiary landscape from recent clinical studies, systematic reviews, and healthcare policy analyses. The evidence reveals that while early diagnosis offers profound advantages—including access to emerging disease-modifying therapies, enhanced care planning, and improved quality of life—the specter of false positives poses significant risks, from psychological distress to inappropriate treatment allocation. Achieving optimal diagnostic precision requires sophisticated integration of multiple biomarker modalities, careful patient selection, and robust counseling frameworks that acknowledge both the promise and limitations of current diagnostic capabilities.

## 1. Introduction: The Imperative of Early Detection

Alzheimer's disease has emerged as one of the most feared conditions of old age, affecting millions worldwide and imposing enormous burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems [1]. The disease's pathophysiology begins decades before clinical manifestations become apparent, with amyloid-beta accumulation, tau pathology, and neurodegeneration progressing silently within the brain [2]. This extended preclinical period presents both an opportunity and a challenge: an opportunity to intervene before irreversible neuronal damage occurs, and a challenge to accurately identify individuals who will progress to symptomatic disease versus those whose amyloid positivity may never manifest clinically.

The World Alzheimer Report 2011 established that individuals with dementia are entitled to a "timely" diagnosis at the moment when they and their families first express concerns and have a need for advice, treatment, or support [3]. This principle has gained renewed urgency as therapeutic landscapes evolve. The emergence of anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies has fundamentally altered the risk-benefit calculus of early diagnosis, creating situations where timely identification of amyloid pathology may determine eligibility for treatments that can modify disease trajectory [4]. However, these same treatments carry significant risks, including amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which makes accurate patient selection critically important [5].

The current diagnostic paradigm relies on two complementary approaches: cognitive assessments that evaluate functional impairment and biomarker tests that detect underlying pathology. Cognitive tests, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and comprehensive neuropsychological batteries, establish the presence and severity of cognitive decline [6]. Amyloid biomarkers—including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assays measuring Aβ42, Aβ40, total tau, and phosphorylated tau (p-tau181), as well as amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and emerging blood-based biomarkers—provide evidence of the characteristic neuropathology [7]. The integration of these approaches offers greater diagnostic accuracy than either alone, yet neither achieves perfect specificity, creating inherent uncertainty in individual patient management.

This report synthesizes current evidence on how early Alzheimer's diagnosis balances the competing demands of detecting true disease cases while minimizing false positive errors. We examine the benefits of timely intervention, the mechanisms and consequences of diagnostic error, and the clinical strategies that optimize this balance in practice.

## 2. The Case for Early Detection: Benefits and Opportunities

### 2.1 Access to Disease-Modifying Therapies

The most compelling argument for early diagnosis has transformed dramatically with the advent of amyloid-targeting therapeutics. Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, including lecanemab and donanemab, have demonstrated efficacy in slowing cognitive decline when administered to patients with early symptomatic Alzheimer's disease [8]. These agents work by clearing amyloid plaques from the brain, but their benefit diminishes as tau pathology spreads and neurodegeneration advances [9]. Consequently, identifying patients at the earliest stages of symptomatic disease—when they retain sufficient cognitive function but already demonstrate amyloid pathology—has become a clinical imperative.

The window of therapeutic opportunity is constrained by the natural history of Alzheimer's pathophysiology. AD-related changes in the brain may begin up to 25 years prior to diagnosis, but many patients are diagnosed only after advanced symptom onset [10]. Once neurons are lost to Alzheimer's disease, they cannot be regenerated, making early intervention essential for preserving cognitive function [11]. The amyloid cascade hypothesis, while representing only one component of the complex AD disease process, provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how early amyloid accumulation triggers downstream molecular pathways, including tau pathology, that ultimately cause neurodegeneration [12].

### 2.2 Quality of Life and Care Planning

Beyond access to pharmacological interventions, early diagnosis offers substantial benefits for patients and families navigating the emotional and practical dimensions of Alzheimer's disease. A timely diagnosis provides an explanation for the symptoms and signs patients are experiencing, ending the uncertainty and suspicion that often accompanies progressive cognitive decline [13]. This diagnostic clarity can reduce anxiety and uncertainty, allowing individuals and their families to understand and contextualize their experiences within a coherent medical framework.

Early diagnosis enables patients to take control of their condition, accessing appropriate services and support that can help them live independently in their own homes for longer periods [14]. Quality of life in the early phases of illness can be maintained for several years with appropriate support, and forewarned individuals can plan ahead while they still have the capacity to participate in legal, financial, and future care decisions [15]. This proactive approach to care planning represents a fundamental shift from reactive management of advanced disease to anticipatory support that preserves autonomy and dignity.

The Alzheimer's Association emphasizes that early detection is essential to providing access to information, care, and support to people living with Alzheimer's and other dementias [16]. Many individuals living with Alzheimer's are never diagnosed, and even among those who are, a significant proportion remain unaware of their diagnosis [17]. This underdiagnosis represents a failure to deliver care that could meaningfully improve outcomes and quality of life.

### 2.3 Economic and Societal Benefits

The economic argument for early detection complements the clinical rationale. The World Alzheimer Report 2011 noted that costs of early detection are more than likely offset by projected future savings [18]. While specific cost-effectiveness analyses vary based on healthcare system context and treatment availability, the general principle holds that identifying Alzheimer's disease early allows for more efficient resource allocation, avoiding the substantial costs associated with crisis management of advanced disease, unnecessary hospitalizations, and delayed interventions for comorbid conditions.

Earlier diagnosis also has the potential to change the way societies view and approach Alzheimer's disease and other dementias [19]. By demonstrating that meaningful intervention is possible, early detection programs can help counter the stigma and nihilism that historically surrounded dementia diagnosis. The false belief that nothing can be done for people with dementia remains prevalent even in well-resourced, high-income countries, perpetuating underdiagnosis and limiting access to available support services [20].

## 3. Diagnostic Tools: Amyloid Biomarkers and Cognitive Assessment

### 3.1 Cognitive Testing Paradigms

Cognitive assessment forms the foundation of Alzheimer's disease diagnosis, establishing the presence, severity, and pattern of cognitive impairment that characterizes the clinical syndrome. Standardized tests evaluate multiple cognitive domains, including memory, executive function, language, and visuospatial abilities, allowing clinicians to identify the characteristic amnestic presentation of typical Alzheimer's while differentiating it from other neurocognitive disorders [21].

The KAER Approach to Cognitive Impairment toolkit, developed by the Gerontological Society of America, provides a framework for systematic cognitive assessment in clinical practice [22]. This approach emphasizes the importance of recognizing early symptoms, conducting appropriate assessments, and referring patients for comprehensive evaluation when cognitive impairment is suspected. The Medicare Annual Wellness Visit includes cognitive assessment provisions, and reimbursement is available for comprehensive care planning through CPT® code 99483, reflecting the recognized importance of cognitive evaluation in routine geriatric care [23].

However, cognitive testing alone cannot establish the underlying etiology of cognitive impairment. The signs of Alzheimer's overlap with a number of other conditions, many of which may be treatable and even possibly reversible [24]. Normal-pressure hydrocephalus, vitamin deficiencies, medication effects, depression, and other conditions can produce cognitive profiles that mimic Alzheimer's, making differential diagnosis challenging based on cognitive assessment alone [25]. This limitation underscores the need for biomarker confirmation when specific treatment decisions depend on accurate etiological attribution.

### 3.2 Amyloid Biomarker Modalities

Amyloid biomarkers have become central to the diagnostic assessment of people with Alzheimer's symptoms, providing direct evidence of the characteristic neuropathology [26]. The three primary modalities—CSF analysis, amyloid PET imaging, and blood-based biomarkers—each offer distinct advantages and limitations that influence their clinical application.

Cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, including measurements of Aβ42, Aβ40, total tau, and p-tau181, reflect the biochemical changes in the central nervous system that accompany amyloid deposition [27]. The Aβ42/40 ratio has emerged as a particularly useful parameter, correcting for individual variations in total Aβ production and improving diagnostic accuracy [28]. CSF testing is less expensive than PET imaging and widely available, though lumbar puncture remains invasive and may be poorly tolerated by some patients.

Amyloid PET imaging provides direct visualization of amyloid plaque distribution in the brain, offering superior spatial resolution and the ability to quantify regional burden [29]. The centiloid scale has been developed to standardize PET imaging results, facilitating comparison across studies and tracers by transforming PET measures into a common metric [30]. However, PET imaging is expensive and of limited availability in many healthcare systems, constraining its widespread clinical application [31].

Blood-based biomarkers represent the most recent advancement in amyloid detection, offering the potential for minimally invasive, widely accessible testing [32]. Plasma p-tau has demonstrated analytical validity and initial evidence of clinical validity, while amyloid beta biomarkers continue to mature toward clinical application [33]. These tests could revolutionize early detection by enabling screening of large populations, though validation against established CSF and PET methods remains ongoing.

### 3.3 Diagnostic Performance Characteristics

Understanding the statistical properties of diagnostic tests is essential for interpreting their results and managing patient expectations. Sensitivity represents the true positive rate—the proportion of individuals with Alzheimer's disease who are correctly identified as positive [34]. High sensitivity ensures that most true cases are detected, reducing the likelihood of false negatives that would deny patients access to appropriate treatment and care.

Specificity represents the true negative rate—the proportion of healthy individuals correctly identified as negative [35]. High specificity minimizes false positives, which is important for avoiding undue anxiety, unnecessary interventions, and the psychological harm of being given a dire prognosis that proves incorrect. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate at various classification thresholds, with the area under the curve (AUC) providing a single-value measure of discriminatory power [36].

In practice, diagnostic tests rarely achieve both perfect sensitivity and perfect specificity. A study examining the diagnosis of over 900 patients found that one in four patients had an Alzheimer's misdiagnosis, with an even split between false positives and false negatives [37]. This error rate has significant clinical implications, as misdiagnosis can lead to undue stress for those incorrectly diagnosed and delays in treatment for those whose true diagnosis is missed [38].

## 4. The Harm of False Positives: Mechanisms and Consequences

### 4.1 Psychological and Emotional Impact

The psychological harm of a false positive Alzheimer's diagnosis extends far beyond the moment of disclosure. A diagnosis is often experienced with shock, feelings of disbelief, anger, fear, hopelessness, despair, and grief [39]. When this diagnosis proves incorrect, patients must process not only the emotional burden of the erroneous label but also the complex emotions associated with having their fears apparently allayed. The experience can erode trust in medical systems and create anxiety about future health concerns that may persist long after the error is recognized.

Fear of the stigma surrounding Alzheimer's disease may prevent people from seeking medical treatment, receiving an early diagnosis, living the best quality of life possible, making plans for their future, and benefiting from available treatments and support systems [40]. This stigma operates even when the diagnosis is accurate, but it is amplified when applied to individuals who do not have the disease. The diagnostic label carries social weight that affects how patients are perceived by healthcare providers, family members, and society at large.

The psychological harm is compounded by the repeated assessments that often accompany cognitive concerns. Patients may find these assessments humiliating, particularly when they must repeatedly demonstrate cognitive competence to confirm or refute an initial diagnosis [41]. Each assessment session represents a potential reminder of the uncertainty and fear that prompted the evaluation, reinforcing anxiety and potentially contributing to iatrogenic cognitive concern in susceptible individuals.

### 4.2 Clinical Consequences of Misattribution

Beyond psychological harm, false positive diagnoses can lead to inappropriate medical management that directly harms patients. Consider an elderly patient with objective memory impairment who also has depression, sleep problems, or uses medications that interfere with memory [42]. A positive amyloid test might lead the physician to mistakenly attribute the patient's memory impairment to Alzheimer's disease, minimizing attention to potentially treatable causes of cognitive dysfunction.

This diagnostic attribution has profound consequences for clinical management. Once a patient carries an Alzheimer's diagnosis, care providers tend not to expect, and therefore tend not to strive for, full recovery of cognitive abilities [43]. The diagnostic label creates a self-fulfilling prophecy in which treatable conditions are overlooked because the cognitive impairment is attributed to irreversible neurodegeneration. Depression-related cognitive impairment, medication effects, sleep disorders, and metabolic abnormalities all represent potentially reversible causes of cognitive decline that may be missed when amyloid positivity prematurely closes the diagnostic inquiry.

The high prevalence of amyloid pathology in cognitively normal older adults complicates this picture further. Because amyloid pathology may be present for a decade or more prior to the onset of clinical symptoms, a positive amyloid test in the absence of cognitive injury does not inform prognosis and may lead to an erroneous diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease [44]. The distinction between amyloid positivity (which indicates pathology) and Alzheimer's disease (which requires both pathology and clinical symptoms) is critical, yet it can be lost in clinical communication and patient understanding.

### 4.3 Treatment-Related Harms

The stakes of accurate diagnosis have escalated dramatically with the availability of anti-amyloid therapies. These treatments carry significant risks, including amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), characterized as ARIA with edema (ARIA-E) and ARIA with hemosiderin deposition (ARIA-H) [45]. The incidence and timing of ARIA vary among treatments, but ARIA usually occurs early in treatment and, while usually asymptomatic, can rarely cause serious and life-threatening events. Serious intracerebral hemorrhages greater than 1 cm, some of which have been fatal, have been observed in patients treated with this class of medications [46].

Administering these treatments to patients who do not have Alzheimer's disease—either because their cognitive impairment has another cause or because they would never progress to symptomatic disease despite amyloid positivity—exposes them to significant risks without potential benefit. This calculus becomes particularly concerning when considering that clinicians might be inclined to delay prescription until overt symptoms of cognitive decline specific to Alzheimer's are evident, using these symptoms as a trigger for initiating treatment [47]. This cautious approach carries the risk of patients' conditions advancing beyond the stage where they would be eligible for effective treatment, yet the alternative—treating patients who may not need it—carries its own substantial harms.

## 5. Balancing Benefits and Harms: The Diagnostic Challenge

### 5.1 Pre-Test Probability and Risk Stratification

The key to balancing the benefits of early detection against the harms of false positives lies in appropriate patient selection and test interpretation. The positive predictive value of any diagnostic test depends not only on its sensitivity and specificity but also on the prevalence of disease in the tested population [48]. Testing individuals at low pre-test probability of Alzheimer's disease—even with high-specificity tests—will generate substantial numbers of false positives that could overwhelm confirmatory testing capacity and cause unnecessary patient distress.

Clinical practice and healthcare systems' readiness to detect, diagnose, and treat the disease effectively are still lagging behind the scientific understanding of Alzheimer's pathophysiology [49]. The use of biomarkers, which are central to diagnostic assessment for people with AD symptoms, as well as relevant diagnostic facilities, are under-utilized [50]. PET imaging is expensive and of limited availability, and CSF sampling may be considered invasive, creating barriers to confirmatory testing that compound the harms of initial false positive results.

A modeling study using a sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 85% found that 525 patients would receive a positive result from a triaging blood-based biomarker test, and of the 200 individuals with access to confirmatory testing, 86% would have amyloid pathology [51]. However, 325 patients with a positive triaging result would not have access to confirmatory testing, and critically, only 46 would have a false-positive test result. The most clinically concerning issue with triaging tests is the number of individuals with a false-positive result and no ability to obtain follow-up testing—these individuals might accept the false-positive triaging result as final, with adverse consequences for their care [52].

### 5.2 Combining Biomarker Modalities

One promising approach to reducing false positive rates involves combining multiple biomarker modalities to achieve greater diagnostic specificity. Research has demonstrated that combining blood-based biomarkers with amyloid status can dramatically reduce false positive rates compared to using amyloid tests alone [53]. When participants positive for both amyloid and neurodegeneration biomarkers were considered as having Alzheimer's disease, 100% specificity and 39.7% sensitivity were achieved, resulting in 0% false positives [54].

The B-HEALED test, which integrates multiple blood-based biomarkers, predicted Alzheimer's from non-Alzheimer's patients with 92.5% specificity and 64.1% sensitivity, resulting in a 7.3% false-positive rate [55]. This represented a significant improvement over amyloid deposit-based tests, reducing the false positive rate by nearly 50% [56]. The comparative analysis demonstrates the advantage of combining biomarker modalities to minimize false positive rates, as opposed to relying on single tests.

These findings suggest that clinical algorithms incorporating both amyloid and neurodegeneration biomarkers may offer optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity. The AT(N) framework—classifying patients based on Amyloid, Tau, and Neurodegeneration status—provides a systematic approach to biomarker integration that can guide both diagnosis and treatment decisions [57].

### 5.3 The Role of Clinical Context and Expertise

Diagnosing Alzheimer's disease is a complex and tricky process that relies heavily on the expertise of the patient's healthcare provider [58]. Globally, almost 62% of healthcare providers think that dementia is part of normal aging, and many general practitioners do not receive enough training about Alzheimer's and other dementias to facilitate diagnosis, identify early symptoms, and differentiate these symptoms from other disorders [59]. Consequently, they have difficulty recognizing the early symptoms of dementia or tend to overlook their importance, and they lack confidence in their ability to detect dementia, particularly in its early stages [60].

The European Brain Council's White Paper on Alzheimer's detection and diagnosis emphasizes the need to address healthcare practitioners' misconceptions about Alzheimer's and improve their ability to recognize early symptoms [61]. Training programs, clinical decision support tools, and specialist referral networks all contribute to more accurate diagnosis. However, even experienced clinicians face challenges in distinguishing Alzheimer's from other conditions that cause cognitive impairment, particularly in the early stages when symptoms are subtle and overlapping.

Before undergoing Alzheimer's biomarker testing, patients and their caregivers should be counselled about potential risks and benefits, the results of which could affect their work, driver licensing, and insurance, and could also cause stigmatization [62]. Measures must be taken to mitigate these issues and thereby maximize the potential of biomarker tests for improving the diagnosis of early symptomatic Alzheimer's [63].

## 6. Clinical Implications and Recommendations

### 6.1 Indications for Biomarker Testing

Given the current state of knowledge, amyloid testing is not recommended in the diagnostic work-up of patients lacking evidence of neurodegeneration [64]. However, if amyloid-modifying therapies become available, amyloid biomarkers may become essential in risk-benefit determination [65]. The clinical scenarios where amyloid testing may be considered include patients with mild cognitive impairment who are candidates for disease-modifying treatment, patients with atypical clinical presentations where Alzheimer's is in the differential diagnosis, and patients enrolled in clinical trials requiring biomarker confirmation.

For patients with clear-cut Alzheimer's dementia clinical syndrome and no competing diagnoses, biomarker confirmation may not change management and exposes patients to the risks of false positive results without clear benefit [66]. Conversely, for patients with mild cognitive impairment or subjective cognitive concerns, biomarker status can inform prognosis and treatment eligibility, justifying the risks of testing.

### 6.2 Interpretive Framework and Counseling

When biomarker testing is performed, results must be interpreted within the appropriate clinical context, estimating the likelihood of amyloid pathology based on factors such as age and severity of symptoms, while keeping in mind that Alzheimer's biomarkers become abnormal many years before the onset of cognitive impairment [67]. Providers need to know the level of accuracy of the specific tests they are ordering and communicate this information clearly to patients.

The counseling process should include discussion of the potential benefits of knowing one's amyloid status (access to treatments, ability to plan, reduction of uncertainty) alongside the potential harms (false positive results, psychological distress, insurance and employment implications, stigmatization) [68]. Patients should understand that a positive biomarker result indicates the presence of amyloid pathology but does not definitively predict the development of symptomatic Alzheimer's disease, particularly in asymptomatic individuals.

### 6.3 Healthcare System Preparedness

The changing clinical landscape, driven by increased longevity, aging populations, and new therapeutic options, requires healthcare systems to prepare for expanded biomarker testing and early diagnosis [69]. This preparation includes ensuring access to confirmatory testing for patients with positive screening results, developing clinical pathways for managing early-stage Alzheimer's disease, training healthcare providers in biomarker interpretation and counseling, and establishing infrastructure for monitoring patients on disease-modifying therapies.

The European Brain Council's recommendations call on policymakers to drive national, EU, and international policies that can improve the lives of people living with Alzheimer's and ensure health systems are better prepared to support early detection and diagnosis now and when future innovative detection and diagnostic tools and therapies become available [70]. Cooperation and knowledge-sharing are key to addressing the plurality of skills needed to detect and diagnose Alzheimer's and learning from evidence-based data to improve healthcare systems [71].

## 7. Conclusion

The balance between benefit and harm in early Alzheimer's diagnosis represents a dynamic challenge that evolves with advances in diagnostic technology and therapeutic options. Amyloid biomarkers and cognitive tests offer unprecedented ability to identify Alzheimer's pathology before significant cognitive decline, creating opportunities for early intervention that can preserve function and quality of life. The emergence of anti-amyloid therapies has transformed early diagnosis from an academic exercise into a clinical necessity, as timely identification of amyloid-positive patients determines eligibility for disease-modifying treatment.

However, this imperative must be tempered by recognition of the substantial harms associated with false positive results. Psychological distress, inappropriate treatment allocation, and the closure of diagnostic inquiry that prevents identification of treatable alternative conditions all represent significant risks that must be managed through careful patient selection, test interpretation, and counseling. The one-in-four misdiagnosis rate documented in clinical studies underscores the limitations of current diagnostic approaches and the need for continued refinement.

The path forward requires integration of multiple biomarker modalities to achieve optimal diagnostic accuracy, with particular attention to combining amyloid and neurodegeneration markers to minimize false positive rates. Healthcare systems must develop the infrastructure and expertise necessary to support expanded biomarker testing while ensuring that patients receive appropriate counseling about the implications of testing. Provider education must address misconceptions about Alzheimer's and build confidence in early detection, while also fostering appropriate skepticism about test results that may not reflect the patient's true clinical status.

Ultimately, the goal of early Alzheimer's diagnosis is not simply to identify pathology but to improve outcomes for patients and families. This requires balancing the undoubted benefits of timely detection against the very real harms of false positives, recognizing that perfect diagnostic accuracy remains elusive. As the field advances, the integration of new biomarker technologies, refined clinical algorithms, and patient-centered counseling approaches will continue to shift this balance, offering hope for a future where early diagnosis reliably delivers benefit while minimizing harm.
