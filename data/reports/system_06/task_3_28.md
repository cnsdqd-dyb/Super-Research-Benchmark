# Institutional Guarantees for Long-Term Value Chains in Creative Industries: Balancing Economic Benefits with Cultural Heritage

## A Research Report by the Office of the Senior Technical Analyst

---

## Executive Summary

The intersection of creative industries and cultural heritage presents one of the most complex policy challenges facing governments and international organizations in the contemporary era. As creative economies expand globally, the imperative to harness cultural assets for economic development must be carefully balanced against the imperative to preserve and protect the cultural heritage that gives these industries their distinctive value. This report examines the institutional guarantees—comprising international legal frameworks, national policies, public-private partnership mechanisms, and indigenous rights protections—that collectively ensure long-term value chains in creative industries maintain this critical balance.

The research reveals that effective institutional guarantees operate across multiple levels simultaneously. At the international level, instruments including the WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty, the Beijing Treaty on Audiovisual Performances, and various UNESCO conventions establish foundational protections for traditional cultural expressions while creating frameworks for their commercial exploitation [1]. At the national and local levels, adaptive governance structures, sui generis intellectual property laws, and public-private partnership models provide operational mechanisms for translating these international standards into sustainable value chains [2]. The evidence suggests that successful long-term value chains require not merely legal protections but also institutional arrangements that ensure meaningful community participation, equitable benefit-sharing, and the integration of cultural heritage considerations into broader economic development strategies.

## 1. Introduction: The Challenge of Balancing Value Creation and Heritage Preservation

### 1.1 The Emergence of Creative Industries as Economic and Cultural Forces

The creative and cultural industries have undergone a remarkable transformation in their perceived role within national and regional economies over the past several decades. What was once viewed primarily as a domain of artistic production and cultural preservation has emerged as a significant contributor to economic development, employment generation, and international competitiveness. According to the United Nations Industrial Development Organization, the creative sector's contribution to GDP is positively related to innovation, competitiveness, international property rights protection, and institutional quality [3]. This economic significance has prompted policymakers worldwide to incorporate creative industries into their development agendas, recognizing both their potential for economic growth and their capacity to generate cultural value that serves broader societal objectives.

The conceptual evolution of creative industries reflects this dual economic and cultural character. The notion of "cultural industries" was introduced in the 1970s by UNESCO, marking the initial step in connecting cultural production with economic frameworks and establishing new relationships between culture, technology, industry, and markets [4]. Subsequently, the term "creative industries" emerged in the 1990s, broadening the concept beyond traditional cultural sectors to include more commercially oriented activities driven by creativity, skills, and intellectual property. While cultural industries focus primarily on cultural value and heritage preservation, creative industries prioritize economic value and innovation—though increasingly these two domains are recognized as interconnected and mutually reinforcing [4].

### 1.2 The Tension Between Economic Exploitation and Cultural Integrity

The integration of cultural heritage into commercial value chains creates inherent tensions that require careful institutional management. Traditional cultural expressions (TCEs), including music, dance, handicrafts, and oral traditions, face significant risks when entering commercial markets. Indigenous and traditional communities frequently cite examples of cultural appropriation and commercial exploitation: traditional artistic designs copied onto mass-produced textiles and greeting cards, indigenous music fused with contemporary genres and sold as "world music" without attribution or compensation, and traditional knowledge patented by outside parties without community consent [5]. These concerns have prompted decades of international negotiation and policy development aimed at creating mechanisms that enable economic utilization of cultural heritage while protecting its integrity and ensuring equitable benefit-sharing with source communities.

The challenge is compounded by the fundamental characteristics of cultural heritage, which often exists in collective forms that resist individual ownership paradigms embedded in conventional intellectual property systems. Traditional knowledge and cultural expressions are frequently inseparable from spiritual, cultural, social, and economic aspects of community life, and the notion of collective ownership challenges the individualist assumptions underlying most intellectual property regimes [6]. As the World Intellectual Property Organization has acknowledged, the intellectual property system was designed to recognize, protect, and reward new and improved creations and innovations—by definition, this would seem to exclude traditional cultural expressions that have evolved over generations through collective human creativity [5].

### 1.3 Research Objectives and Scope

This report addresses the central question: What institutional guarantees ensure that long-term value chains in creative industries effectively balance economic benefits with cultural heritage preservation? The analysis draws upon international legal instruments, national policy frameworks, public-private partnership models, and indigenous rights mechanisms to identify the key institutional components that enable this balance. The research scope encompasses both tangible cultural heritage (historic sites, monuments, artifacts) and intangible cultural heritage (traditional practices, knowledge systems, artistic expressions), recognizing that creative industry value chains frequently draw upon both categories.

## 2. International Legal and Institutional Frameworks

### 2.1 The Architecture of International Protection

The international framework for protecting cultural heritage while enabling its commercial utilization has developed through multiple institutional channels, creating a complex architecture of overlapping and complementary protections. The World Intellectual Property Organization, UNESCO, the International Labour Organization, and more recently the Convention on Biological Diversity have all contributed to this framework, each approaching the protection of traditional knowledge and cultural expressions from distinct institutional perspectives and policy priorities.

The development of this international framework can be traced back to the 1950s, when discussions about the protection of cultural heritage emerged at the level of individual states [7]. At that time, elements that would later be classified as traditional cultural expressions were referred to as "expressions of folklore," were treated as state cultural property, and fell under state jurisdiction. The 1967 establishment of WIPO introduced an intellectual property approach to cultural heritage protection, though the organization's mission to apply intellectual property to "promote innovation and creativity for the economic, social and cultural development of all countries" initially generated developments primarily focused on using culture for monetary and commercial profits [7].

### 2.2 Key International Instruments

Several international instruments provide foundational protections that shape how cultural heritage enters creative industry value chains. The International Labour Organization Convention No. 169 on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples in Independent Countries, adopted in 1989, represents a particularly significant development in recognizing the relationship between cultural preservation and economic autonomy. Article 23 of this Convention explicitly recognizes handicrafts, rural and community-based production, and traditional activities of indigenous peoples as important factors in the preservation of their culture and economic autonomy, while requiring participating state governments to guarantee the promotion and development of such activities [1].

The Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions, adopted by UNESCO in 2005, established important principles for balancing cultural diversity protection with the economic dimensions of cultural production [1]. This Convention recognizes that cultural diversity is strengthened when protected by favorable conditions for the creation, production, distribution, dissemination, access, and enjoyment of diverse cultural expressions. Simultaneously, it affirms the sovereign right of states to formulate and implement cultural policies and to adopt measures aimed at protecting and promoting the diversity of cultural expressions.

The International Convention for the Safeguarding of Intangible Cultural Heritage, also adopted by UNESCO in 2003, provides a framework for identifying, safeguarding, and promoting intangible cultural heritage while respecting the wishes of communities, groups, and individuals that create, maintain, and transmit such heritage [1]. The Convention emphasizes the importance of community participation in all phases of safeguarding activities and recognizes that intangible cultural heritage is constantly recreated by communities in response to their environment and history.

### 2.3 Intellectual Property Treaties and Performers' Rights

The intellectual property dimension of cultural heritage protection has been addressed through specific treaties that extend protection to performances of traditional cultural expressions. The WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty, adopted in 1996, and the Beijing Treaty on Audiovisual Performances, adopted in 2012, provide protection for performances of TCEs, including music, dance, and drama [1]. These instruments recognize that performers should be able to prevent the unauthorized fixation of their live performances and the broadcasting and communication to the public of such fixations, while also securing equitable remuneration for the commercial use of their performances.

The WIPO Intergovernmental Committee on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources, Traditional Knowledge and Folklore (IGC), established around 2000, has been the primary forum for ongoing negotiations regarding the protection of traditional knowledge and traditional cultural expressions [7]. The Committee has focused on developing an intellectual property-based mechanism for protecting indigenous creations, both tangible and intangible, and particularly on intellectual property deemed commercially viable. While these negotiations have been protracted, they have contributed to growing international consensus on the need for specialized protection mechanisms that address the distinctive characteristics of traditional cultural expressions.

### 2.4 Indigenous Peoples' Rights Framework

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted in 2007, establishes a comprehensive framework for indigenous peoples' rights over their cultural heritage. Article 31 of the Declaration is particularly significant for creative industry value chains, recognizing that indigenous peoples have the right to maintain, control, protect, and develop their cultural heritage, traditional knowledge, and traditional cultural expressions, as well as the manifestations of their sciences, technologies, and cultures [8]. The Declaration further establishes that indigenous peoples have the right to maintain, control, protect, and develop their intellectual property over such cultural heritage, and that states shall take effective measures, in conjunction with indigenous peoples, to recognize and protect the exercise of these rights.

This framework has important implications for how creative industry value chains engage with indigenous cultural heritage. It establishes the principle of prior informed consent for the use of traditional knowledge and cultural expressions, requires benefit-sharing arrangements that return economic value to source communities, and recognizes the continuing rights of indigenous peoples to control how their cultural heritage is utilized in commercial contexts. While the Declaration is not legally binding in the same manner as treaties, it represents an international consensus that increasingly shapes national legislation and corporate practices.

## 3. Mechanisms for Balancing Economic and Cultural Values

### 3.1 Sui Generis Intellectual Property Systems

The inadequacy of conventional intellectual property regimes for protecting traditional cultural expressions has prompted the development of sui generis systems specifically designed to address the distinctive characteristics of such expressions. These specialized legal frameworks recognize that traditional cultural expressions often lack identifiable individual authors, may have been in existence for centuries, and are collectively owned and maintained by communities over time. Sui generis systems typically provide for perpetual protection (or protection for extended terms), collective ownership, and moral rights that ensure attribution and prevent derogatory treatment [5].

The WIPO-UNESCO Model Provisions for the Protection of Expressions of Folklore, developed in 1982, provided an early framework for national sui generis legislation [9]. These Model Provisions recognized that expressions of folklore belong to the heritage of a nation and should be protected against illicit exploitation and prejudicial actions. While the Model Provisions were not binding, they influenced national legislation in numerous countries and established principles that continue to inform international discussions. The Model Provisions distinguished between uses of folklore that require authorization (such as commercial exploitation) and uses that are permitted without authorization (such as research, teaching, and certain non-commercial uses).

The ongoing work of WIPO's IGC to develop an international legal instrument on traditional knowledge and traditional cultural expressions represents the most significant current effort to establish harmonized sui generis protections at the international level [5]. While negotiations have proven challenging due to differing national interests and perspectives on the appropriate scope and nature of protection, there is growing consensus on the need for mechanisms that protect against misappropriation and ensure equitable benefit-sharing while permitting uses that support cultural continuity and non-commercial utilization.

### 3.2 Benefit-Sharing and Equitable Remuneration

The principle of equitable benefit-sharing has emerged as a central mechanism for balancing economic utilization of cultural heritage with the interests of source communities. This principle, which has its roots in the Convention on Biological Diversity and its Nagoya Protocol, recognizes that those who contribute to the development and maintenance of traditional knowledge and cultural expressions should share in the benefits derived from their commercial utilization [1]. Benefit-sharing arrangements can take various forms, including monetary payments (such as royalties, licensing fees, and percentage-based compensation), non-monetary benefits (such as capacity building, technology transfer, and community development support), and recognition and attribution.

The practical implementation of benefit-sharing mechanisms in creative industry value chains presents significant challenges. Creative products often incorporate multiple cultural inputs, making it difficult to identify and compensate all relevant source communities. The informal and collective nature of much traditional cultural production complicates the establishment of clear ownership rights and compensation structures. Furthermore, the globalized nature of creative industries means that cultural inputs may cross multiple jurisdictions with different legal frameworks and enforcement mechanisms. Despite these challenges, benefit-sharing principles are increasingly incorporated into national legislation, international agreements, and corporate social responsibility programs.

### 3.3 Cultural Heritage Management and Integration into Economic Development

Effective institutional guarantees require not only legal protections but also institutional arrangements that integrate cultural heritage considerations into broader economic development planning. Cultural heritage management at the macro-economic level encompasses strategies, action plans, priority intervention lists, inter-governmental committees, and cross-sectoral task forces [10]. These mechanisms ensure that cultural heritage is not treated as a separate domain but is integrated into sectoral policies of significance for its protection and preservation, including zoning, spatial planning, fiscal policy, and economic development planning.

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on the Value of Cultural Heritage for Society, adopted in 2005, made an important contribution to this integration by recognizing heritage as a factor of sustainable economic development and emphasizing the necessity of respecting its specific character [10]. The Convention encouraged the inclusion of cultural heritage in sustainable development processes, the development of cross-sectoral strategies on heritage protection, and the balanced and sustainable use of cultural heritage for tourism development. It also promoted improved cooperation between the private, civil, and public sectors in heritage management.

Research on the relationship between cultural heritage protection and economic development has provided empirical support for these integrated approaches. Studies examining Chinese provincial data from 2010 to 2021 found that heritage protection enhances economic development both directly and indirectly, with tourism mediating approximately 23% of the total effect [11]. The research revealed that human capital strengthens this relationship, as regions with higher education levels show greater heritage-driven development. These findings suggest that institutional guarantees for balancing economic and cultural values are most effective when combined with investments in education and capacity building that enable communities to fully participate in and benefit from creative industry development.

## 4. Public-Private Partnerships and Governance Models

### 4.1 The Rationale for Multi-Stakeholder Approaches

Public cultural institutions including museums, creative industry hubs, and audiovisual production centers are recognized as cornerstones of heritage preservation and creativity, yet they often face structural financial challenges that threaten their long-term sustainability [2]. Many such venues face structural deficits, meaning their operating costs consistently outpace revenues from admissions, programming, and other sources. This financial vulnerability has prompted exploration of alternative governance and funding models that can ensure institutional sustainability while maintaining cultural missions.

Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) have emerged as an innovative solution that taps private capital and expertise to support cultural infrastructure while preserving public interest in heritage preservation and access [2]. The PPP model is based on a partnership ethos that leverages the strengths of each sector: the public sector provides initial support through capital grants, guarantees, and policy incentives while protecting the public interest through requirements for access and preservation; the private or third sector partner brings efficiency, innovation, and additional resources. When structured effectively, PPPs can deliver world-class facilities without endemic deficits, as revenues and responsibilities are shared between partners.

### 4.2 PPP Models for Cultural Heritage and Creative Industries

The application of PPP models to cultural heritage projects has taken various forms, reflecting the diverse characteristics of cultural assets and the specific policy objectives of different jurisdictions. Models range from concessions and management contracts to nonprofit operating agreements and trust funds [2]. Each model involves different allocations of risk, responsibility, and reward between public and private partners, and the appropriate model depends on factors including the nature of the cultural asset, market conditions, and policy priorities.

The Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) model has been applied in several notable cultural infrastructure projects. The Taiwan Museum of Marine Biology & Aquarium, opened in 2000, was one of the first cases where a public museum's operations were outsourced to a private company under a BOT arrangement [2]. The government retained ownership but received a fixed royalty and a share of operating profits from the private operator. This model ensured that the museum would not run at a loss while benefiting from private sector management expertise and efficiency. Similar models have been applied to heritage building restoration and adaptive reuse, where private operators develop and manage heritage properties for cultural and commercial purposes.

The Pittsburgh Cultural Trust represents a different approach to cultural PPPs, emphasizing cross-sector investment and long-term collaboration to revitalize urban areas through arts and cultural development [12]. The Cultural District now attracts over 2 million visitors annually, hosts over 1,500 events, and has generated an estimated economic impact of $303 million. This model demonstrates how arts-focused PPPs can drive economic growth, cultural tourism, and creative placemaking at scale while maintaining focus on cultural mission.

### 4.3 The 5P Model: Integrating People and Policy

Recent scholarship has advanced the concept of Public-Private-People-Policy Partnerships (5P model) as an evolution of traditional PPP approaches that addresses limitations in conventional public-private arrangements [13]. The 5P model brings citizens (People) into partnerships alongside public and private actors and strategic local policymakers (Policy). This approach recognizes that cultural heritage projects often involve multiple stakeholders beyond the immediate public and private partners, including community organizations, nonprofit cultural institutions, and the broader public that has interests in heritage preservation and access.

The 5P model addresses several limitations of traditional PPPs in the cultural heritage context. Cultural heritage is fundamentally a public good that serves broad societal interests beyond those of immediate project partners. The participation of community members and civil society organizations can help ensure that projects reflect local values, serve community needs, and maintain cultural authenticity. The model also recognizes that cultural projects can be politically sensitive, requiring sustained political will and community support for long-term success [2]. By incorporating people and policy dimensions explicitly into partnership structures, the 5P model provides a more comprehensive framework for balancing economic and cultural objectives.

### 4.4 Governance and Risk-Sharing Frameworks

Effective PPP arrangements for cultural heritage require carefully designed governance and risk-sharing frameworks that align the interests of different partners while protecting cultural values. Governance structures typically involve representation from multiple stakeholders on governing boards or advisory committees, with clear decision-making authority and accountability mechanisms. Risk-sharing arrangements must address the distinctive risks associated with cultural projects, including risks to cultural heritage integrity, reputational risks, and the inherent uncertainty of cultural market demand.

The literature on cultural PPPs emphasizes that success depends on intangibles including trust between sectors, political will, and community support [2]. These factors cannot be guaranteed by contractual arrangements alone but require sustained relationship-building and commitment from all partners. The European experience with heritage PPPs demonstrates that long-term leases of heritage buildings to private operators who develop museums or cultural centers with mixed commercial use can be successful when accompanied by appropriate safeguards for heritage protection and public access [2]. However, the literature also notes that many heritage PPPs involve "white elephant" buildings—difficult-to-reuse properties for which the private sector alone would rarely take the lead—suggesting that public support remains essential even in partnership arrangements.

## 5. Indigenous Rights and Community Participation

### 5.1 The Centrality of Community Rights

The protection of indigenous peoples' cultural heritage and intellectual property has been recognized in numerous international instruments, including the Universal Declaration on Human Rights, the International Covenants on Economic, Cultural and Social Rights and on Civil and Political Rights, and the International Labour Organization's Convention No. 169 [6]. However, as noted earlier, the nature of indigenous peoples' intellectual property—which is often inseparable from spiritual, cultural, social, and economic aspects of indigenous life—and the notion of collective ownership have not been adequately addressed in existing international intellectual property law. This gap has prompted ongoing efforts to develop specialized mechanisms that recognize and protect indigenous peoples' rights over their cultural heritage.

The WIPO-UNESCO Model Treaty on the Protection of Expressions of Folklore against Illicit Exploitation represents an important early effort to address these gaps [6]. The Model Treaty recognizes indigenous peoples as the traditional owners of artistic heritage, including folklore, music, and dance, created within indigenous territories and passed down through generations. While the Model Treaty is not legally binding, it has influenced national legislation and established principles that continue to inform international discussions on the protection of traditional cultural expressions.

### 5.2 Prior Informed Consent and Benefit-Sharing

The principle of prior informed consent has emerged as a fundamental requirement for ethical engagement with indigenous and traditional communities regarding the use of their cultural heritage. This principle, which has been elaborated in various international instruments including the Nagoya Protocol and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, requires that communities be fully informed about proposed uses of their cultural heritage and provide explicit consent before such uses proceed [8]. Prior informed consent processes must be conducted in culturally appropriate ways, with information provided in accessible languages and formats, and with adequate time for community deliberation and decision-making.

Benefit-sharing arrangements complement prior informed consent by ensuring that communities receive fair and equitable returns from the commercial utilization of their cultural heritage. These arrangements can take various forms depending on the nature of the cultural heritage involved, the commercial applications, and community preferences. Monetary benefits may include upfront payments, ongoing royalties, or equity stakes in commercial ventures. Non-monetary benefits may include capacity building, technology transfer, support for cultural preservation activities, or preferential access to markets and opportunities. The design of benefit-sharing arrangements should reflect genuine community participation and should be subject to ongoing monitoring and adjustment as circumstances change.

### 5.3 Community Participation in Governance

Beyond consent for specific uses, indigenous rights frameworks increasingly recognize the importance of community participation in broader governance decisions affecting cultural heritage. Article 31 of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples recognizes the right of indigenous peoples to maintain, control, protect, and develop their cultural heritage, implying a role in governance decisions that affect these rights [8]. This participatory dimension is essential for ensuring that institutional guarantees for balancing economic and cultural values reflect community perspectives and priorities rather than being imposed externally.

The practical implementation of community participation in cultural heritage governance requires institutional mechanisms that enable meaningful involvement. These may include designated seats for community representatives on governing bodies, consultation requirements for decisions affecting cultural heritage, community veto powers over particularly sensitive uses, or community ownership or co-ownership of cultural assets. The specific mechanisms will vary depending on the nature of the cultural heritage, the community context, and the applicable legal framework. However, the underlying principle—that communities should have substantive voice in decisions affecting their cultural heritage—is increasingly recognized as essential for legitimate and effective governance.

## 6. Implementation Challenges and Solutions

### 6.1 Structural Deficits and Financial Sustainability

One of the most persistent challenges facing cultural institutions engaged in creative industry value chains is the structural deficit problem, where operating costs consistently exceed revenues from commercial activities [2]. This challenge reflects the public goods characteristics of cultural heritage, which generate benefits that extend beyond those who directly pay for access or products. Cultural heritage provides option value (the value of preserving options for future use), existence value (the value derived from simply knowing that heritage exists), and bequest value (the value of preserving heritage for future generations)—none of which are captured through market transactions.

Solutions to the structural deficit problem typically involve a combination of approaches. Public subsidies remain essential for supporting activities that generate primarily public benefits, though the form and conditions of such subsidies vary across jurisdictions. Revenue diversification through commercial activities, including merchandising, licensing, events, and hospitality services, can reduce dependence on public funding while generating additional resources for heritage preservation. Efficiency improvements through private sector management expertise can reduce operating costs and improve service quality. Endowments and trust funds can provide stable, long-term funding that is insulated from annual budget fluctuations.

### 6.2 Balancing Commercial Viability with Cultural Authenticity

Creative industry value chains that incorporate cultural heritage face inherent tensions between commercial viability and cultural authenticity. Commercial success often requires adaptations that make cultural products accessible to broader audiences—translations, modernizations, simplifications, or combinations with contemporary elements. Such adaptations may be perceived as diluting or distorting cultural authenticity, particularly when conducted without community involvement or when they perpetuate stereotypes or misrepresentations. Conversely, strict adherence to traditional forms may limit commercial appeal and economic returns.

Effective institutional frameworks for managing this tension typically combine several elements. Clear ethical guidelines for the adaptation and commercialization of cultural heritage can establish boundaries while permitting commercially viable adaptations. Community involvement in product development and approval processes can ensure that adaptations reflect community perspectives and receive community endorsement. Labeling and certification systems can help consumers distinguish between authentic cultural products and inappropriate imitations or adaptations. Benefit-sharing mechanisms ensure that commercial success generates returns for source communities, aligning economic incentives with cultural preservation.

### 6.3 Cross-Border and Digital Challenges

The globalized and digital nature of contemporary creative industries creates particular challenges for institutional frameworks that were primarily designed for national contexts. Cultural heritage elements may be utilized in creative products that cross multiple jurisdictions with different legal frameworks, enforcement mechanisms, and policy priorities. Digital technologies enable rapid copying, distribution, and transformation of cultural content, making it difficult to control unauthorized uses or to track the incorporation of cultural elements into complex creative products.

Addressing these challenges requires enhanced international cooperation and coordination. Harmonization of legal frameworks through international treaties and agreements can reduce conflicts between national regimes and provide consistent protection across borders. Capacity building in developing countries and indigenous communities can enable more effective participation in global creative industry value chains. Technological solutions, including digital watermarking, blockchain-based tracking systems, and automated content recognition, can help monitor and manage the use of cultural heritage in digital environments. Multi-stakeholder governance structures that bring together governments, private sector actors, civil society organizations, and indigenous communities can provide the comprehensive perspective needed to address these complex challenges.

## 7. Conclusions and Recommendations

### 7.1 Summary of Key Findings

This analysis has identified several key institutional guarantees that ensure long-term value chains in creative industries balance economic benefits with cultural heritage preservation. First, international legal frameworks including WIPO treaties, UNESCO conventions, ILO conventions, and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples establish foundational protections and principles that shape national legislation and corporate practices. These instruments recognize the distinctive characteristics of traditional cultural expressions, affirm the rights of indigenous and traditional communities, and establish frameworks for balancing commercial utilization with cultural preservation.

Second, sui generis intellectual property systems provide specialized protections that address the limitations of conventional intellectual property regimes for traditional cultural expressions. These systems recognize collective ownership, provide for extended or perpetual protection terms, and incorporate moral rights that ensure attribution and prevent derogatory treatment. The ongoing work of WIPO's ICG to develop an international instrument on traditional knowledge and traditional cultural expressions represents continued progress toward harmonized sui generis protection.

Third, public-private partnership models and multi-stakeholder governance structures provide operational mechanisms for translating international principles and legal protections into sustainable value chains. Effective PPPs leverage the strengths of both public and private sectors while protecting cultural heritage through appropriate governance and risk-sharing frameworks. The evolution toward 5P models that explicitly incorporate people and policy dimensions reflects growing recognition of the importance of community participation and political commitment for long-term success.

Fourth, indigenous rights frameworks including prior informed consent requirements, benefit-sharing principles, and community participation in governance ensure that source communities have substantive voice in decisions affecting their cultural heritage. These frameworks recognize that legitimate and effective cultural heritage management requires the meaningful involvement of those who create, maintain, and transmit traditional knowledge and cultural expressions.

### 7.2 Policy Recommendations

Based on this analysis, several policy recommendations emerge for governments, international organizations, and other stakeholders seeking to strengthen institutional guarantees for balancing economic and cultural values in creative industry value chains.

Governments should develop or strengthen sui generis intellectual property frameworks that provide appropriate protection for traditional cultural expressions while enabling their commercial utilization. These frameworks should recognize collective ownership, provide for extended protection terms, incorporate moral rights, and establish clear benefit-sharing mechanisms. They should be developed through meaningful consultation with indigenous and traditional communities and should reflect community perspectives and priorities.

International organizations should continue and intensify efforts to harmonize protections for traditional cultural expressions across jurisdictions. The ongoing negotiations in WIPO's IGC should be advanced with a view to adopting an international instrument that provides effective protection while respecting national sovereignty and community rights. UNESCO should continue to promote the integration of cultural heritage considerations into sustainable development planning and should support capacity building in developing countries for cultural heritage management.

Public-private partnerships for cultural heritage should be designed with careful attention to governance structures, risk-sharing arrangements, and safeguards for cultural heritage protection. Partnership agreements should include clear requirements for community participation, benefit-sharing, and heritage preservation. The 5P model provides a useful framework for ensuring that partnerships incorporate people and policy dimensions alongside traditional public and private sector engagement.

Indigenous and traditional communities should be supported in developing their own capacities for engaging with creative industry value chains. This includes legal literacy and advocacy skills, business and entrepreneurial capabilities, and organizational capacity for collective action. Community-controlled enterprises and cooperatives can provide mechanisms for communities to participate directly in commercial activities while maintaining cultural integrity and capturing greater value from their cultural heritage.

### 7.3 Future Directions

The institutional landscape for balancing economic benefits with cultural heritage in creative industries continues to evolve. Several developments warrant continued attention and analysis. The digital transformation of creative industries creates both opportunities and challenges for cultural heritage protection, requiring adaptive regulatory approaches and technological solutions. Climate change and environmental degradation threaten cultural heritage sites and practices, requiring integration of heritage considerations into climate adaptation and environmental management. The growing recognition of indigenous rights and the emphasis on decolonizing cultural institutions may prompt reexamination of existing governance structures and benefit-sharing arrangements.

The relationship between cultural heritage protection and economic development is increasingly supported by empirical research, providing evidence that can inform policy decisions. Studies demonstrating the positive effects of heritage protection on economic development, tourism, human capital formation, and innovation strengthen the case for continued investment in heritage protection and suggest that the perceived tension between economic and cultural objectives may be more apparent than real. When properly managed, creative industry value chains can generate economic returns that support heritage preservation while heritage assets provide distinctive cultural content that differentiates creative products in global markets.

Ultimately, the effectiveness of institutional guarantees depends on sustained commitment from multiple stakeholders—governments, international organizations, private sector actors, civil society organizations, and indigenous and traditional communities. The complexity of the challenges involved requires collaborative approaches that draw on diverse perspectives and capabilities. The stakes are high: creative industry value chains that effectively balance economic and cultural values can generate sustainable livelihoods, preserve cultural diversity, and enrich human experience. Value chains that fail to achieve this balance risk depleting cultural heritage for short-term commercial gain, leaving future generations impoverished in ways that cannot easily be remedied.
