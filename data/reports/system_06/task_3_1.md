# Final Research Report: Home Management Strategy for Chronic Heart Failure Balancing NYHA Functional Classification Thresholds with Quality of Life Adherence

## Executive Summary

Chronic heart failure (CHF) represents one of the most significant challenges in contemporary cardiovascular medicine, affecting millions of patients worldwide and imposing substantial burdens on healthcare systems. The management of this complex syndrome requires sophisticated strategies that effectively balance disease severity—often quantified through the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification system—with meaningful improvements in patient quality of life (QoL). This report synthesizes current evidence from multiple clinical trials, systematic reviews, and expert consensus statements to identify home management approaches that optimally reconcile these sometimes competing objectives.

The evidence demonstrates that remote patient monitoring (RPM) integrated with structured care pathways offers the most promising framework for achieving this balance. Telemonitoring strategies, particularly those incorporating multiparameter surveillance, personalized algorithmic responses, and sustained patient engagement, have demonstrated significant improvements in both functional status and QoL metrics across NYHA classification strata. The IN-TIME trial demonstrated that RPM reduced composite clinical outcomes from 27.2% to 18.9% in NYHA class II-III patients [1], while longer-duration interventions (≥52 weeks) have been associated with statistically significant QoL improvements [2]. However, the effectiveness of these strategies depends critically on the degree of patient activation, with interactive systems showing substantially better adherence and clinical outcomes compared to passive monitoring approaches.

This report concludes that optimal home management requires a paradigm shift from episodic care to continuous, technology-enabled self-care support systems that are calibrated to individual NYHA classification levels while maintaining focus on patient-centered outcomes. The integration of guideline-directed medical therapy with personalized telemonitoring, structured self-care education, and regular clinical feedback represents the evidence-based standard for contemporary CHF management.

## 1. Introduction and Clinical Context

### 1.1 The Burden of Chronic Heart Failure

Chronic heart failure has emerged as a defining challenge in modern cardiovascular medicine, representing the end-stage manifestation of various cardiac pathologies and imposing substantial burdens on patients, caregivers, and healthcare systems alike. The syndrome's prevalence continues to rise in parallel with aging populations and improved survival from acute cardiac events, creating an imperative for effective home-based management strategies that can reduce hospitalizations while preserving or enhancing patient quality of life [3].

The American Medical Association has identified heart disease among the chronic conditions requiring innovative approaches for patient care, recognizing that physicians and health systems must navigate the changing terrain of these complex long-term health conditions [3]. This recognition underscores the necessity of developing management strategies that extend beyond traditional clinical settings into patients' homes and daily lives, where the majority of disease management decisions are executed.

### 1.2 NYHA Functional Classification Framework

The New York Heart Association functional classification system remains the cornerstone for stratifying heart failure severity in clinical practice and research. This four-tiered framework categorizes patients based on their symptoms and physical activity limitations:

- **NYHA Class I**: Patients with cardiac disease but without resulting limitations of ordinary physical activity
- **NYHA Class II**: Patients with slight limitation of physical activity; comfortable at rest but ordinary activity causes symptoms
- **NYHA Class III**: Patients with marked limitation of physical activity; comfortable at rest but less than ordinary activity produces symptoms
- **NYHA Class IV**: Patients unable to carry out any physical activity without discomfort; symptoms present even at rest

The HeartCycle study demonstrated that telemonitoring programs can effectively engage patients across this classification spectrum, with 66% of enrolled patients in NYHA class III and 34% in NYHA class II [4]. Importantly, the study authors noted that daily monitoring of signs and symptoms allows for early detection of deterioration, suggesting that patients in NYHA class IV would show similar adherence patterns to monitoring protocols, though physical activity recommendations would require modification given their severe limitations [4].

### 1.3 Quality of Life as a Primary Outcome

Quality of life assessment has emerged as a critical outcome measure in heart failure management, reflecting the shift toward patient-centered care paradigms. The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) represents the most widely validated instrument for quantifying health-related quality of life in this population, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 and higher values indicating better function.

Clinical trials have demonstrated that effective interventions can produce meaningful QoL improvements. The CONFIRM-HF trial of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose showed mean KCCQ scores increasing from 52±1 to 66±1 points over 24 weeks [5], representing a clinically significant improvement. Patients in these studies consistently identified self-care behaviors—including medication adherence, dietary modification, physical activity, weight monitoring, and symptom management—as essential components of maintaining good quality of life [6].

## 2. Remote Patient Monitoring: The Evidence Base

### 2.1 Mortality and Hospitalization Outcomes

The TEMA-HF 1 long-term follow-up study provided crucial insights into the durability of telemonitoring effects on clinical outcomes. This randomized controlled trial assessed whether an initial six-month telemonitoring program compared with usual care would result in reduced all-cause mortality, heart failure admissions, and healthcare costs in CHF patients at long-term follow-up [7]. The study enrolled 142 CHF patients (65% male; age: 76±10 years; ejection fraction: 36±15%) who were alive at the follow-up time point.

Results indicated that compared with usual care, the initial six-month telemonitoring program had no significant effect on all-cause mortality (hazard ratio: 0.83; 95% confidence interval, 0.57 to 1.20; p=0.32) [7]. However, the number of days lost due to heart failure readmissions was significantly lower in the telemonitoring group (p=0.04), demonstrating that even without mortality benefit, meaningful clinical improvements can be achieved through structured remote monitoring [7]. Healthcare costs did not differ significantly between groups (€9140±10580 vs €12495±22433; p=0.87), suggesting cost-neutrality at minimum [7].

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in Heart & Lung confirmed these findings across multiple studies, concluding that telemonitoring reduces mortality and rehospitalization in heart failure patients and can be integrated into care pathways to ensure continuity of care [8]. The meta-analysis synthesized evidence related to the impact of telemonitoring strategies on mortality and hospital readmissions, providing robust support for the implementation of these approaches in routine clinical practice.

### 2.2 The IN-TIME Trial and Implantable Device Monitoring

The IN-TIME (Implant-based multiparameter telemonitoring of patients with heart failure) trial further validated the role of remote patient monitoring in heart failure management. This randomized, multicenter, international study enrolled 664 patients with New York Heart Association class II-III symptoms, an ejection fraction of less than 35%, and recent cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator (CRT-D) or dual-chamber ICD implantation [1].

The primary outcome was a composite clinical score that included all-cause mortality, heart failure-related hospitalizations, and changes in NYHA class. After one year, the trial demonstrated that 18.9% of patients in the remote patient monitoring group experienced worsened outcomes compared to 27.2% in the standard care group [1]. These findings underscored the importance of remote patient monitoring in improving clinical outcomes, particularly when incorporating multiparameter assessment from implantable devices that can detect early signs of decompensation.

### 2.3 Functional Classification Improvements

Evidence from clinical trials consistently demonstrates that effective home management strategies produce improvements in NYHA functional classification. The CONFIRM-HF trial of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose in heart failure patients with reduced ejection fraction showed that 47% of patients achieved NYHA functional class I or II at week 24 after treatment, compared with only 30% of patients assigned to placebo [5]. Secondary endpoints included the 6-minute walk distance, which increased from 274±6 to 313±7 meters after 24 weeks, demonstrating functional capacity improvements [5].

Economic analyses have supported the cost-effectiveness of such interventions, driven by improved symptomatic status (NYHA class) and reduced hospitalization rates [5]. Because a complete iron dose may be delivered in fewer infusions with ferric carboxymaltose compared to other formulations, treatment burden is minimized while maximizing therapeutic benefit—a consideration of particular importance for home-based management strategies.

## 3. Balancing NYHA Classification with Quality of Life

### 3.1 The Adherence-Quality of Life Nexus

The relationship between treatment adherence and quality of life in heart failure patients represents a critical consideration for home management strategy development. Research has consistently demonstrated that adherence to treatment regimens is associated with quality of life outcomes, with patients who receive heart transplantation showing particularly strong correlations between adherence and post-transplant quality of life [6].

Self-care behaviors may impact quality of life when they affect symptom status, creating a feedback loop where effective symptom management improves both functional capacity and subjective wellbeing [6]. Patients have identified social support—including role replacement, provision of information, and creation of suitable environments for self-care—as important factors influencing quality of life [6]. Many heart failure patients are elderly, live alone without spouses, have impaired cognitive function, and possess multiple comorbidities, all of which complicate treatment adherence and underscore the need for comprehensive support systems [6].

### 3.2 Duration of Intervention Effects

A pivotal finding from recent research concerns the relationship between intervention duration and quality of life outcomes. Moderator analysis based on intervention duration revealed that shorter-duration interventions were not inferior to usual care, while longer-duration interventions (≥52 weeks) were associated with statistically significant quality of life improvements [2].

This duration-dependent effect likely reflects the time required for patients to develop and consolidate self-care skills. Longer-duration telemonitoring has been associated with refined self-care skills sustained with constant engagement over time, reduced hospital and emergency admissions, and improved functional and health-related quality of life [2]. Improvements in quality of life were more frequently reported in studies with longer follow-up durations (≥12 months), suggesting that sustained engagement in telemonitoring may be necessary to achieve meaningful patient-reported benefits [2].

### 3.3 NYHA-Specific Considerations

The HeartCycle study provided important insights into how management strategies should be tailored across NYHA classification levels. In this study, 66% of patients were in NYHA class III and 34% in NYHA class II [4]. Self-reported self-care behavior scores improved significantly during the observation period for daily weighing, low-salt diet, physical activity (P<0.001), and fluid restriction (P<0.05) [4].

Average adherence to measuring weight was 90%±16%, to measuring blood pressure was 89%±17%, and to symptom reporting was 66%±32% [4]. These adherence patterns reveal important asymmetries in self-care behavior, with objective physiological measurements showing higher adherence than subjective symptom reporting. The study authors noted that for NYHA class IV patients, who have severe limitations with physical activity and are mostly bedbound, increasing physical activity level would be particularly challenging [4]. However, daily monitoring of signs and symptoms should show similar adherence patterns across classification levels [4].

## 4. Technology-Enabled Management Strategies

### 4.1 Remote Monitoring Modalities

The JACC Scientific Statement on Remote Monitoring for Heart Failure Management at Home established that remote monitoring coupled with a system of care that engages, informs, and empowers patients is essential for effective home management of heart failure [9]. This approach serves to control symptoms, avoid hospitalization, and ameliorate the patient's perception of illness. Effective remote monitoring requires an accurate, reliable signal that is actionable through personalized algorithms [9].

Early telemonitoring of weights and symptoms did not decrease heart failure hospitalizations but helped identify steps toward effective monitoring programs [9]. A signal that is accurate and actionable with response kinetics for early re-assessment is required for the treatment of patients at high risk, while signal specifications differ for surveillance of low-risk patients [9]. Tracking of congestion with cardiac filling pressures or lung water content has shown the most impact in decreasing hospitalizations, while multiparameter scores from implanted rhythm devices have identified patients at increased risk [9]. Algorithms require better personalization of signal thresholds and interventions [9].

### 4.2 Passive Versus Active Monitoring

A critical distinction in remote monitoring technology concerns the degree of patient engagement required. Patient engagement emerged as a critical yet underutilized component across telemonitoring interventions, with only approximately 9.3% of studies employing models that required active participation from patients [2]. The overwhelming majority relied on passive data collection, with minimal patient interaction [2].

This low level of engagement likely limited the effectiveness of telemonitoring in improving clinical outcomes, as active involvement is increasingly recognized as essential for enhancing adherence, self-management, and clinical benefit [2]. Regarding feedback frequency, 62.5% of studies implemented periodic feedback, most commonly daily (59%), while 31.2% used continuous monitoring, and 6.2% provided on-demand feedback [2].

More interactive telemonitoring systems, which actively engage patients rather than relying on passive monitoring, have been associated with better adherence and improved clinical outcomes [2]. This suggests that telemonitoring alone may be insufficient in preventing readmissions unless combined with comprehensive in-person care and structured intervention strategies [2].

### 4.3 Video Monitoring and Nurse-Patient Relationships

An emerging modality in remote heart failure management involves video monitoring, which fosters a stronger connection between patients and healthcare professionals compared to mobile-based or voice telemonitoring strategies [10]. This approach aligns with personalized nursing interventions, reinforcing education and behavioral changes beyond the intervention period [10].

The study utilizing video monitoring highlighted its role in sustaining self-care practices in heart failure management [10]. By strengthening the nurse-patient relationship and promoting long-term adherence, video monitoring has the potential to reduce readmissions and improve quality of life [10]. This finding aligns with patient perceptions that social support and professional relationships are important components of effective self-care [6].

### 4.4 Device Selection and Data Transmission

The choice of devices and method of data transmission represent critical factors influencing both provider and patient adherence [1]. Passive data transmission and the use of minimally invasive sensors are more likely to be accepted by patients and result in better adherence to monitoring protocols [1]. Furthermore, setting appropriate clinical alert thresholds is essential to reduce false positives and ensure that only clinically actionable data prompts provider intervention [1]. This optimization helps avoid alert fatigue among healthcare providers, which can undermine the effectiveness of monitoring systems [1].

## 5. Self-Care Behavior and Adherence Patterns

### 5.1 Core Self-Care Components

The literature consistently identifies five core components of heart failure self-care: medication adherence, dietary modification (particularly sodium restriction), regular physical activity, weight monitoring, and symptom management [6]. Patients have articulated these behaviors as essential ways to maintain good quality of life, with one patient noting: "Well, the only thing I can do is keep taking my medication, and try the diet, and see if the diet will give me more strength" [6].

The need to adhere to treatment regimens can produce dramatic changes in lifestyle, and nonadherence can result in worsening of heart failure symptoms, which is an important factor affecting quality of life [6]. Therefore, self-care should be improved in patients with heart failure, and for appropriate self-care, patients need social support [6].

### 5.2 Adherence Variability Across Behaviors

The HeartCycle study revealed important patterns in adherence variability across different self-care behaviors. Adherence to daily weight measurement reached 90%±16%, while blood pressure monitoring adherence was 89%±17% [4]. In contrast, adherence to daily symptom reporting was substantially lower at 66%±32% and declined over the long-term [4].

This pattern suggests that objective, easily quantified behaviors achieve higher adherence than subjective symptom assessment, which requires more cognitive engagement and interpretation. Strategies that improve symptom reporting adherence—such as structured prompts, simplified reporting tools, and regular feedback—may enhance overall self-care effectiveness.

### 5.3 The Role of Telemonitoring in Self-Care Improvement

It is well established that self-care in heart failure is directly influenced by telemonitoring [10]. A previously published study utilized remote monitoring through software and non-invasive devices to monitor heart failure patients at home, finding that self-care scores in the intervention group increased by 6 points over 12 months, compared to a 1-point increase in the control group [10].

Another clinical trial using text messages as a remote monitoring tool showed a significant increase in self-care scores in the intervention group compared to the standard care group (score difference at 465 days of -2.08 points, P<0.01) [10]. These findings demonstrate that technology-enabled monitoring can enhance self-care behaviors across multiple domains, though the magnitude of improvement varies by intervention type and intensity.

## 6. Integrated Care Models and Chronic Care Management

### 6.1 Chronic Care Management Framework

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services has established a Chronic Care Management (CCM) framework that provides reimbursement for care coordination services for patients with multiple chronic conditions [11]. Eligible patients have two or more chronic conditions expected to persist for at least 12 months or until death, which place them at increased risk of acute exacerbation and/or decompensation, functional decline, or death [11].

Chronic care management includes patient care coordination services such as care planning, care management, help with transitions of care, and support with achieving health goals while prioritizing continuity of care [11]. CCM services are delivered by a care team led by a physician or other Licensed Independent Practitioner and are typically conducted outside of regular office visits [11], making this framework particularly relevant to home-based management strategies.

### 6.2 Exercise and Physical Activity Recommendations

Current guidelines recommend regular physical activity and/or exercise training for heart failure patients, specifying that these interventions are effective and safe [12]. Exercise training may improve functional capacity, quality of life, and reduce hospitalization [12]. However, physical activity advice should incorporate patients' views, preferences, needs, and values [12].

Sexual activity can be resumed for patients in NYHA class I or II and for patients who are in NYHA class III or IV if their condition is stable and optimally managed [12]. Professional attitudes and behavior should involve asking patients about physical activity and exercise tolerance as part of routine clinical encounters, referring to exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation programs, and supporting patients who struggle with renewing exercise after diagnosis [12].

### 6.3 Comprehensive Self-Care Support

For appropriate self-care, patients need comprehensive support that addresses the multidimensional nature of heart failure management. This includes monitoring signs and symptoms of heart failure, monitoring deterioration of comorbidities and dehydration, monitoring side effects, and monitoring response to self-care management behaviors [12].

Patients in NYHA class IV are advised against driving, and all patients should receive immunization for influenza and pneumococcal disease [12]. Lifestyle modifications including smoking cessation (cigarettes, e-cigarettes, waterpipe) and avoidance of recreational drugs are essential components of comprehensive care [12]. Family members and friends should be encouraged to support patients in their self-care efforts [12].

## 7. Synthesis: Optimal Home Management Strategy

### 7.1 Key Principles

Based on the synthesis of available evidence, several key principles emerge for optimizing home management strategies that balance NYHA functional classification thresholds with quality of life improvements:

**First**, remote patient monitoring must be integrated with active patient engagement rather than relying solely on passive data collection. The evidence clearly demonstrates that interactive systems produce superior adherence and clinical outcomes compared to passive monitoring approaches [2].

**Second**, intervention duration should be sufficient to allow for self-care skill development and consolidation. Longer-duration interventions (≥52 weeks) are associated with statistically significant quality of life improvements [2], suggesting that abbreviated programs may not achieve optimal patient-centered outcomes.

**Third**, monitoring strategies should be calibrated to individual NYHA classification levels. While daily monitoring of signs and symptoms shows similar adherence patterns across classification levels [4], physical activity recommendations require modification for NYHA class IV patients who have severe limitations [4].

**Fourth**, multiparameter monitoring approaches that track congestion through cardiac filling pressures or lung water content show the greatest impact on hospitalization reduction [9], while multiparameter scores from implanted devices can identify patients at increased risk [9].

### 7.2 Recommended Framework

The optimal home management strategy incorporates the following elements:

1. **Technology-Enabled Monitoring**: Implementation of remote monitoring systems with accurate, actionable signals and personalized algorithmic thresholds [9]. Passive data transmission and minimally invasive sensors should be prioritized to maximize patient acceptance [1].

2. **Structured Self-Care Education**: Comprehensive education covering medication adherence, dietary modification (particularly sodium restriction), physical activity within individual limitations, daily weight monitoring, and systematic symptom reporting [6].

3. **Regular Clinical Feedback**: Periodic feedback, most commonly daily, combined with continuous monitoring capabilities and on-demand support options [2]. Healthcare provider alert thresholds should be optimized to reduce false positives and prevent alert fatigue [1].

4. **Sustained Engagement**: Duration of at least 52 weeks to achieve meaningful quality of life improvements [2], with transition strategies to maintain self-care behaviors after formal monitoring concludes [10].

5. **Social Support Integration**: Involvement of family members and caregivers in self-care efforts [6], with video monitoring approaches that strengthen nurse-patient relationships [10].

6. **NYHA-Adapted Interventions**: Physical activity recommendations tailored to functional classification [12], with particular attention to the needs of NYHA class IV patients who require modified approaches [4].

### 7.3 Outcome Measurement

Assessment of strategy effectiveness should incorporate multiple outcome domains:

- **Functional Status**: NYHA classification, 6-minute walk distance, and peak VO2 measurements [5]
- **Quality of Life**: Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire scores, with clinically meaningful improvements defined as increases of ≥5 points [5]
- **Adherence Metrics**: Daily weight and blood pressure monitoring adherence (target >85%), symptom reporting adherence (target >70%) [4]
- **Clinical Events**: All-cause mortality, heart failure hospitalizations, and days lost due to readmissions [7]
- **Healthcare Utilization**: Emergency department visits, outpatient visits, and total healthcare costs [7]

## 8. Conclusions and Future Directions

The evidence synthesized in this report supports the implementation of comprehensive, technology-enabled home management strategies that integrate remote patient monitoring with structured self-care support, personalized to individual NYHA functional classification levels while maintaining focus on quality of life outcomes. The paradigm shift from episodic clinical care to continuous, technology-enabled self-care support systems represents the evidence-based standard for contemporary chronic heart failure management.

Remote monitoring coupled with systems that engage, inform, and empower patients is essential for effective home management to control symptoms, avoid hospitalization, and ameliorate the patient's perception of illness [9]. However, telemonitoring alone may be insufficient unless combined with comprehensive in-person care and structured intervention strategies [2]. Future research should focus on methodological improvements to better understand variations in outcomes and determine the most effective telemonitoring configurations for different patient populations.

The integration of guideline-directed medical therapy with personalized telemonitoring, structured self-care education, regular clinical feedback, and sustained patient engagement offers the most promising approach for balancing NYHA functional classification improvements with meaningful quality of life gains. As healthcare systems continue to evolve toward value-based care models, these comprehensive home management strategies will become increasingly important for optimizing outcomes in the growing population of patients living with chronic heart failure.
