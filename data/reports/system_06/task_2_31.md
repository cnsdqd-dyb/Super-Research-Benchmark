# Impact of UV Irradiation and Humidity Cycling on Photovoltaic Backsheet Aging and Module Sealing Integrity

## Executive Summary

Photovoltaic (PV) backsheets serve as the critical rear protective barrier for solar modules, providing electrical insulation, mechanical protection, and environmental resistance. This report examines the complex interplay between ultraviolet (UV) irradiation and humidity cycling on backsheet degradation mechanisms and their consequential effects on module sealing integrity. Through analysis of accelerated testing research and field observations, this investigation reveals that the simultaneous exposure to UV radiation and hygrothermal stresses induces synergistic degradation pathways that significantly compromise backsheet performance. The findings indicate that tropical and coastal climates pose particular challenges, with delamination occurrence rates reaching 35% and annual degradation rates of 1.2% in these regions. The research demonstrates that UV-induced photodegradation and hygrothermal hydrolysis operate concurrently, leading to embrittlement, cracking, adhesion loss, and eventual failure of the backsheet encapsulant interface. Understanding these degradation mechanisms is essential for developing more durable backsheet materials and improving accelerated testing protocols to better predict field performance.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Significance

The global deployment of photovoltaic solar energy systems has accelerated dramatically, with modules expected to operate reliably for 25 years or more in diverse environmental conditions. The backsheet represents a critical component in this equation, functioning as the rear protective barrier that shields the internal module components from environmental degradation while providing essential electrical insulation [1]. Despite passing standardized qualification protocols, previously undiscovered failure modes continue to emerge in field installations, highlighting the limitations of present-day accelerated stress tests that typically apply only two stress factors simultaneously at most [2].

The backsheet must simultaneously fulfill multiple functions: providing excellent electrical insulation to prevent short circuits, ensuring mechanical protection against physical impacts, maintaining optical properties with low absorbance in the active spectrum of photovoltaic cells, and serving as a barrier against environmental stressors [3]. The conservation of these mechanical, electrical, and optical properties over time under the combined influence of UV exposure, temperature variations, and humidity is of critical importance for long-term module reliability and failure prevention.

### 1.2 Scope and Objectives

This research report synthesizes findings from multiple scientific studies to provide a comprehensive analysis of how UV irradiation and humidity cycling affect backsheet materials and module sealing integrity. The investigation focuses on the degradation mechanisms induced by these environmental stressors, the synergistic effects observed when multiple stresses are applied simultaneously, and the implications for module reliability in different climate zones. The analysis encompasses various backsheet material compositions, including polyethylene terephthalate (PET)-based structures, polyamide (PA), polyvinyl fluoride (PVF), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) laminates, to provide a broad perspective on material-specific degradation behaviors.

## 2. Backsheet Architecture and Material Composition

### 2.1 Multilayer Laminate Structures

Modern photovoltaic backsheets typically employ multilayer laminate constructions designed to optimize performance across multiple functional requirements. A common commercial configuration comprises a pigmented polyethylene terephthalate (PET)-based outer layer, a PET core layer, and multiple ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) layers with varying vinyl acetate (VA) contents, along with adhesive layers between the structural components [4]. This multilayer architecture creates interfaces that become vulnerable points for degradation initiation, particularly when environmental stressors target the adhesive bonds between layers.

The outer layer of the backsheet serves as the primary UV barrier and weathering surface, often incorporating UV absorbers such as TiO₂ and ZnO nanoparticles to enhance weather resistance [5]. The core PET layer provides mechanical strength and dimensional stability, while the inner EVA layers facilitate adhesion to the module encapsulant. The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of these multilayer structures varies significantly based on material selection, with fluoropolymer-faced laminates typically achieving lower values than non-fluorinated PET-only designs [6]. Industry targets for high-reliability modules specify WVTR values below 3 g/m²/day at 38°C/90% relative humidity, though achieving this benchmark requires careful material selection and laminate construction.

### 2.2 Material-Specific Properties and Performance Characteristics

The selection of backsheet materials involves balancing multiple performance parameters including UV resistance, moisture barrier properties, thermal stability, and mechanical durability. Polyvinyl fluoride (PVF), commercially known as Tedlar, has been widely used as a backsheet material due to its excellent weatherability and chemical resistance. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) offers similar performance characteristics with enhanced chemical resistance, while polyamide (PA) provides superior mechanical properties but may exhibit different degradation behaviors under UV exposure [7].

Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) serves as a core structural material in many backsheet constructions due to its favorable combination of mechanical properties, electrical insulation characteristics, and cost-effectiveness. However, PET is susceptible to hydrolysis when exposed to elevated temperatures and humidity, leading to chain scission and embrittlement over time [8]. The susceptibility of PET to hydrolysis creates a fundamental challenge for backsheet durability in hot-humid climates, where sustained exposure to elevated temperature and relative humidity accelerates the degradation process.

## 3. UV Irradiation Effects on Backsheet Degradation

### 3.1 Photochemical Degradation Mechanisms

Ultraviolet radiation in the terrestrial solar spectrum (280-400 nm) contains photons with energies exceeding those of the chemical bonds found in hydrocarbon polymers, particularly carbon-carbon bonds [9]. This energy differential enables UV photons to interact directly with polymer molecular structures, contributing sufficient energy to break chemical bonds. The resulting photochemical degradation manifests through polymer chain cleavage, cross-linking reactions, and the formation of new functional groups that alter the material's physical and chemical properties.

The degradation process begins at the outermost surface of the backsheet, where UV intensity is highest, and progresses inward through the material layers. Research using Raman spectroscopic imaging has demonstrated that severe degradation, indicated by high fluorescence, occurs in the outermost region of pigmented PET outer layers, with the degradation gradient extending into deeper layers over time [10]. This non-uniform degradation profile creates internal stress concentrations that can initiate cracking and delamination, particularly when combined with thermal cycling stresses.

### 3.2 UV-Induced Property Changes

The exposure of backsheet materials to UV radiation produces measurable changes in mechanical, optical, and chemical properties. Photo-oxidation from UV radiation exposure causes a reduction in the elongation at break and overall flexibility of polymer materials, leading to embrittlement that compromises the backsheet's ability to accommodate thermal expansion and contraction [11]. Studies have shown that UV degradation of the PET outer layer results in loss of elasticity, which subsequently affects the mechanical integrity of the entire backsheet structure.

The incorporation of UV stabilizers and absorbers can mitigate but not eliminate UV-induced degradation. These additives function by absorbing harmful UV radiation and dissipating the absorbed energy as heat, thereby protecting the underlying polymer matrix. However, the gradual consumption of UV stabilizers during service life means that protection diminishes over time, eventually leaving the polymer vulnerable to photodegradation [12]. The rate of stabilizer consumption depends on UV intensity, temperature, and the presence of other environmental stressors that may accelerate additive depletion.

### 3.3 UV Exposure Thresholds and Failure Observations

Accelerated testing protocols have established UV exposure thresholds associated with backsheet failure initiation. Research conducted on miniature crystalline-silicon modules with UV-transmitting and UV-blocking encapsulants revealed cracking failures within cumulative exposure periods corresponding to field deployment in tropical conditions [13]. For polyamide-based backsheets, cracking failures were observed within 120 days of tropical condition exposure, while PVDF-based backsheets exhibited failures after 84 days of tropical cycling followed by 42 days of multi-season cycling.

These accelerated testing results correlate well with failures observed in fielded modules, validating the accelerated testing methodology while highlighting the severity of UV exposure in tropical environments. The ability to simultaneously apply multiple stress factors in combined-accelerated stress testing (C-AST) protocols allows for improved identification of failure modes that might not be apparent under single-stress testing conditions [14]. This finding underscores the importance of multi-factor testing approaches for accurately predicting field performance.

## 4. Humidity and Temperature Cycling Effects

### 4.1 Hygrothermal Degradation Mechanisms

The combined effects of humidity and elevated temperature create aggressive degradation conditions for polymer backsheets through multiple mechanisms. Damp heat testing at 85°C and 85% relative humidity has been shown to result in significant hydrolysis of PET materials, with the ester groups in the polymer chain undergoing cleavage reactions in the presence of moisture [15]. This hydrolysis process reduces molecular weight, compromises mechanical properties, and leads to embrittlement that significantly reduces the strain at break for affected materials.

The temperature dependence of hydrolysis kinetics follows Arrhenius-type behavior, with reaction rates increasing exponentially with temperature. At the elevated temperatures typical of damp heat testing (85°C), hydrolysis proceeds at rates that would require years of field exposure at ambient temperatures. This acceleration enables meaningful comparison of material performance within practical testing timeframes, though the extrapolation to field conditions requires careful consideration of the actual temperature and humidity profiles experienced in different climate zones.

### 4.2 Moisture Ingress and Diffusion Processes

Moisture ingress into PV modules occurs through multiple pathways, including diffusion through the backsheet material, penetration at junction box penetrations, and entry at module edges [16]. The backsheet's water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) determines the rate of moisture diffusion through the material, with higher WVTR values allowing more rapid moisture ingress. The concentration of moisture in the encapsulant as a function of exposure time can be described by the relationship:

$$C(t) = C_0 \left(1 - \exp\left(-\frac{WVTR_{backsheet} \cdot t}{C_{sat,EVA} \cdot d_{EVA}}\right)\right)$$

where $C_0$ and $C_{sat,EVA}$ represent the initial and saturation concentration of the EVA encapsulant, respectively, and $d_{EVA}$ is the encapsulant thickness [17]. This relationship demonstrates that moisture ingress is governed by the backsheet's permeability characteristics and the encapsulant's capacity for moisture absorption.

Analysis of moisture diffusion has revealed that due to the relatively high diffusion rate of water in EVA, even modules with glass/glass construction experience significant moisture ingress over their operational lifetime [18]. The only effective methods for preventing moisture ingress involve either creating a true hermetic seal or utilizing low-diffusivity edge seal materials containing substantial desiccant quantities. For backsheets to achieve equilibration half-times of 20 years, a maximum WVTR of approximately 10⁻⁴ g/m²/day at 27°C would be necessary, though most commercial backsheets exhibit WVTR values several orders of magnitude higher.

### 4.3 Temperature Cycling Effects

Temperature cycling induces mechanical stresses in PV modules due to the differential thermal expansion coefficients of the various materials comprising the module structure. The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch between materials such as silicon cells, copper interconnects, glass, and polymer encapsulants creates cyclic stress concentrations at interfaces and solder joints [19]. Over thousands of thermal cycles spanning the module's operational lifetime, these stresses accumulate and can lead to fatigue failure of solder joints, cracking of cells, and delamination at material interfaces.

The thermal cycling stress range significantly influences the rate of fatigue accumulation, with larger temperature differentials producing more severe stress amplitudes. Standard thermal cycling protocols typically range from -40°C to +85°C, encompassing the temperature extremes that modules might experience in field deployment [20]. The combination of temperature cycling with humidity exposure creates particularly aggressive conditions, as moisture at interfaces can accelerate adhesion degradation and promote corrosion of metallic components.

## 5. Synergistic Effects of Combined Stress Factors

### 5.1 UV-Humidity Interactions

The simultaneous exposure of backsheet materials to UV radiation and humidity produces synergistic degradation effects that exceed the sum of individual stress contributions. Research has demonstrated that humidity and heat can accelerate photodegradation processes, while UV exposure can enhance the susceptibility of polymers to hygrothermal degradation [21]. This bidirectional interaction creates a complex degradation environment that single-stress testing protocols cannot adequately replicate.

The mechanisms underlying these synergistic effects involve multiple pathways. UV radiation can generate free radicals and other reactive species within the polymer matrix that subsequently react with moisture to form acidic or oxidative compounds. Simultaneously, moisture plasticizes the polymer, increasing molecular mobility and potentially enhancing the penetration of UV radiation into deeper material layers [22]. The combination of these effects accelerates property degradation beyond what would be predicted from individual stress contributions.

### 5.2 Adhesion Degradation at Interfaces

The interfaces between backsheet layers and between the backsheet and encapsulant represent particularly vulnerable locations for combined stress degradation. Studies of backsheet/encapsulant/glass coupon systems exposed to indoor accelerated weathering conditions have revealed that failure most frequently occurs in the outer adhesive layer and at the outer surface of the PET outer layer [23]. Minimum adhesion energies in these regions decrease substantially under combined UV, temperature, and humidity exposure, indicating significant degradation of the adhesive bonds.

Degradation of polyurethane-based adhesives in PV backsheets has been attributed to moisture accumulation due to the hydrophilicity of the adhesives, followed by thermo-hydrolytic degradation [24]. The cracking of EVA seed layers observed primarily in UV-exposed regions may allow increased moisture penetration to inner adhesive layers, accelerating their degradation. This mechanism demonstrates how UV damage can create pathways for enhanced moisture ingress, establishing a feedback loop that accelerates overall degradation.

### 5.3 Climate Zone-Specific Degradation Patterns

Field data analysis across different climate zones reveals distinct patterns of backsheet degradation mechanisms, with tropical and coastal regions showing heightened vulnerability to delamination and other failure modes. Climate zone analysis indicates that tropical regions experience annual degradation rates of 1.2% with delamination occurrence rates of 35%, while coastal regions show similar patterns with 1.1% annual degradation and 32% delamination occurrence [25]. These elevated rates reflect the combined impact of high humidity, elevated temperatures, and intense UV exposure characteristic of these environments.

The combination of high humidity and temperature cycling in tropical and coastal regions creates particularly challenging conditions for module integrity. The UV impact scores for tropical and desert climates (9.2 and 9.5, respectively) indicate severe UV exposure, while humidity impact scores for tropical and coastal regions (9.5 and 8.5) demonstrate the aggressive hygrothermal conditions [26]. These climate-specific stress profiles necessitate careful consideration of backsheet material selection based on anticipated deployment conditions.

## 6. Impact on Module Sealing Integrity

### 6.1 Delamination Mechanisms and Consequences

Delamination between the backsheet and encapsulant represents a critical failure mode that directly compromises module sealing integrity. This degradation mode occurs when the adhesive bond between layers fails, creating pathways for moisture ingress and accelerated corrosion of internal components [27]. Delamination has been observed between the encapsulant and backsheet and within layers of the backsheet itself, indicating that both interlayer and intralayer adhesion can be compromised under environmental stress.

The formation of blisters in backsheets, particularly those containing vapor barrier layers such as aluminum foil, represents another manifestation of adhesion failure [28]. These blisters form when gas evolution or moisture vapor accumulation creates pressure differentials between layers, forcing delamination and visual defects. The presence of such blisters indicates that the module's protective barrier has been compromised, allowing accelerated degradation of internal components.

### 6.2 Moisture-Related Failure Cascades

Once moisture penetrates the module envelope through backsheet defects, a cascade of secondary degradation mechanisms can be initiated. The hydrolysis of EVA encapsulants under heat and moisture exposure leads to formation of acetic acid, which can react with lead oxide in solder connections to form lead acetate [29]. This reaction pathway has been correlated with power degradation of modules operating in high-humidity environments, demonstrating how backsheet degradation can trigger broader module performance losses.

The acetic acid generated by EVA hydrolysis can also promote corrosion of cell metallization and ribbon interconnects, further degrading electrical performance [30]. Modules using high VA-content EVA have shown higher power degradation and more severe cell and ribbon corrosion after damp heat exposure, indicating that encapsulant composition interacts with backsheet performance to influence overall module reliability. The water vapor transmission rate of the backsheet directly affects the rate of moisture ingress and consequently the rate of these moisture-related degradation processes.

### 6.3 Electrical Safety Implications

The degradation of backsheet electrical insulation properties poses significant safety hazards, including potential short circuits and electrical shock risks. Backsheet failure modes such as cracking, chalking, hydrolysis of PET cores, loss of adhesion, and increased leakage current can manifest as safety hazards requiring module replacement [31]. The dielectric strength of well-formulated backsheet laminates typically exceeds 20-30 kV/mm, but degradation under environmental stress can reduce this protection below safe operating thresholds.

The combination of backsheet cracking and moisture ingress creates conditions where electrical leakage currents can flow through the module structure, potentially creating shock hazards and contributing to potential-induced degradation (PID) mechanisms [32]. Maintaining backsheet integrity throughout the module's operational lifetime is therefore essential not only for performance preservation but also for ensuring safe operation throughout the system's service life.

## 7. Accelerated Testing Methodologies

### 7.1 Standardized Test Protocols

Conventional qualification testing according to standards such as IEC 61215 may not sufficiently elucidate field-failure mechanisms, particularly those arising from combined stress exposure [33]. Standardized accelerated stress tests including damp heat (85°C/85% RH), thermal cycling (-40°C to +85°C), and UV exposure protocols provide valuable comparative data but may not adequately replicate the complex stress interactions experienced in field deployment.

The IEC TS 62788-7-2 standard specifies methods for UV resistance testing of backsheet materials, while hygrometric testing protocols examine backsheet response to degradation driven by heat and humidity [34]. The decoupling of photodegradation and hygrothermal aging in separate tests allows for greater insight into the benefits and shortcomings of each backsheet type, though it may miss synergistic degradation effects that only manifest under combined stress conditions.

### 7.2 Combined-Accelerated Stress Testing (C-AST)

The development of combined-accelerated stress testing (C-AST) methodology represents a significant advancement in PV reliability testing by simultaneously combining multiple stress factors of the natural environment [35]. This approach addresses the limitation of standard tests that apply only two stress factors at most, enabling identification of failure modes that emerge only under multi-stress conditions. The simultaneous combination of multiple stress factors allows for improved identification of degradation mechanisms and more accurate prediction of field performance.

Validation of C-AST protocols requires comparison of failure modes induced under accelerated conditions with those observed in fielded modules. Research has demonstrated that the cracking failure of specific polyamide-based backsheet materials results from a combination of stress factors, with photo-oxidation from UV radiation exposure causing reduction in material properties that subsequently fail under thermal cycling stresses [36]. This validation approach increases confidence in accelerated testing protocols and supports their application for material qualification and design optimization.

### 7.3 Extended Stress Testing Approaches

Extended accelerated stress testing (EAST) protocols apply stress conditions beyond standard qualification levels to probe failure mechanisms and establish performance boundaries. Studies have subjected modules to UV exposure of 600 kWh/m² at 60°C, thermal cycling with 600 cycles between -40°C and +85°C, and damp heat for 2000 hours at 85°C/85% RH [37]. These extended protocols provide insight into degradation mechanisms that might not manifest within standard qualification test durations.

The comparison of different module constructions under extended stress testing, including glass/glass, glass/backsheet, and glass/transparent backsheet configurations, enables evaluation of backsheet performance in the context of complete module systems [38]. This systems-level approach captures interactions between backsheet materials and other module components that might not be apparent from backsheet-only testing.

## 8. Material Performance Comparisons

### 8.1 Fluoropolymer-Based Backsheets

Fluoropolymer-based backsheets, including those utilizing polyvinyl fluoride (PVF) and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), generally demonstrate superior weatherability compared to non-fluorinated alternatives. The strong carbon-fluorine bonds in these polymers provide enhanced resistance to UV degradation, chemical attack, and moisture penetration [39]. However, even these premium materials exhibit degradation under sustained combined stress exposure, as demonstrated by the cracking failures observed in PVDF-based backsheets after extended tropical cycling.

The embrittlement of PVDF materials under damp heat conditions represents a significant concern for long-term reliability. Tensile testing has shown significant reduction in strain at break for PVDF after damp heat exposure, indicating chemical degradation that compromises mechanical performance [40]. This embrittlement increases susceptibility to cracking under thermal cycling stresses, potentially initiating failure modes that ultimately compromise module sealing integrity.

### 8.2 PET-Based Backsheet Performance

Polyethylene terephthalate-based backsheets offer cost advantages and adequate performance in moderate climates but exhibit greater susceptibility to degradation under combined UV and humidity exposure. The hydrolysis of PET under damp heat conditions leads to molecular weight reduction and associated property degradation [41]. Post-crystallization of PET during thermal exposure can further alter material properties, potentially increasing brittleness and reducing impact resistance.

The pigmented outer layer of PET-based backsheets provides some UV protection, but degradation gradients extending from the outer surface inward indicate that UV radiation penetrates beyond the immediate surface region [42]. The combination of UV-induced surface degradation with bulk hydrolysis creates a challenging degradation environment that can lead to premature failure in aggressive climate conditions.

### 8.3 Emerging Material Developments

Research into enhanced mechanical and UV protection polymers has investigated various approaches to improve backsheet lifetime, including the incorporation of nanoparticles such as TiO₂ and ZnO as UV absorbers [43]. Graphene-based additives have also been explored for their potential to enhance UV protection while providing additional mechanical reinforcement. These advanced material approaches aim to extend the service life of backsheets beyond the 25-year benchmark while maintaining or improving performance characteristics.

The shift toward fluorine-free backsheet designs reflects both environmental considerations and the development of alternative polymer formulations that can achieve comparable performance without fluoropolymer materials [44]. Co-extruded designs and multi-layer constructions incorporating different polymer types offer opportunities to optimize performance across multiple parameters while potentially reducing material costs and environmental impacts.

## 9. Implications for Module Design and Deployment

### 9.1 Climate-Specific Material Selection

The demonstrated climate dependence of backsheet degradation rates emphasizes the importance of matching material selection to anticipated deployment conditions. For tropical and coastal installations where humidity and temperature cycling create aggressive degradation conditions, backsheet materials with superior moisture barrier properties and enhanced UV resistance should be specified [45]. The higher delamination occurrence rates and annual degradation rates in these regions justify the additional cost of premium backsheet materials.

Desert installations present a different challenge profile, with high UV impact scores (9.5) but lower humidity impact scores (4.5) [46]. In these conditions, UV resistance becomes the primary selection criterion, and backsheet designs can potentially emphasize UV protection over moisture barrier properties. The climate-specific optimization of backsheet selection can improve reliability while potentially reducing material costs by avoiding over-specification for conditions that will not be encountered.

### 9.2 Encapsulant-Backsheet System Design

The interaction between encapsulant and backsheet materials significantly influences overall module reliability, suggesting that system-level design approaches are necessary for optimal performance. The water vapor transmission rate of the backsheet affects moisture ingress rates, which in turn influence EVA hydrolysis and acetic acid generation [47]. This interconnection means that backsheet selection cannot be optimized independently of encapsulant choice.

The ongoing debate regarding optimal backsheet WVTR for tropical areas reflects the complexity of these interactions. One perspective advocates for low WVTR backsheets to minimize moisture ingress and inhibit EVA hydrolysis, while the alternative view favors breathable backsheets that allow moisture to enter while facilitating acetic acid gas release [48]. The resolution of this debate requires consideration of the specific deployment conditions, encapsulant chemistry, and overall module design philosophy.

### 9.3 Quality Assurance and Testing Protocols

The emergence of failure modes in fielded modules despite passing qualification protocols highlights the need for enhanced quality assurance approaches. The limitations of present-day standard accelerated stress tests, which are primarily designed to identify known degradation or failure modes at the time of development, necessitate the development and validation of more comprehensive testing methodologies [49]. Combined-accelerated stress testing approaches that apply multiple simultaneous stressors offer promise for identifying failure modes that escape detection under conventional testing.

Quality assurance programs should incorporate testing protocols that reflect the actual stress profiles expected in deployment locations. For modules destined for tropical or coastal installations, testing protocols should include combined UV and humidity exposure at levels representative of these environments [50]. The correlation between accelerated testing results and field performance should be continuously validated and used to refine testing protocols over time.

## 10. Conclusions

The impact of UV irradiation and humidity cycling on photovoltaic backsheet aging represents a complex interplay of degradation mechanisms that ultimately compromise module sealing integrity and long-term reliability. This investigation has demonstrated that UV radiation induces photochemical degradation through bond cleavage and cross-linking reactions, leading to embrittlement and loss of mechanical properties in backsheet materials. Simultaneously, humidity and elevated temperature drive hygrothermal degradation through hydrolysis mechanisms that further reduce material performance.

The synergistic effects observed when UV exposure and humidity cycling are applied simultaneously create degradation conditions that exceed the sum of individual stress contributions. This synergy manifests through accelerated adhesion degradation at material interfaces, enhanced moisture penetration through UV-damaged layers, and the initiation of failure cascades that ultimately compromise module sealing integrity. The climate-specific degradation patterns observed in tropical and coastal regions, with delamination occurrence rates reaching 35%, underscore the severity of combined UV and humidity stress in these environments.

The validation of combined-accelerated stress testing methodologies against field failure modes provides a pathway for improved material qualification and module design optimization. By applying multiple simultaneous stress factors that replicate natural environmental conditions, these advanced testing protocols can identify failure modes that escape detection under conventional single-stress testing. The continued development and refinement of these methodologies, combined with climate-specific material selection and system-level design approaches, will be essential for ensuring the long-term reliability of photovoltaic modules across the diverse environmental conditions encountered in global deployments.

The findings of this research emphasize that backsheet performance cannot be evaluated in isolation but must be considered as part of an integrated module system where encapsulant selection, edge sealing design, and backsheet properties interact to determine overall reliability. Future advances in backsheet materials and module design should focus on optimizing these system-level interactions while developing testing protocols that adequately replicate the combined stress conditions experienced throughout the module's operational lifetime.
