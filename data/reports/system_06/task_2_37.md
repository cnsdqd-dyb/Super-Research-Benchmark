# Air Circulation Filtration Technology in Space Stations: Trade-off Analysis Between Microbial Contamination Control and Energy Consumption

## Abstract

The maintenance of air quality within sealed space habitats represents one of the most critical challenges in environmental control and life support systems (ECLSS). This research report provides a comprehensive analysis of air circulation filtration technologies employed in space stations, with particular emphasis on the trade-off between microbial contamination control efficacy and energy consumption. Drawing upon recent developments in High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filtration, ultraviolet germicidal irradiation (UVGI), scroll filter systems, and emerging nanofiber membrane technologies, this report evaluates the current state-of-the-art implementations aboard the International Space Station (ISS) while examining advanced solutions proposed for long-duration deep space missions. The analysis reveals that traditional HEPA-based systems, while demonstrating proven reliability with 99.97% to 99.99% efficiency for particles 0.3 μm in diameter, impose significant energy burdens through pressure drop requirements and fan power consumption. Emerging multi-stage filtration architectures incorporating inertial separation, HEPA media, and regenerable components offer promising improvements in the efficiency-microbial control balance, though challenges related to maintenance frequency, filter longevity, and mission logistics continue to drive technological innovation. The findings indicate that optimized systems combining mechanical filtration with complementary technologies such as UVGI achieve net pathogen inactivation rates of 98.5% to 99.9% per pass while minimizing energy penalties associated with high-pressure-drop configurations.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Significance

The confined environment of a space station presents unique challenges for atmospheric management that have no parallel in terrestrial applications. Unlike buildings on Earth, where natural air exchange and gravitational settling provide continuous particulate removal mechanisms, space habitats operate as hermetically sealed systems where no outside air ventilation is possible [1]. This fundamental constraint imposes extraordinary demands on filtration systems, as particulate matter continuously accumulates in the cabin atmosphere without the benefit of gravitational sedimentation that naturally occurs in terrestrial environments. The resulting particulate loading on filtration systems significantly exceeds that encountered in typical terrestrial HEPA applications, creating both performance and energy consumption challenges that must be carefully balanced against microbial contamination control requirements.

The International Space Station, as humanity's longest continuously inhabited space platform, provides an invaluable proving ground for air filtration technologies. The ISS Environmental Control and Life Support system architecture employs a distributed particulate filtration approach utilizing traditional HEPA filters deployed at multiple locations throughout each U.S. Segment module [1]. These filters, designated as Bacterial Filter Elements (BFEs), serve the dual purpose of removing airborne particulate matter and capturing microbial contaminants, thereby protecting both crew health and sensitive scientific experiments from biological contamination. The operational experience gained from decades of ISS filter deployment has generated substantial data regarding filter performance, degradation mechanisms, and replacement intervals, providing a foundation for understanding the trade-offs inherent in space station air filtration design.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 Filtration Mechanisms and Efficiency Metrics

The effectiveness of air filtration systems in controlling microbial contamination depends on the physical mechanisms by which particles are captured from the air stream. For HEPA filtration media, which forms the foundation of current space station air purification, particle capture occurs through a combination of mechanisms including interception, impaction, diffusion, and electrostatic attraction [3]. The relative importance of each mechanism varies with particle size, with the most penetrating particle size (MPPS) typically occurring in the range of 0.1 to 0.3 μm for conventional HEPA media. At this particle size, diffusion mechanisms are not yet dominant, while impaction and interception efficiencies have not reached their maximum values, resulting in a local minimum in overall filtration efficiency.

Traditional HEPA filters are specified to achieve an efficiency of 99.97% for particles 0.3 μm in diameter when tested with dioctyl phthalate (DOP) aerosol per established standards [3]. However, flight-grade HEPA filters aboard the ISS have been found to achieve 99.99% efficiency, a performance improvement that may be attributable to the specific filter media selected for space applications or to the integration of activated charcoal pre-filters in the Charcoal HEPA Integrated Particle Scrubber (CHIPS) configuration [4]. Understanding these efficiency characteristics is essential for evaluating microbial contamination control, as many airborne microorganisms and virus-laden aerosols fall within the size range where filtration efficiency is most critical.

The efficiency of pathogen removal can be significantly enhanced through the integration of multiple technologies in series. Research on advanced air purification systems has demonstrated that combining HEPA filtration with ultraviolet germicidal irradiation achieves net effectiveness of approximately 98.5% to 99.9% per pass [5]. In this configuration, HEPA filters capture at least 95% of viruses-laden aerosols, while UVC radiation inactivates 80% to 99% of residual pathogens based on exposure dose. The multiplicative effect of these technologies provides defense-in-depth against microbial contamination while potentially allowing each individual technology to operate at reduced intensity, thereby conserving energy.

### 2.2 Pressure Drop and Energy Consumption Relationship

The energy consumption of air filtration systems is fundamentally linked to the pressure drop across the filter media. The pressure drop, typically measured in pascals (Pa) or inches of water column (in. H₂O), represents the resistance that the filter imposes on airflow. According to the fundamental relationship for fan power, the power required to move air through a filter is proportional to the product of volumetric flow rate and pressure drop:

$$P = \frac{Q \cdot \Delta P}{\eta}$$

where $P$ is power consumption, $Q$ is volumetric flow rate, $\Delta P$ is pressure drop, and $\eta$ is the overall efficiency of the fan-motor system. For a given required airflow rate to maintain cabin air quality, minimizing pressure drop directly reduces energy consumption, making filter media selection a critical factor in system efficiency.

Qualification testing of ISS Bacterial Filter Elements indicates a typical clean pressure drop of 70 Pa (0.28 in. H₂O) at a flow rate of 119 m³/hr (70 cfm) [6]. This baseline pressure drop increases as the filter accumulates particulate loading during service, eventually reaching a threshold at which replacement is required. The ISS BFE design allows loading with approximately 50 grams of test dust before the allowable pressure drop is exceeded, representing a capacity 56% higher than the specified 32 grams [6]. The relationship between loading and pressure drop is not linear, as particle accumulation can alter the effective filter geometry and potentially increase the rate of pressure rise as the filter approaches the end of its service life.

For space station applications, the energy implications of pressure drop extend beyond direct electrical consumption. Higher pressure drop requirements necessitate more powerful fans, which contribute additional mass to the vehicle—a critical consideration for space missions where launch mass directly affects mission cost and capability. Furthermore, fan noise and vibration, which increase with fan power, can affect crew comfort and potentially interfere with sensitive scientific experiments. Design approaches that reduce pressure drop while maintaining filtration efficiency thus offer benefits across multiple dimensions of system performance.

### 2.3 Microbial Control Requirements and Standards

NASA has established comprehensive technical requirements for environmental control and life support systems through NASA-STD-3001, which defines acceptable limits for various atmospheric parameters including microbial contamination [7]. The technical requirement V2 6059 specifies that the system shall provide air in the habitable atmosphere that is microbiologically safe for human health and performance. Meeting this requirement necessitates filtration systems capable of consistently reducing microbial loads to levels that protect crew health over extended mission durations.

The ISS Program has adapted the Federal Standard 209, Revision E, for a class 100,000 clean room standard as the allowable particulate matter concentration requirement aboard the ISS [8]. This adapted standard maintains airborne particulate matter concentrations in the cabin atmosphere to 0.05 mg/m³ for particles ranging in size from 0.5 μm to 100 μm, with allowable periodic peaks to 1 mg/m³. Providing this level of cleanliness while maintaining safe levels of microbial air contaminants requires HEPA-rated filters with an efficiency of 99.97% for airborne particles 0.3 μm in diameter [8]. This requirement is approximately four times lower than the maximum recommended to maintain crew health, indicating that the ISS filtration system operates with substantial margin for particulate control.

Microbial monitoring performed for the ISS air and water systems provides ongoing assessment of filtration system effectiveness [9]. The data from routine monitoring enables detection of potential process escapes and identification of areas where greater control may be needed. For air revitalization specifically, the ISS experiences high-volume exchange through HEPA filters, which are regularly inspected, vacuumed, and maintained. While these controls provide exceptional microbial control, they do not eliminate all risk, necessitating the continued development of improved filtration technologies and strategies.

## 3. Current Space Station Filtration Technologies

### 3.1 HEPA-Based Bacterial Filter Elements

The current state-of-the-art in spacecraft cabin air filtration is represented by the ISS Bacterial Filter Element, which consists of a HEPA filter with a mesh screen pre-filter attachment [10]. This configuration has demonstrated reliable performance over decades of ISS operation, providing the foundation for microbial contamination control in the station's habitable modules. The distributed deployment strategy places BFEs at multiple locations throughout each U.S. Segment module, ensuring that cabin air receives continuous filtration regardless of the specific module or activity area.

The BFE design reflects careful consideration of the trade-offs between filtration performance and practical operational requirements. The mesh screen pre-filter serves as the first stage of filtration, capturing larger particles and fibrous material that could prematurely clog the more expensive HEPA media. This extendable pre-filter life reduces maintenance frequency and provides a cost-effective approach to managing the high particulate loading characteristic of space station environments. The HEPA media itself provides the high-efficiency removal of smaller particles, including microorganisms, that is essential for crew health protection.

Long-term testing of returned BFEs has provided valuable data regarding filter degradation mechanisms and service life expectations. Research has identified oxidation, loss of volatile constituents in the sealing adhesive, and crystallization of the glass fiber media as potential failure mechanisms for BFEs in service and storage [1]. Testing conducted in 2012 on seven BFEs in controlled storage indicated that performance remained consistent with original acceptance testing for media tensile strength, 0.3-micron particle removal efficiency, random vibration, pressure drop, and proof pressure [1]. Based on these results, the ISS Program increased the total use life (in-service life plus shelf life) from 10 years to 22 years, demonstrating the durability and reliability of the HEPA-based approach.

The operational experience with ISS BFEs has also revealed challenges that must be addressed for future missions. The filters are subjected to larger particulate loading compared to typical terrestrial HEPA applications due to the absence of gravitational sedimentation [1]. Additionally, the accumulation of fire retardant and forever chemical compounds in ISS HEPA filters has been documented, with analysis of dust vacuumed from filters revealing levels comparable to or higher than those found in average U.S. homes [11]. While NASA has confirmed that ISS HEPA filters are working as designed to minimize crew exposure risk, these findings highlight the importance of understanding filter loading characteristics for long-duration missions.

### 3.2 Charcoal HEPA Integrated Particle Scrubber (CHIPS)

The Charcoal HEPA Integrated Particle Scrubber represents an evolution of the basic BFE design that addresses additional air quality concerns beyond particulate filtration. The CHIPS configuration combines activated charcoal filtration for trace contaminant control with HEPA filtration for particulate removal, providing a more comprehensive approach to cabin air purification [4]. This integration is particularly important for addressing volatile organic compounds and siloxanes that may be present in the cabin atmosphere from crew activities, off-gassing of materials, and experimental payloads.

Testing conducted at NASA Glenn Research Center has characterized the efficiency curve and most penetrating particle size of CHIPS filters using standard salt crystal aerosolization [4]. The filters were tested in three configurations: as they are oriented on the ISS (activated charcoal filter followed by flight-grade HEPA filter), with the HEPA portion alone, and with the activated charcoal portion alone. The results provide valuable insight into the contribution of each filter stage to overall performance and the combined effect when operated in series.

The efficiency characteristics of CHIPS filters are particularly relevant for microbiology and Environmental Control and Life Support Systems applications. Understanding the most penetrating particle size in conjunction with levels of airborne microbes enables prediction of which and how many microbes are likely to get through the filtration system [4]. This information is essential for assessing the overall microbial contamination risk and determining whether additional control measures are necessary. The combined charcoal and HEPA configuration may offer advantages for microbial control beyond simple particulate filtration, as the activated charcoal can adsorb certain microbial metabolites and odor-causing compounds that might otherwise support microbial growth within the filtration system or cabin environment.

### 3.3 Ultraviolet Germicidal Irradiation Integration

The integration of ultraviolet germicidal irradiation (UVGI) with mechanical filtration represents a powerful approach to enhancing microbial contamination control while potentially reducing the burden on filter media. UVGI systems utilize ultraviolet radiation in the C band (UVC, wavelengths 200-280 nm) to inactivate microorganisms by causing damage to their DNA and RNA, preventing reproduction and neutralizing their pathogenic potential [12]. This approach provides a complementary mechanism to physical filtration, addressing microorganisms that may already be present in the air stream rather than relying solely on capture.

Research on air purification systems has demonstrated that UVC irradiation can inactivate 80% to 99% of residual pathogens that pass through or escape initial filtration stages, depending on the exposure dose [5]. The exposure dose, measured in μW·s/cm², is the product of UV irradiance and exposure time. For effective pathogen inactivation, sufficient exposure time must be provided, which can be achieved through design features such as low-turbulence ducts with baffles that extend the residence time of air within the UV irradiation zone [5]. Even if airflow passes too quickly through a single pass, multi-pass recirculation in high-air-change-rate environments can compensate for exposure deficiency.

The energy implications of UVGI integration are generally favorable compared to alternatives that might achieve similar microbial control through increased filtration intensity. UV lamps consume relatively modest electrical power compared to the fan energy required to overcome high-pressure-drop filters, and the UVGI system adds minimal pressure drop to the air flow path. However, UVGI effectiveness is highly dependent on proper maintenance, as lamp output degrades over time and quartz sleeves can become coated with deposits that reduce irradiance. For space station applications, where maintenance opportunities may be limited and replacement parts difficult to obtain, the reliability and longevity of UVGI components become critical design considerations.

### 3.4 Airflow Distribution and Circulation Patterns

The effectiveness of filtration systems depends not only on the filter media characteristics but also on the overall airflow distribution and circulation patterns within the cabin. The ISS employs a distributed filtration approach where air flow through BFEs varies from module to module to maintain optimum cabin ventilation characteristics [6]. For example, flow through BFEs in the Destiny laboratory is normally about 113 m³/hr (66.7 cfm), while those in Unity and Quest modules may experience approximately 127 m³/hr (75 cfm) and 85 m³/hr (50 cfm), respectively [6]. This variation ensures that each module receives adequate air changes while accounting for differences in module function, occupancy, and geometry.

The design approach for controlling suspended particulate matter in the ISS cabin is similar to that employed for trace chemical contaminant control, seeking to find a balance between performance, power consumption, and maintainability [6]. The most important design parameters for particulate filtration are nominal filtration efficiency and filter element pressure drop. To achieve the most effective design solution, the selected filter media must provide high single-pass efficiency for the smallest particle size possible while maintaining a characteristically low pressure drop [6]. This balance is essential for minimizing the energy burden of continuous fan operation while ensuring adequate microbial contamination control.

Engineering analysis conducted before flight indicated that even when challenged with very high particulate generation rates on the order of 1.8 × 10⁶ to 5.6 × 10⁶ particles per minute, the specified cabin particulate concentration is maintained with nearly 12 percent margin [6]. These generation rates are 79 to 247 times higher than the generation rate from a single person as defined by the BFE design load model, demonstrating the substantial safety margin built into the ISS filtration system design. However, this margin comes at the cost of potentially conservative filter sizing and associated mass and volume penalties—considerations that become increasingly important for deep space missions where launch mass is at a premium.

## 4. Emerging Technologies and Advanced Approaches

### 4.1 Scroll Filter Technology

NASA is developing an advanced cabin air filtration system known as the Scroll Bacterial Filter Element, which provides up to three stages of filtration in a compact form factor [10]. The Scroll BFE maintains the same form and fit as current ISS BFE filters, enabling potential retrofitting of existing systems while incorporating technological advances developed for deep space exploration applications. This backward compatibility represents an important design feature that reduces the risk associated with technology insertion and enables incremental capability enhancement.

The previous version of the Scroll Filter consisted of two stages, while the current updated design incorporates three main filtration components within a framed assembly resembling the ISS BFE [10]. The filter frame and replaceable cassette architecture facilitates maintenance and reduces the logistics burden associated with complete filter replacement. Instead of indexing or scrolling the filter media as in the full-scale Scroll Filter System, the Scroll BFE scrolls a roll of screen mesh media, incorporating inertial and HEPA filtration along with regenerable and automated maintenance technologies [10].

The regenerable and automated maintenance capabilities of the Scroll Filter System are particularly relevant for long-duration deep space missions where resupply is not available. Travel to Mars, with the shortest mission being 1000 days, will require filtration systems that are truly self-sustaining and almost maintenance-free [10]. The logistical remoteness of these missions and the significant challenges posed by planetary dust, particularly lunar dust, to landers, habitats, and other surface systems will require innovation in filtration technology. Advances are needed to reduce maintenance and increase performance to deal with the increased particle load from dust intrusion during extravehicular activities and habitat pressurization cycles.

Ground development and testing of the Scroll BFE has been ongoing to validate performance and reliability before potential deployment on the ISS or future exploration missions [10]. The filter advances state-of-the-art in spacecraft cabin air filtration by incorporating features relevant for long-duration missions while fitting within the constraint volume available in existing module interfaces. This approach enables technology demonstration on the ISS while maintaining the form factor compatibility that simplifies installation and reduces crew training requirements.

### 4.2 Nanofiber Membrane Technologies

Emerging filtration technologies including nanofiber membranes offer promising alternatives to traditional HEPA media with potential advantages in pressure drop and filtration efficiency [13]. Nanofiber membranes are produced through electrospinning processes that create fibers with diameters in the nanometer range, resulting in filter media with high surface area and small pore sizes. The small fiber diameter enables efficient capture of fine particles while maintaining relatively low pressure drop compared to conventional HEPA media with larger fiber diameters.

Research has demonstrated that nanofiber-based filters can achieve higher filtration efficiencies for sub-micron particles while exhibiting lower pressure drop characteristics than traditional glass fiber HEPA media [13]. This combination of high efficiency and low pressure drop directly addresses the fundamental trade-off between microbial contamination control and energy consumption. For space station applications, where fan power consumption contributes to overall vehicle energy budget and higher pressure drop filters require more massive fan systems, the potential benefits of nanofiber technology are substantial.

However, challenges related to cost and scalability currently limit the adoption of nanofiber membranes for space station applications [13]. The electrospinning production process is more complex and expensive than the wet-laid or melt-blown processes used for conventional HEPA media, and producing the large filter formats required for space station applications at consistent quality presents manufacturing challenges. Additionally, the long-term stability of nanofiber materials in the space station environment, including resistance to radiation damage and thermal cycling, requires further investigation before operational deployment.

### 4.3 Biofiltration Systems

Biofiltration systems represent an alternative approach to air purification that utilizes biological processes to degrade airborne contaminants [13]. In these systems, contaminated air passes through a moist medium colonized by microorganisms that metabolize organic compounds, converting them to carbon dioxide, water, and biomass. For space station applications, biofiltration could potentially address both particulate matter and gaseous contaminants simultaneously, reducing the number of separate systems required and simplifying overall ECLSS architecture.

The potential advantages of biofiltration for space stations include lower pressure drop operation, reduced energy consumption, and the ability to handle a broad spectrum of contaminants [13]. The biological processes operate at ambient temperatures without requiring the energy input for thermal oxidation or the electrical power for UV radiation. Additionally, biofiltration systems can potentially achieve steady-state operation where microbial populations adapt to the incoming contaminant load, maintaining performance over extended periods without media replacement.

The application of biofiltration to space station environments faces significant challenges, however. The closed nature of the space station habitat means that any microorganisms that escape the biofilter could colonize other surfaces and potentially create health risks for crew members. Maintaining the proper moisture and nutrient conditions for biofilter operation in microgravity presents engineering challenges, and the response of biofilter microbial communities to the space environment, including altered gravity and elevated radiation levels, is not well characterized. These factors currently limit the near-term applicability of biofiltration for primary air purification in space stations, though continued research may identify niche applications where biofiltration could complement mechanical filtration systems.

## 5. Comparative Trade-off Analysis

### 5.1 Efficiency-Energy Performance Matrix

Evaluating the trade-off between microbial contamination control and energy consumption requires systematic comparison of the technologies discussed in preceding sections. The primary metrics for this comparison include single-pass filtration efficiency for relevant microbial sizes, pressure drop at rated airflow, and the resulting energy consumption for fan operation. Secondary considerations include filter service life, maintenance frequency, and system complexity that may indirectly affect energy requirements through operational constraints.

Traditional HEPA filters in the BFE configuration achieve 99.97% efficiency for 0.3 μm particles, with ISS flight-grade filters demonstrating up to 99.99% efficiency [1][4]. The clean pressure drop of approximately 70 Pa at 119 m³/hr establishes the baseline energy consumption for fan operation [6]. As filters load with particulate matter during service, pressure drop increases, requiring either increased fan speed to maintain flow rate or reduced flow as the filter approaches replacement threshold. The 50-gram dust loading capacity provides substantial service life, but the increasing pressure drop throughout the service period means that energy consumption is not constant over the filter's operational lifetime.

The integration of UVGI with HEPA filtration enhances microbial control to 98.5% to 99.9% net effectiveness per pass while adding minimal pressure drop to the system [5]. The energy consumption of UV lamps is typically modest compared to fan power, making this an efficient approach to improving microbial control. However, the effectiveness of UVGI depends on achieving adequate exposure dose, which requires either low-turbulence duct design to extend residence time or multi-pass recirculation to accumulate exposure over multiple cycles. In high-air-change-rate environments typical of space stations, the multi-pass approach can effectively compensate for short single-pass exposure times.

### 5.2 Mission Duration Considerations

The optimal trade-off between microbial control and energy consumption varies significantly depending on mission duration. For short-duration missions, maximizing microbial control efficiency may justify higher energy consumption, as the total energy burden over the mission is limited and the consequences of microbial contamination are unacceptable regardless of mission length. For long-duration missions, however, the cumulative energy consumption of filtration systems becomes a significant factor in overall mission resource planning, and technologies that reduce energy consumption per unit time may prove advantageous even if they provide somewhat lower single-pass efficiency.

The ISS experience with BFE filters demonstrates that traditional HEPA filtration can provide reliable service for periods exceeding 2.5 years with returned filters showing consistent performance [1]. The extension of total use life to 22 years based on storage testing indicates that the filter media itself is capable of long-duration operation when properly protected [1]. However, the actual in-service life may be limited by particulate loading rather than media degradation, and the increasing pressure drop as loading accumulates means that energy consumption increases throughout the service period.

For deep space missions to Mars, the Scroll Filter technology with regenerable and automated maintenance capabilities offers potential advantages over traditional replaceable filters [10]. The ability to clean or regenerate filter media in situ eliminates the logistics burden of carrying replacement filters for the entire mission and avoids the increasing energy consumption associated with loaded filters. However, the regenerable filter system itself may consume energy for the regeneration process, and the complexity of moving parts and control systems introduces potential failure modes that must be carefully evaluated against the benefits of reduced logistics requirements.

### 5.3 Maintenance and Logistics Trade-offs

The frequency of filter maintenance and replacement represents a significant factor in the overall efficiency of space station air filtration systems. Each replacement operation requires crew time, generates waste material that must be stored or disposed of, and introduces the risk of contamination exposure during filter handling. For missions far from Earth, where resupply is not available and waste disposal options are limited, reducing maintenance frequency becomes as important as minimizing energy consumption.

Traditional BFE filters on the ISS require regular inspection and vacuuming to remove accumulated debris from the exterior surface [9]. The mesh screen pre-filter extends the service life of the HEPA media by capturing larger particles before they reach the high-efficiency media. However, complete filter replacement is required when pressure drop exceeds allowable limits, generating a spent filter that must be stored or processed as waste. Over the lifetime of the ISS program, the accumulation of spent filters has created significant storage requirements and logistical complexity.

The Scroll Filter System addresses maintenance and logistics concerns through its regenerable design [10]. Instead of replacing spent filters, the system can clean the filter media through mechanical or other processes that restore performance without requiring media replacement. This approach dramatically reduces the logistics burden for long-duration missions, as only the initial filter media need be launched and no waste filter material accumulates. The energy cost of regeneration must be weighed against the savings in launch mass and crew time, but for missions where resupply is impossible, the regenerable approach may prove essential.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Implications for Current Space Station Operations

The analysis presented in this report has immediate implications for the operation of existing space stations, including the ISS and potential future commercial space habitats. The energy consumption associated with these systems, while not negligible, represents an acceptable trade-off given the reliability and simplicity of the technology.

However, opportunities exist to optimize current operations through enhanced integration of complementary technologies. The experience gained during the COVID-19 pandemic has heightened awareness of airborne disease transmission risks, and space agencies have appropriately considered these risks in crew health protection protocols. Multi-stage filtration with UVGI provides defense-in-depth that addresses limitations of either technology operating alone.

Improved monitoring and predictive maintenance could also enhance the efficiency of current filtration operations. Analysis of pressure drop trends and microbial sampling results could enable optimization of filter replacement timing, reducing the risk of operating beyond filter capacity while avoiding premature replacement that wastes filter media and crew time. The integration of AI-driven analytics, while representing a significant investment in data infrastructure and algorithm development, could provide substantial returns in operational efficiency over the remaining ISS lifetime and future space habitat operations.

### 6.2 Design Considerations for Future Space Habitats

For future space habitats, including lunar surface bases and Mars transit vehicles, the trade-off analysis presented in this report supports several design recommendations. First, the proven reliability of HEPA-based filtration should be maintained as the foundation of the filtration system, as the consequences of filtration failure in deep space missions are severe and the track record of HEPA technology in space applications is excellent. However, the integration of complementary technologies can enhance performance while managing energy consumption within acceptable bounds.

The multi-stage approach exemplified by the Scroll Filter design, incorporating inertial separation, HEPA filtration, and regenerable media technologies, offers a promising path forward for exploration missions [10]. The inertial separation stage removes larger particles with minimal pressure drop, extending the service life of the HEPA media and reducing the frequency of maintenance operations. The regenerable capability addresses the logistics challenges of long-duration missions where resupply is unavailable. The automated maintenance features reduce crew time requirements for filter upkeep, freeing crew resources for other mission activities.

The selection of specific technologies and configurations should be guided by detailed mission requirements analysis, including crew size, mission duration, expected particulate loading from extravehicular activities and dust intrusion, and available power generation capacity. Missions with abundant power generation capacity might prioritize maximum microbial control efficiency, accepting higher energy consumption to minimize contamination risk. Missions with constrained power budgets might accept somewhat lower single-pass efficiency in exchange for reduced energy consumption, relying on higher air change rates and multi-pass exposure to achieve equivalent overall microbial control.

### 6.3 Research Gaps and Future Investigation Needs

Despite the substantial body of operational experience and research reviewed in this report, several gaps remain in understanding the optimal trade-off between microbial contamination control and energy consumption for space station air filtration. The long-term efficacy of UVGI systems against emerging pathogens requires further investigation, as the mutation and adaptation of microorganisms could potentially reduce the effectiveness of UV inactivation over extended mission durations [5]. Testing with a broader range of pathogens, including those that might be carried by crew members or introduced through scientific experiments, would strengthen confidence in UVGI-based approaches.

The integration of advanced features such as real-time air quality monitoring and automated operation could improve user convenience and system efficiency, but these enhancements must align with cost constraints to maintain affordability [5]. For space applications, the cost constraint is replaced by launch mass and volume constraints, but the principle remains relevant—advanced features must justify their contribution to system mass and complexity. Standardized frameworks for evaluating the energy efficiency of space station air filters and their impact on overall system performance would facilitate comparison of alternative technologies and support informed design decisions.

The effects of the space environment on advanced filtration materials, including nanofiber membranes and biofilter media, require additional investigation. Exposure to elevated radiation levels, thermal cycling between sun and shadow, and the unique conditions of microgravity could affect material properties and filtration performance in ways not fully characterized by ground-based testing. Long-duration exposure experiments, potentially utilizing facilities on the ISS or future platforms, would provide valuable data for validating the suitability of advanced materials for exploration missions.

The clean pressure drop of approximately 70 Pa at rated airflow establishes a baseline energy consumption that grows throughout the filter service life as particulate loading increases resistance to airflow.

The integration of ultraviolet germicidal irradiation with HEPA filtration offers an efficient approach to enhancing microbial control without proportional energy penalties. Research demonstrates that combined HEPA-UVGI systems achieve net pathogen inactivation rates of 98.5% to 99.9% per pass, with UVGI adding minimal pressure drop while providing inactivation of microorganisms that escape physical capture [5]. This synergistic approach provides defense-in-depth that addresses limitations of either technology alone while maintaining favorable energy efficiency.

For long-duration deep space missions, the Scroll Filter technology incorporating multi-stage filtration with regenerable and automated maintenance capabilities addresses the critical logistics challenges that would otherwise limit traditional filter-based approaches [10]. The ability to regenerate filter media in situ eliminates the need to carry replacement filters for the entire mission and avoids the accumulating energy consumption associated with loaded filters. While this technology is not yet flight-qualified, its development represents an essential step toward enabling sustainable human presence beyond low Earth orbit.

The optimal trade-off between microbial control and energy consumption ultimately depends on mission-specific requirements, including crew size, mission duration, power generation capacity, and acceptable risk levels. For current space station operations, the proven HEPA-based systems should continue to serve as the foundation, with opportunities for enhancement through UVGI integration and improved monitoring. For future exploration missions, multi-stage architectures incorporating inertial separation, HEPA filtration, and regenerable technologies offer the best prospects for balancing microbial control efficacy with energy and logistics constraints. Continued research into advanced materials, AI integration, and space-environment effects on filtration performance will further inform technology selection and system design for the next generation of space habitats.
