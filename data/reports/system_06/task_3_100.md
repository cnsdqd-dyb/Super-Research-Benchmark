# How Does Genome Editing Ethics Rebuild Cross-Species Justice Foundations Beyond Anthropocentrism?

## Abstract

The emergence of CRISPR and other genome editing technologies has precipitated profound ethical questions that extend far beyond traditional human-centered frameworks. This report examines how emerging ethical approaches in genome editing governance are actively reconstructing the foundations of cross-species justice by challenging deeply entrenched anthropocentric assumptions. Drawing on diverse philosophical traditions—including Indian Hindu philosophy, Indigenous ethical systems, and relational ethics frameworks—this analysis explores how non-Western philosophical perspectives offer critical alternatives to dominant Western bioethical paradigms. The report argues that genuine cross-species justice requires a fundamental reconceptualization of moral consideration, moving from instrumental valuations of non-human life toward recognition of intrinsic value across all species. Furthermore, it examines how inclusive deliberation, global governance frameworks, and pluralistic ethical approaches can facilitate more just and sustainable applications of genome editing technologies. By synthesizing insights from bioethics, environmental philosophy, and governance studies, this report contributes to the developing discourse on how genome editing ethics can serve as a catalyst for rebuilding ethical relationships between humans and non-human species.

## 1. Introduction

The advent of precise genome editing technologies, particularly CRISPR-Cas9, has fundamentally transformed humanity's capacity to modify the genetic material of living organisms. This technological capability extends across an unprecedented spectrum of applications, from therapeutic interventions in human genetics to modifications in agricultural systems, environmental conservation efforts, and fundamental research on non-human organisms. However, alongside these remarkable capabilities comes a corresponding responsibility to develop ethical frameworks capable of guiding the just and sustainable use of such powerful technologies.

Traditional bioethical discourse has been predominantly shaped by Western philosophical traditions, which have historically positioned human beings as the central—or exclusive—subjects of moral consideration. This anthropocentric orientation, while providing certain foundations for human rights and dignity, proves inadequate when confronting the complex ethical terrain opened by genome editing technologies that affect organisms across the entire tree of life. The capacity to modify the genomes of non-human animals, plants, and microorganisms raises fundamental questions about our moral obligations to these species and the ecosystems they comprise.

The challenge confronting contemporary bioethics is not merely to extend existing human-centered frameworks to encompass non-human entities, but rather to fundamentally reconceptualize the ethical foundations upon which our relationships with other species are based. This reconceptualization requires engaging with philosophical traditions that have never accepted the sharp demarcation between human and non-human realms that characterizes much of Western thought. Indian Hindu philosophy, with its non-dualistic understanding of reality, offers one such alternative framework, presenting an integrated view of humans as part of, rather than separate from, the natural world [1].

This report examines how genome editing ethics can serve as a catalyst for rebuilding cross-species justice foundations by drawing on these diverse philosophical resources. It argues that the development of genuinely just approaches to genome editing requires moving beyond anthropocentrism toward ethical frameworks that recognize the intrinsic value and moral standing of non-human species. Such a transformation demands not merely technical adjustments to existing governance frameworks, but a fundamental shift in the philosophical foundations upon which those frameworks rest.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of Non-Anthropocentric Ethics

### 2.1 The Problem of Anthropocentrism in Bioethics

Anthropocentrism, the view that human beings constitute the primary or exclusive focus of moral consideration, has been the dominant paradigm in Western ethical thought for centuries. Within this framework, non-human entities are valued primarily—or exclusively—instrumentally, insofar as they serve human interests or contribute to human flourishing. This orientation has profoundly shaped the development of bioethics, which emerged as a discipline primarily concerned with ethical questions arising in medical and research contexts involving human subjects.

The limitations of anthropocentrism become particularly apparent when considering the ethical implications of genome editing technologies. The capacity to modify the genetic material of non-human organisms raises questions that cannot be adequately addressed within a strictly human-centered ethical framework. When we consider whether it is permissible to edit the genomes of non-human primates for research purposes, whether we should resurrect extinct species through genetic technologies, or whether we might engineer ecosystems through the release of gene-edited organisms, we encounter the inadequacy of ethical frameworks that recognize no moral standing in non-human entities independent of their utility to humans.

As scholars have noted, anthropocentrism represents one of the major challenges facing environmental ethics, as it positions non-human entities at the periphery—or entirely outside—the moral sphere [2]. This positioning has significant practical consequences for how genome editing technologies are governed and deployed. When regulatory frameworks are premised on the assumption that only human interests matter morally, the interests of non-human species and ecosystems are systematically discounted or ignored entirely.

### 2.2 Beyond Anthropocentrism: Sentiocentrism, Biocentrism, and Ecocentrism

Environmental ethics has developed several influential alternatives to anthropocentrism, each extending the scope of moral consideration in different ways. Sentiocentrism grounds moral standing in the capacity for suffering, extending moral consideration to all sentient beings regardless of their species membership. This approach recognizes that the capacity for pleasure and pain, rather than species membership itself, constitutes the relevant criterion for moral concern.

Biocentrism goes further, arguing that all living beings possess inherent worth by virtue of being teleological centers of life with their own goods and purposes. Within this framework, each organism has a good of its own that deserves moral consideration, regardless of whether it possesses the capacity for suffering. This perspective recognizes that living beings have interests in their own survival, health, and flourishing that do not depend on their utility to other species.

Ecocentrism represents the most expansive extension of moral consideration, encompassing the biotic community as a whole as a subject of moral concern. This holistic approach recognizes value not only in individual organisms but also in the relationships and systems that comprise ecological communities. As one scholar notes, ecocentrism values both the organic and inorganic parts of nature intrinsically, recognizing that the flourishing of individual organisms depends upon the health and integrity of the ecosystems within which they exist [2].

These non-anthropocentric frameworks provide essential conceptual resources for developing ethical approaches to genome editing that take seriously the interests of non-human species and ecological systems. Rather than treating non-human organisms merely as raw material for human manipulation, these frameworks suggest that genome editing technologies should be deployed in ways that respect the intrinsic value and moral standing of all living beings.

### 2.3 Relational Ethics and Cross-Species Justice

Relational ethics offers another important perspective on cross-species justice that transcends the limitations of both anthropocentrism and individualistic conceptions of moral standing. Rather than focusing on the inherent properties of individual entities, relational ethics emphasizes the web of relationships that connect all beings and the mutual dependencies that characterize life on Earth. This approach is particularly well-suited to addressing the ethical challenges posed by genome editing, which affects not only individual organisms but also the relationships and systems that connect them.

Among European scholars, Peter Kropotkin's work on mutual aid as a factor of evolution has been influential in articulating how relational ethics operate in nature, demonstrating that many interspecies relationships are based upon principles of reciprocity that allow for mutual flourishing [3]. This observation challenges the view of nature as merely a realm of competition and suggests that human relationships with other species should be guided by similar principles of reciprocity and mutual benefit.

Indigenous ethical systems offer particularly rich resources for relational approaches to cross-species justice. Many Traditional Ecological Knowledge systems recognize the moral significance of relationships between humans and other species, viewing these relationships as characterized by reciprocal obligations and responsibilities. These frameworks understand humans as part of, rather than separate from, the natural world, and they emphasize the importance of maintaining balanced and harmonious relationships with other species and ecological systems [3].

The Earth Charter articulates a vision of governance that recognizes the interdependence of species and the value of all living things regardless of their utility for human animals [4]. This document provides a framework for building a just, sustainable, and peaceful global society that takes seriously the interests of all species. Such frameworks suggest that the essential purpose of human governance systems should be to support people in playing mutually enhancing roles within the community of life on Earth, rather than allowing the subordination of other species to human interests.

## 3. Indian Philosophical Contributions to Genome Editing Ethics

### 3.1 Non-Dualism and the Integration of Human and Natural Worlds

Indian Hindu philosophy offers a particularly rich resource for developing non-anthropocentric approaches to genome editing ethics. Central to many schools of Hindu thought is the concept of non-dualism (advaita), which challenges the sharp separation between self and other, human and nature, that characterizes much of Western thought. This philosophical orientation presents a more integrated view of humans as part of the natural world, rather than as separate from or superior to it [1].

The non-dualistic perspective has profound implications for how we understand our relationships with other species. If the boundaries between self and other are ultimately illusory, then the exploitation or instrumentalization of other beings for human purposes becomes difficult to justify on philosophical grounds. This does not necessarily imply that all use of other species is impermissible, but it does suggest that such use should be guided by principles of respect, reciprocity, and minimization of harm rather than by assumptions of human dominion over nature.

The integrated view of humans as part of the natural world also challenges the dualistic thinking that underlies many ethical concerns in synthetic biology and genome editing. The distinction between the "artificial" and the "natural," between "man" and "machine," creates false dichotomies that obscure the fundamental continuity between human beings and the rest of nature. By recognizing this continuity, Indian philosophy offers resources for developing more nuanced and integrated approaches to the ethical governance of genome editing technologies.

### 3.2 Pluralism and the Recognition of Multiple Perspectives

Another important contribution of Indian philosophy to genome editing ethics is its emphasis on pluralism and the recognition of multiple valid perspectives on reality. The celebrated Rig Vedic verse "Ekam sad viprā bahudhā vadanti" — "Truth is one; the wise describe it in many ways" — encapsulates a foundational principle of Indian thought: the recognition of a singular underlying reality expressed through multiple perspectives [1].

This pluralistic ethos has significant implications for global governance of genome editing technologies. Rather than imposing a single ethical framework developed in one cultural context upon the entire world, this perspective suggests that diverse cultural and philosophical traditions should contribute to the development of ethical norms and governance structures. Such an approach would recognize that different societies may have legitimate reasons for approaching genome editing from different perspectives, and it would seek to integrate diverse insights rather than imposing monocultural solutions.

In contemporary discourse, especially in fields like bioethics and philosophy of science, this pluralistic framework offers a critical alternative to dominant Western paradigms by emphasizing multiplicity, relationality, and inner transformation as essential components of ethical deliberation [1]. Rather than treating technical expertise as the sole source of ethical authority, this approach recognizes that ethical wisdom is distributed across diverse cultures and communities, and that inclusive deliberation is essential for developing norms that can command global legitimacy.

### 3.3 Inner Transformation and Ethical Development

Indian philosophy also emphasizes the importance of inner transformation as a component of ethical development. This focus on the cultivation of virtues and the transformation of consciousness suggests that addressing the ethical challenges posed by genome editing technologies requires not merely the development of external regulations but also the cultivation of wisdom, compassion, and respect among scientists, policymakers, and citizens.

This perspective challenges the view that ethical governance of genome editing can be achieved solely through external constraints and oversight mechanisms. While such mechanisms are certainly necessary, they must be complemented by efforts to cultivate the moral qualities that enable individuals and institutions to make wise and responsible decisions about the use of these powerful technologies. The development of ethical frameworks for genome editing should therefore include attention to the formation of character and the cultivation of wisdom, not merely the establishment of rules and procedures.

## 4. Cross-Species Justice Framework for Genome Editing

### 4.1 Reconceptualizing Moral Standing Beyond Humanity

The development of a genuine cross-species justice framework for genome editing requires fundamental reconceptualization of moral standing. Traditional approaches to bioethics have grounded moral standing in properties that humans uniquely possess, such as rationality, autonomy, or moral agency. This approach systematically excludes non-human entities from the moral sphere, treating them as mere resources for human use rather than as subjects of moral concern in their own right.

A cross-species justice framework requires moving beyond these property-based conceptions of moral standing toward relational or status-based approaches that recognize the moral significance of all living beings regardless of their possession of particular properties. This does not necessarily mean that all entities possess identical moral status, but it does mean that all entities with interests of their own deserve some degree of moral consideration. The challenge is to develop principles that can guide the balancing of competing interests across species in ways that are just and respectful of the inherent worth of all life.

The concept of intrinsic value provides one foundation for such a framework. Intrinsic value refers to the value that something possesses in virtue of what it is in itself, rather than in virtue of its usefulness to others. If non-human species possess intrinsic value, then they deserve moral consideration independent of their utility to human beings. This recognition has significant implications for genome editing governance, suggesting that decisions about whether and how to modify the genomes of non-human organisms should take into account the interests of those organisms themselves, not merely the interests of human beings who might benefit from such modifications.

### 4.2 Balancing Human and Non-Human Interests

The development of cross-species justice frameworks must grapple with the reality that human and non-human interests will sometimes conflict. In such cases, how should we balance competing claims? One approach emphasizes the development of principles that minimize harm to all affected parties, recognizing that all beings with interests deserve consideration of those interests in ethical deliberation.

The last person argument provides one foundation for non-anthropocentric ethics by demonstrating the inadequacy of purely human-centered value systems. If the last surviving human were to destroy all other life, such destruction would be permissible only within a framework that recognizes no value independent of human interests. The intuition that such destruction would be wrong even if no human were harmed suggests that we do recognize value in non-human entities that transcends their utility to us [2].

In the context of genome editing, this suggests that decisions about modifying non-human organisms should involve careful consideration of the impacts on those organisms and on the ecological systems of which they are part. While human interests may sometimes justify interventions in non-human genomes, such interventions should not be undertaken lightly, and should be guided by principles of respect, minimization of harm, and recognition of the intrinsic value of non-human life.

### 4.3 Interspecies Justice and Ecosystem Integrity

A genuinely cross-species justice framework must also attend to the integrity of ecosystems as wholes, not merely to the interests of individual organisms. Ecological systems are characterized by complex webs of relationships and dependencies, and interventions in one component can have cascading effects throughout the system. Genome editing technologies have the potential to affect not only individual organisms but also the ecological relationships and processes that sustain life.

The concept of ecosystem integrity provides important guidance for the responsible use of genome editing technologies. Interventions should be designed and evaluated with attention to their effects on ecological processes, biodiversity, and the long-term health of ecosystems. This requires moving beyond individual-level considerations to encompass system-level impacts and long-term consequences.

The governance of genome editing should therefore incorporate ecological expertise and perspectives that can assess the potential impacts of proposed interventions on ecosystem integrity. Such governance should also be precautionary, recognizing that ecological systems are complex and that interventions may have unintended consequences that are difficult to predict or control. The burden of proof should be on those proposing interventions to demonstrate that they will not cause significant harm to ecological systems and the species that depend upon them.

## 5. Genome Editing: Ethical Challenges and Opportunities

### 5.1 Safety and Risk Considerations

Safety concerns represent one of the primary ethical considerations in genome editing applications. The possibility of off-target effects, where edits occur in unintended locations in the genome, and mosaicism, where some cells carry the edit while others do not, raises significant concerns about the safety of genome editing interventions [5]. These concerns are particularly acute in the context of germline editing, where changes can be transmitted to future generations, and where the potential for harm extends to individuals who cannot consent to the risks.

The ethical consensus among researchers and ethicists who have addressed these questions is that germline genome editing should not be used for clinical reproductive purposes until it is deemed safe through research, and that the risks cannot be justified by potential benefits until that threshold is met [5]. This precautionary approach recognizes both the potential benefits of the technology and the profound risks associated with premature clinical application.

However, safety considerations extend beyond human applications to encompass impacts on non-human species and ecosystems. The release of gene-edited organisms into the environment raises concerns about potential ecological impacts that may be difficult to predict or control. Gene drives, which are designed to spread genetic modifications through populations, raise particular concerns about unintended ecological consequences that could affect entire species and ecosystems.

### 5.2 Instrumentalization and Animal Welfare

Genome editing technologies can contribute to the instrumentalization of animals for human interests in problematic ways. The use of genome editing to create animal models for research, to enhance agricultural productivity, or to produce pharmaceuticals raises fundamental questions about the moral status of non-human animals and the extent to which their interests can be legitimately subordinated to human purposes.

On the other hand, genome editing could potentially be used to decrease animal suffering, for example by reducing the occurrence of unwanted genetic effects in research animals or by reducing the number of animals needed to create animal model systems compared to traditional methods [6]. These potential benefits must be weighed against concerns that increased efficiency in the use of animals for research could perpetuate and even expand the use of animals in scientific experimentation.

The moral relevance of non-human organisms in genome editing contexts derives from universal relations, since all organisms are connected and the earth itself can be considered a living system that has achieved homeostasis equilibrium [7]. Under this eco-centric view, nature is a unitary being and any organism is defined through its relations with other organisms and the environment, giving them moral relevance that must be considered in decisions about genome editing.

### 5.3 Conservation and Ecological Applications

Genome editing technologies offer both opportunities and challenges for conservation and ecological applications. These technologies have been proposed as means to understand and preserve corals and their ecosystems, to diversify agriculture to shore up food security, to combat invasive species plaguing ecosystems around the world, and even to resurrect extinct species [8]. Each of these applications raises distinct ethical considerations that must be weighed against the potential benefits.

The use of genome editing for conservation purposes requires careful consideration of the goals and values that guide conservation efforts. If conservation is understood solely in instrumental terms—as protecting biodiversity for the sake of human interests—then different ethical conclusions follow than if biodiversity is recognized as possessing intrinsic value. The debates surrounding genome editing applications in conservation therefore reflect deeper disagreements about the value of nature and humanity's relationship to the natural world.

The potential to resurrect extinct species through genome editing raises particularly complex ethical questions. The revived animals may suffer as a result of the processes used or because of their particular genomic variations, and the re-created species may become vectors or reservoirs for viruses that can be harmful to other animals [6]. These concerns must be weighed against the potential benefits of restoring lost species and ecosystems.

## 6. Governance and Regulatory Considerations

### 6.1 The Need for Inclusive Deliberation

The governance of genome editing technologies requires inclusive deliberation that can confer democratic legitimacy on decisions affecting the future of humanity and other species [9]. Exclusive reliance on expert-driven processes, while potentially efficient, risks reproducing technocratic forms of decision-making that fail to engage the full range of stakeholders and perspectives that should inform ethical governance of these technologies.

Inclusive deliberation requires the involvement of diverse stakeholders, including not only scientists and policymakers but also representatives of civil society, indigenous communities, and other groups whose lives may be affected by genome editing applications. Such deliberation should take into account not only technical considerations but also ethical, social, and cultural dimensions of proposed applications. The goal should be to develop governance frameworks that reflect a broad consensus on acceptable uses of these technologies, rather than imposing the views of technical experts on the broader public.

The pluralistic ethos that characterizes Indian philosophy suggests that such deliberation should be genuinely open to diverse perspectives and should seek to integrate insights from multiple philosophical and cultural traditions. Rather than treating Western bioethical frameworks as universal norms to be imposed on all societies, inclusive deliberation should recognize the legitimacy of diverse approaches to ethical questions and should seek to develop governance frameworks that can accommodate this diversity.

### 6.2 Global Coordination and Equity

The international scope of biological research and the global nature of the challenges posed by genome editing technologies require coordinated governance at the international level. The rapid development and diffusion of genome editing techniques has been enabled by the international character of research, which transcends national interests and communicates through a common language [10]. This international character of research creates both opportunities and challenges for governance.

On one hand, international coordination can help ensure that genome editing technologies are developed and deployed in ways that benefit all of humanity and that risks are managed responsibly. On the other hand, the global nature of research and development creates challenges for governance, as different jurisdictions may have different regulatory frameworks and ethical standards. This can create regulatory arbitrage, where activities migrate to jurisdictions with weaker oversight, and can undermine efforts to establish consistent global standards.

Equity considerations are particularly important in the global governance of genome editing. The development of these technologies has largely been concentrated in wealthy, developed nations, raising concerns about access and benefit-sharing. There is a clear need to include lower-income countries in decision-making about genome editing governance and to ensure equitable access to the technology globally [11]. International organizations such as the United Nations can coordinate efforts and establish universal ethical standards for genome editing use.

### 6.3 Regulatory Approaches Across Jurisdictions

Regulatory approaches to genome editing vary widely across jurisdictions, reflecting differing cultural values, legal traditions, and ethical frameworks. In the United States, CRISPR-based applications are primarily regulated by the Food and Drug Administration and other agencies, with different regulatory pathways depending on the intended use [12]. The European Union is developing harmonized genomic regulations that aim to balance scientific innovation with strong ethical safeguards.

These differing regulatory approaches reflect deeper disagreements about the appropriate relationship between scientific innovation and ethical oversight. Some jurisdictions have adopted relatively permissive regulatory frameworks that emphasize innovation and allow substantial freedom for research and development. Others have adopted more restrictive approaches that prioritize precaution and require extensive oversight before new applications can be deployed.

The diversity of regulatory approaches creates both challenges and opportunities for global governance. On one hand, the lack of harmonization can create confusion and can allow activities to migrate to jurisdictions with weaker oversight. On the other hand, the diversity of approaches can serve as a form of experimentalism, allowing different jurisdictions to try different approaches and providing opportunities to learn from experience. The challenge is to develop mechanisms for coordination and information-sharing that can capture the benefits of regulatory diversity while minimizing the risks of regulatory arbitrage and inadequate oversight.

## 7. Challenges and Future Directions

### 7.1 Technical Uncertainty and Precaution

One of the fundamental challenges facing the governance of genome editing technologies is the technical uncertainty surrounding their effects. The complexity of biological systems means that interventions in genomes can have unintended consequences that are difficult to predict or control. This uncertainty is compounded by the potential for effects to propagate through ecological systems in ways that are difficult to anticipate.

The precautionary principle provides important guidance for governing technologies under conditions of uncertainty. This principle suggests that when potential harms are severe and scientific understanding is incomplete, caution should be exercised and potentially harmful activities should be restrained until more is known about their effects. In the context of genome editing, this suggests that applications with potential for significant harm should be subject to rigorous assessment before deployment, and that novel applications should be approached with caution.

However, the precautionary principle must be balanced against the potential benefits of genome editing technologies. Excessive caution could foreclose opportunities to address pressing problems in human health, agriculture, and environmental conservation. The challenge is to develop governance frameworks that are appropriately precautionary without being so restrictive that they prevent the realization of beneficial applications.

### 7.2 Balancing Innovation and Oversight

The governance of genome editing must balance the need for oversight with the benefits of innovation. Excessive regulatory burden can slow the development of beneficial applications and can drive research and development to jurisdictions with weaker oversight. At the same time, inadequate oversight can allow harmful applications to proceed and can erode public trust in the technology and the institutions that govern it.

Adaptive governance approaches that can respond to new information and changing circumstances are particularly important for governing rapidly evolving technologies like genome editing. Such approaches should include mechanisms for ongoing assessment of risks and benefits, for incorporating new scientific understanding into regulatory decisions, and for adjusting regulatory frameworks as needed. They should also include mechanisms for public engagement and democratic deliberation that can ensure that regulatory decisions reflect societal values and priorities.

The development of responsible integration of genome editing technology demands transparent oversight, broad stakeholder engagement, and adaptive legal mechanisms to harness benefits while safeguarding against harms [12]. This requires governance frameworks that are both rigorous and flexible, capable of ensuring safety and ethical compliance while allowing beneficial innovation to proceed.

### 7.3 Integrating Diverse Knowledge Systems

The development of genuinely just and effective governance frameworks for genome editing requires the integration of diverse knowledge systems, including not only Western scientific and bioethical traditions but also Indigenous knowledge systems and non-Western philosophical frameworks. Many Traditional Ecological Knowledge systems offer rich insights into sustainable human-environment relationships and the ethical treatment of other species that can inform governance of genome editing.

Indigenous frameworks for relational ethics in land-based design offer particularly valuable resources for developing approaches to genome editing that understand humans as part of the natural world rather than as separate from or superior to it [3]. These frameworks emphasize the importance of community building, interspecies engagements, and intergenerational conversations at the center of governance. They also emphasize the importance of spiritual frameworks and the restoration of biocultural practices that have sustained harmonious relationships between humans and other species for millennia.

The integration of diverse knowledge systems into genome editing governance requires genuine openness to perspectives that differ from dominant Western paradigms. It also requires mechanisms for ensuring that marginalized voices are heard and that their perspectives are genuinely incorporated into decision-making processes. This is not merely a matter of procedural fairness but of substantive improvement in the quality of governance, as diverse perspectives can identify risks and opportunities that might be missed by more homogeneous groups.

## 8. Conclusion

The development of genome editing ethics offers an unprecedented opportunity to rebuild the foundations of cross-species justice on more adequate philosophical grounds. The challenges posed by these powerful technologies demand ethical frameworks that transcend the limitations of anthropocentrism and recognize the moral significance of non-human species and ecological systems. Such frameworks must draw on diverse philosophical traditions, including Indian Hindu philosophy with its non-dualistic understanding of reality, Indigenous ethical systems with their emphasis on relationality and reciprocity, and Western environmental philosophy with its development of biocentric and ecocentric ethics.

The reconstruction of cross-species justice foundations requires not merely the extension of existing human-centered frameworks to encompass non-human entities, but a fundamental reconceptualization of the relationships between humans and other species. This reconceptualization must recognize the intrinsic value of non-human life, the importance of ecological integrity, and the mutual dependencies that characterize life on Earth. It must also recognize the legitimacy of diverse perspectives on ethical questions and the importance of inclusive deliberation in governance.

The practical implementation of cross-species justice in genome editing governance will require significant changes in how decisions are made about the development and deployment of these technologies. It will require the development of regulatory frameworks that take seriously the interests of non-human species and ecosystems, that are grounded in precautionary principles, and that ensure equitable participation in decision-making by all affected parties. It will also require the cultivation of wisdom, compassion, and respect among scientists, policymakers, and citizens, so that these powerful technologies are used in ways that enhance the flourishing of all life on Earth.

The path forward is neither simple nor assured. The tensions between human interests and non-human interests, between innovation and precaution, and between global coordination and local autonomy will continue to challenge efforts to develop just and effective governance frameworks. However, the recognition that such challenges exist and the commitment to addressing them through inclusive deliberation and philosophical reflection provide grounds for optimism that genome editing ethics can serve as a catalyst for building a more just relationship between humans and the rest of nature.

As the Rig Vedic verse reminds us, truth is one but the wise describe it in many ways. The development of cross-species justice in the age of genome editing will require drawing on the wisdom of diverse traditions, integrating their insights into governance frameworks that can guide the responsible use of these powerful technologies. In this endeavor, the goal should not be the imposition of a single ethical framework but the development of pluralistic approaches that can accommodate diverse perspectives while ensuring that the interests of all species are taken seriously in decisions about the future of life on Earth.
