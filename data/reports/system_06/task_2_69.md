# Balancing Therapeutic Response and Seizure Risk in Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation for Depression: A Clinical Analysis

## Abstract

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has emerged as a transformative neuromodulation therapy for treatment-resistant depression, offering a non-invasive alternative to electroconvulsive therapy with a favorable safety profile. However, the clinical application of rTMS requires careful navigation of stimulation parameters that optimize therapeutic efficacy while maintaining safety within established seizure risk boundaries. This report synthesizes current evidence examining the indications for rTMS in depression treatment, with particular emphasis on how stimulation frequency, intensity, coil geometry, and patient-specific factors interact to influence both therapeutic outcomes and seizure risk. The analysis reveals that low-frequency protocols (≤1 Hz) present the most favorable safety profile with seizure rates approaching zero per thousand sessions, while high-frequency protocols (≥5 Hz) and deep stimulation coils such as H-coils demonstrate elevated but still clinically acceptable risk profiles. Evidence-based patient selection, rigorous pre-treatment screening using validated instruments, and adherence to established safety guidelines enable clinicians to maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing adverse neurological events. The findings support rTMS as a evidence-based intervention for major depressive disorder, particularly in treatment-resistant populations, when appropriate protocols are selected and safety boundaries are respected.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Clinical Context

Major depressive disorder (MDD) represents one of the leading causes of disability worldwide, affecting hundreds of millions of individuals and imposing substantial burden on healthcare systems, workplaces, and personal quality of life. Despite the availability of multiple pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions, a significant proportion of patients fail to achieve adequate response to first-line treatments. Treatment-resistant depression (TRD), typically defined as failure to respond to adequate trials of multiple antidepressant medications, affects between 15% and 30% of patients with MDD and presents considerable therapeutic challenges. This treatment-resistant subset has driven the development of alternative somatic treatments, among which repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation has achieved prominence as a evidence-based neuromodulation approach.

The conceptual foundation for electromagnetic stimulation of the brain dates to the early twentieth century, with psychiatrists Pollacsek and Beer filing a patent in 1902 for an electromagnetic device designed to treat depression and neuroses. However, the modern era of transcranial magnetic stimulation began in 1985 when Barker and colleagues in Sheffield, England, developed a focal electromagnetic device capable of inducing currents in the human nervous system. This technological breakthrough enabled direct, non-invasive stimulation of the brain, launching a new paradigm in neuropsychiatric treatment that has evolved over subsequent decades into the sophisticated rTMS protocols employed in contemporary clinical practice.

### 1.2 The Imperative for Safety-Efficacy Balance

The clinical utility of rTMS for depression hinges on achieving an optimal balance between therapeutic efficacy and safety, with seizure risk representing the primary neurological safety concern requiring careful management. Unlike pharmacological interventions that carry risks of systemic side effects and drug interactions, rTMS exerts its effects through electromagnetic induction of neural activity, with safety profile heavily determined by stimulation parameters including frequency, intensity, train duration, and inter-train intervals. The fundamental challenge for clinicians lies in selecting stimulation parameters that achieve sufficient cortical excitation to produce antidepressant effects while remaining within established safety boundaries that minimize the probability of seizure induction.

This balance is particularly critical given the patient population for whom rTMS is typically indicated. Patients with TRD have often exhausted multiple pharmacological options and may be vulnerable to medication-related adverse effects. The appeal of rTMS lies partly in its non-invasive nature and favorable side effect profile compared to electroconvulsive therapy, yet this advantage must be preserved through rigorous attention to safety parameters. Understanding the specific factors that influence both therapeutic response and seizure risk enables evidence-based clinical decision-making that optimizes outcomes for individual patients.

### 1.3 Scope and Objectives

This report examines the current evidence regarding indications for rTMS in depression treatment, with systematic analysis of how therapeutic response thresholds relate to seizure risk safety boundaries. The primary objectives include: (1) characterizing the relationship between stimulation parameters and both efficacy and safety outcomes; (2) identifying patient-specific factors that modify seizure risk; (3) evaluating evidence from meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials comparing different stimulation protocols; and (4) synthesizing clinical guidelines and practical recommendations for balancing therapeutic benefit with safety considerations.

## 2. Mechanisms of Action and Stimulation Parameters

### 2.1 Neurophysiological Basis of rTMS Effects

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation produces therapeutic effects in depression through modulation of neural activity in prefrontal cortical regions, particularly the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). The electromagnetic coil generates rapidly changing magnetic fields that penetrate the skull and induce electrical currents in underlying cortical tissue, thereby altering neuronal excitability and functional connectivity. The direction and magnitude of these effects depend critically on stimulation parameters, with different frequency ranges producing distinct neurophysiological consequences.

High-frequency rTMS (typically 5-20 Hz) generally produces excitatory effects on targeted cortical regions, increasing neural firing rates and potentially promoting neuroplastic changes associated with antidepressant response. This excitatory modulation is thought to counteract the hypoactivity observed in left prefrontal regions among patients with depression. Conversely, low-frequency rTMS (≤1 Hz) typically produces inhibitory effects, which may be therapeutically relevant when applied to right prefrontal regions or when global cortical excitability requires suppression. The theta burst stimulation paradigm, delivered as intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS), represents an alternative approach that mimics natural theta rhythms and produces excitatory effects with substantially reduced treatment duration.

### 2.2 Frequency Parameters and Their Impact

The frequency of rTMS delivery represents perhaps the most critical parameter influencing both therapeutic efficacy and seizure risk. Clinical evidence consistently demonstrates that high-frequency stimulation protocols (≥5 Hz) are associated with greater seizure risk compared to low-frequency protocols, yet may offer enhanced cognitive benefits and potentially superior antidepressant effects in certain patient subgroups. This frequency-dependent risk profile reflects the neurophysiological principle that higher-frequency stimulation produces greater cumulative depolarization of cortical neurons, approaching thresholds for synchronous discharge that may manifest as seizure activity.

Low-frequency rTMS (≤1 Hz) typically presents a more favorable safety profile with respect to seizure risk, with meta-analytic data indicating seizure rates approaching zero per thousand sessions in protocols without elevated risk factors. The inhibitory effects of low-frequency stimulation may be therapeutically valuable in specific clinical scenarios, particularly when targeting brain regions where excitation might be undesirable or when treating patients with elevated baseline seizure risk. However, the therapeutic effects of low-frequency protocols may differ qualitatively from those of high-frequency stimulation, necessitating careful consideration of clinical objectives when selecting stimulation frequency.

The intermediate frequency range, particularly theta frequencies (4-7 Hz) delivered in burst patterns, offers a potentially optimal balance for some clinical applications. Intermittent theta burst stimulation, for example, delivers trains of three pulses at 50 Hz repeated at 5 Hz intervals, producing excitatory effects while substantially reducing total treatment time. Clinical trials have demonstrated comparable efficacy between iTBS and conventional high-frequency rTMS, with meta-analytic evidence indicating no significant differences in response rate (odds ratio 0.97, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.16, p = 0.75) or remission rate (odds ratio 1.06, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.31, p = 0.62) between these approaches. This equivalence, combined with the practical advantage of shorter daily treatment sessions, positions iTBS as a valuable option for balancing therapeutic efficiency with safety considerations.

### 2.3 Intensity and Motor Threshold Determination

Stimulation intensity, measured as a percentage of individual motor threshold, constitutes a second critical parameter influencing both efficacy and safety outcomes. Motor threshold is defined as the stimulator output that produces visible thumb movement in 50% of trials when applied over the motor cortex, and serves as an individual reference point for calibrating treatment intensity. Resting motor threshold (RMT) is determined during the initial rTMS session using single magnetic pulses over the motor cortex, with this measurement used to establish treatment levels that minimize seizure risk while providing adequate cortical stimulation.

Standard clinical protocols typically employ intensities ranging from 80% to 120% of motor threshold, with higher intensities potentially enhancing therapeutic effects through greater cortical penetration and neuronal activation. However, this efficacy advantage comes with corresponding increases in discomfort and seizure risk. The relationship between intensity and seizure risk is not strictly linear, but clinical evidence suggests that intensities at the upper end of the therapeutic range warrant additional caution, particularly when combined with high frequencies or prolonged train durations.

Individual variation in cortical excitability and seizure threshold necessitates personalized intensity determination rather than fixed-dose approaches. Factors influencing individual motor threshold include cortical anatomy, skull thickness, and genetic variation in neuronal membrane properties. The practice of individual threshold determination represents a fundamental safety measure that adapts stimulation parameters to each patient's neurophysiological characteristics, thereby optimizing the therapeutic index.

### 2.4 Coil Geometry and Depth of Stimulation

The geometric configuration of the stimulation coil significantly influences both the focality and depth of induced electrical fields, with implications for both therapeutic targeting and seizure risk. Conventional figure-of-eight coils produce relatively focal stimulation with shallow penetration, making them suitable for targeting specific cortical regions while minimizing activation of deeper structures. Round coils produce less focal but more widespread stimulation patterns, while specialized deep TMS coils such as H-coils are designed to reach deeper brain structures with less focal precision.

Evidence regarding seizure risk by coil type indicates meaningful variation across coil geometries. Meta-analytic data examining seizure incidence across 318,560 TMS sessions revealed risk rates of 0.08 per 1,000 sessions for conventional figure-of-eight or round coils, 0.12 per 1,000 sessions for double cone coils, and 0.43 per 1,000 sessions for H-coils. The elevated risk associated with H-coils reflects their capacity to induce electrical fields in deeper cortical and subcortical structures, potentially including regions with lower seizure thresholds or networks involved in seizure propagation. Double cone coils, which are designed to stimulate deeper structures such as the supplementary motor area, were associated with one seizure in the analyzed dataset.

These findings suggest that coil selection should consider not only therapeutic targeting requirements but also individual patient risk profiles. For patients with elevated baseline seizure risk, conventional coils with shallower penetration may offer advantages despite potentially requiring more precise anatomical targeting. Conversely, when deeper structures represent legitimate therapeutic targets and patient risk profiles are favorable, deep TMS coils may be employed with appropriate monitoring and precautions.

## 3. Seizure Risk: Epidemiology and Risk Factors

### 3.1 Incidence and Clinical Characteristics

The overall incidence of rTMS-induced seizures represents a critical consideration for clinical practice, with extensive data now available from large-scale clinical trials and real-world treatment cohorts. Contemporary evidence indicates that the risk of rTMS-induced seizures under ordinary clinical use with figure-of-eight coils is approximately 1 in 30,000 treatments (0.003%), a rate that compares favorably with seizure risks associated with many antidepressant medications. This low baseline incidence reflects the cumulative effect of safety guidelines, individual threshold determination, and conservative parameter selection that characterize contemporary clinical practice.

When seizures do occur during rTMS treatment, they demonstrate characteristic features that distinguish them from spontaneous epileptic seizures. All reported rTMS seizures have occurred during stimulation rather than in delayed fashion, and all have been self-limited with no reported sequelae. This pattern suggests that rTMS-induced seizures are acute physiological responses to cortical stimulation rather than events reflecting underlying epileptogenic processes. The immediate occurrence during stimulation enables rapid intervention and typically results in complete resolution without lasting neurological consequences.

The distribution of seizure risk across stimulation protocols reveals important patterns for clinical risk stratification. Analysis of 318,560 total TMS sessions, of which 19,308 (6%) involved delivery parameters outside 2009 guideline recommendations, demonstrated that sessions conducted without elevated protocol or subject risk showed seizure rates ranging from 0 for low-frequency rTMS (≤1 Hz), iTBS, and continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS), to 0.03 per 1,000 for single/paired-pulse protocols, and 0.32 per 1,000 for high-frequency rTMS conducted with H-coils. These data establish a hierarchy of risk that can inform protocol selection and patient counseling.

### 3.2 Protocol-Related Risk Factors

Certain stimulation parameters and protocols carry elevated seizure risk that necessitates additional clinical caution. High-frequency rTMS (>1 Hz) demonstrates higher seizure incidence compared to low-frequency protocols, with risk concentrated in sessions employing parameters that exceed established safety guidelines. The 2009 international consensus guidelines established safety limits for high-frequency stimulation, including maximum train duration, inter-train interval, and total pulses per session, based on empirical data regarding seizure thresholds.

Sessions conducted with elevated protocol risk, defined as parameter values exceeding guideline recommendations, showed substantially elevated seizure incidence. Among sessions with elevated protocol risk, seizure rates reached 0.82 per 1,000 for single/paired-pulse protocols, 0.58 per 1,000 for high-frequency rTMS, and 0.55 per 1,000 for intermittent theta burst stimulation. These rates, while still representing low absolute risk, are approximately ten-fold higher than rates observed with guideline-concordant parameters, underscoring the importance of adherence to established safety limits.

The combination of elevated protocol risk with elevated subject risk produces multiplicative effects on seizure incidence. High-frequency rTMS conducted with H-coils in subjects with additional risk factors demonstrated seizure rates of 6.13 per 1,000, substantially exceeding rates observed with either factor alone. This interaction emphasizes the importance of comprehensive risk assessment that considers both protocol characteristics and individual patient factors when estimating seizure probability.

### 3.3 Patient-Related Risk Factors

Individual patient characteristics significantly modify seizure risk during rTMS treatment, necessitating systematic pre-treatment screening to identify elevated-risk patients. The pre-rTMS evaluation should systematically assess risk factors associated with seizure induction during high-frequency rTMS, including personal or family history of epilepsy or seizure, past stroke or head injury with neurological sequelae, concurrent use of medications or substances that lower seizure threshold, and presence of neurological disorders or medical conditions associated with lowered seizure threshold.

Personal history of epilepsy represents the strongest individual risk factor, with seizure thresholds substantially reduced in patients with established epileptic disorders. Family history of seizure disorders suggests possible genetic predisposition to lowered seizure threshold, warranting additional caution even in the absence of personal seizure history. Structural brain abnormalities including prior stroke, traumatic brain injury, brain tumors, and active central nervous system infections create localized areas of altered neuronal excitability that may be more susceptible to ictal activation during rTMS.

Medication effects on seizure threshold require careful review prior to rTMS treatment. Several psychotropic medications lower seizure threshold, including certain antidepressants (particularly bupropion and high-dose imipramine), antipsychotics (particularly clozapine), and stimulants. Conversely, dose reduction or discontinuation of medications with anticonvulsant properties, such as benzodiazepines, may transiently lower seizure threshold. Substance use, including alcohol and recreational drugs, can similarly modify seizure risk, with documented cases of rTMS-related seizures occurring in patients with recent heavy alcohol use.

Additional medical conditions influencing seizure risk include sleep deprivation, increased intracranial pressure, electrolyte imbalances, and withdrawal states from substances of abuse. These factors are often modifiable and should be addressed prior to rTMS treatment when possible. The TMS Adult Safety Screen (TASS) and similar validated instruments provide systematic frameworks for quantifying cumulative seizure risk and informing treatment decisions.

### 3.4 Special Populations and Considerations

Certain patient populations warrant modified approaches to rTMS treatment due to altered risk-benefit profiles. Patients with brain trauma, tumors, or active infections require special consideration due to increased seizure risk during transcranial magnetic stimulation procedures. The presence of structural brain lesions may create areas of hyperexcitability that respond abnormally to electromagnetic stimulation, potentially lowering the threshold for seizure generation.

Pediatric and adolescent populations have been studied less extensively than adults, but available evidence suggests that rTMS and other non-invasive brain stimulation techniques show good safety and tolerability in these groups, with low dropout rates and mostly mild, self-limiting adverse events. However, developing brains may have different seizure thresholds than adult brains, and the long-term effects of rTMS on neurodevelopment remain incompletely characterized. The potential for enhanced neuroplasticity in younger patients may also influence therapeutic response patterns, with some evidence suggesting particularly promising remission rates with iTBS in adolescents and young adults.

Elderly patients present distinct considerations related to age-related neuroanatomical changes that may affect both stimulation distribution and seizure risk. Cortical atrophy, common in aging, increases the distance between the stimulation coil and target cortical regions, potentially requiring intensity adjustments to achieve adequate cortical activation. The maintenance of efficacy in middle-aged and elderly cohorts supports clinical utility across the lifespan, though age-related changes may necessitate parameter optimization to balance efficacy and safety.

## 4. Therapeutic Efficacy and Response Thresholds

### 4.1 Clinical Evidence for Antidepressant Efficacy

The therapeutic efficacy of rTMS for major depressive disorder has been established through extensive randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, and real-world effectiveness studies. Meta-analytic evidence demonstrates robust efficacy with large effect sizes on core depressive symptoms, coupled with significantly improved response and remission rates that exceed those typically observed with first-line antidepressant medications. These findings hold particular significance for patients with TRD, who have often failed multiple pharmacological trials and have limited alternative treatment options.

Response rates to rTMS in real-world settings approximate 50% among patients with treatment-resistant depression, with remission occurring in approximately 30-40% of patients. These outcomes compare favorably with response rates observed with subsequent pharmacological trials in similar patient populations and suggest that rTMS offers meaningful therapeutic benefit for patients who have not responded to conventional treatments. The durability of rTMS antidepressant effects, once achieved, appears comparable to that of other antidepressant interventions, with effects lasting weeks to months following completion of acute treatment courses.

Comparative meta-analyses demonstrate that both iTBS and high-frequency rTMS produce significantly higher response rates than sham stimulation, with odds ratios of 4.84 (95% CI 2.66 to 8.80, p < 0.001) for iTBS versus sham and 3.85 (95% CI 2.08 to 7.13, p < 0.001) for high-frequency rTMS versus sham. These effect sizes represent clinically meaningful benefits and establish rTMS as an evidence-based intervention rather than an experimental approach. The comparable efficacy between iTBS and conventional high-frequency rTMS provides clinical flexibility, allowing treatment duration optimization without sacrificing therapeutic benefit.

### 4.2 Protocol-Specific Efficacy Patterns

Different rTMS protocols demonstrate varying efficacy patterns that may inform individualized treatment selection. High-frequency rTMS applied to the left DLPFC represents the most extensively studied protocol, with consistent evidence of antidepressant efficacy across multiple trials and meta-analyses. The standard FDA-cleared protocols employ 10 Hz stimulation at 120% of motor threshold, with 4-second trains and 26-second inter-train intervals, totaling 75 trains per session over approximately 37.5 minutes.

Low-frequency rTMS applied to the right DLPFC offers an alternative approach with potentially different therapeutic mechanisms. While less extensively studied than left high-frequency protocols, right-sided low-frequency stimulation may be particularly relevant for patients who do not respond to excitatory left-sided protocols or who present with specific symptom profiles suggesting right-hemisphere dysfunction. The favorable safety profile of low-frequency stimulation makes it attractive for patients with elevated seizure risk who might not tolerate high-frequency protocols.

Intermittent theta burst stimulation has emerged as an efficient alternative to conventional high-frequency protocols, with equivalent efficacy demonstrated across multiple randomized trials and meta-analyses. The iTBS protocol delivers 600 pulses in approximately 3 minutes compared to 37.5 minutes for conventional 10 Hz protocols, representing a substantial reduction in treatment session duration. This efficiency advantage has important implications for patient convenience, treatment accessibility, and healthcare resource utilization, without apparent sacrifice of therapeutic efficacy.

### 4.3 Factors Influencing Therapeutic Response

Multiple factors influence individual response to rTMS treatment, enabling potential prediction of treatment outcomes and optimization of protocol selection. Treatment resistance severity appears to modify response, with some evidence suggesting greater relative benefit in patients with moderate compared to severe treatment resistance. However, substantial response and remission rates are observed even in stringently defined TRD populations, supporting meaningful utility across the treatment resistance spectrum.

Demographic and clinical factors may influence response patterns, though findings across studies have been inconsistent. Evidence suggests that iTBS demonstrates particularly promising remission rates in adolescents and young adults, with odds ratios of 2.68 compared to sham treatment. This finding is clinically significant given limited approved treatment options and safety concerns with antidepressant medications in youth populations. The maintenance of efficacy in middle-aged and elderly cohorts supports broad clinical applicability, though age-related neuroanatomical changes may necessitate parameter optimization.

Concomitant medication use may influence rTMS response, with some evidence suggesting greater efficacy when rTMS is employed as monotherapy compared to augmentation of existing antidepressant treatment. Meta-analytic findings indicate substantially higher odds of response with monotherapy (OR = 27.94, 95% CI: 4.3-181.53) versus augmentation strategy (OR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.17-4.6). However, these findings must be interpreted cautiously given methodological limitations in the monotherapy evidence base, and many patients require continuation of existing medications during rTMS treatment for clinical stability.

## 5. Clinical Guidelines and Safety Protocols

### 5.1 International Safety Guidelines

The evolution of rTMS safety guidelines reflects accumulating clinical experience and systematic analysis of adverse event data. The 2009 international consensus guidelines established foundational safety parameters based on empirical data regarding seizure thresholds, recommending limits on train duration, inter-train interval, and total session pulses for different frequency ranges. These guidelines have served as the primary reference for clinical practice and research protocols, with high rates of adherence in contemporary treatment settings.

Guideline-adherent treatment demonstrates substantially lower seizure incidence compared to treatments exceeding recommended parameters. Among 318,560 TMS sessions analyzed in large-scale safety studies, only 6% involved delivery parameter values outside 2009 guideline recommendations, and these elevated-protocol-risk sessions resulted in only one seizure. This finding suggests that guideline adherence effectively minimizes seizure risk even when individual patient risk factors are present, though comprehensive risk assessment should consider both protocol and patient factors.

Updated guidelines incorporating evidence from subsequent clinical experience and novel protocols such as theta burst stimulation have been developed by professional societies including the American Psychiatric Association, the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry Task Force, the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments, and the International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology. These guidelines uniformly recognize rTMS as a clinically relevant technique for treating major depression, including treatment-resistant presentations, while emphasizing the importance of appropriate patient selection, staff training, and treatment monitoring.

### 5.2 Pre-Treatment Assessment and Screening

Systematic pre-treatment assessment represents a critical component of rTMS safety protocols, enabling identification of patients with elevated seizure risk and informing appropriate protocol selection. The pre-rTMS evaluation should include comprehensive assessment of seizure risk factors using structured instruments such as the TMS Adult Safety Screen (TASS), which systematically queries personal and family history of seizures, neurological conditions, medications, and other relevant factors.

Patients meeting MRI safety criteria are typically considered appropriate candidates for transcranial magnetic stimulation, as the magnetic field characteristics of rTMS are similar to those employed in diagnostic MRI. Contraindications generally include ferromagnetic implants in or near the head, severe traumatic brain injury with retained metallic fragments, and other conditions that would preclude MRI scanning. Patients with brain trauma, tumors, or active infections require special consideration due to increased seizure risk, with individualized risk-benefit assessment necessary to determine appropriate treatment approaches.

Medication review should identify agents that modify seizure threshold, including antidepressants with pro-convulsant properties, antipsychotics, stimulants, and other psychotropic medications. Decisions regarding medication continuation or modification during rTMS treatment should balance seizure risk against the potential for psychiatric decompensation if effective treatments are discontinued. In many cases, careful monitoring allows continuation of necessary medications while proceeding with rTMS treatment using appropriate safety precautions.

### 5.3 Treatment Monitoring and Emergency Protocols

Ongoing monitoring during rTMS treatment sessions enables rapid identification and management of any adverse events. Treatment should be administered in settings with appropriate emergency equipment and staff trained in seizure management, even though the overwhelming majority of sessions proceed without incident. The fact that all reported rTMS seizures have occurred during stimulation and been self-limited underscores the importance of real-time monitoring while also providing reassurance regarding the typically benign course of any events that do occur.

Patients should be monitored for signs of seizure activity during treatment sessions, with treatment immediately discontinued if any concerning symptoms emerge. Following any suspected seizure event, comprehensive neurological evaluation and reconsideration of future rTMS treatment is warranted, though the self-limited nature of rTMS-induced seizures and absence of reported sequelae suggest that future treatment may be appropriate in carefully selected cases following risk-benefit assessment.

Documentation of treatment parameters, patient tolerance, and any adverse events enables ongoing safety monitoring and quality improvement. Accumulation of real-world safety data through registries and clinical databases continues to refine understanding of risk profiles and inform guideline updates, emphasizing the importance of systematic adverse event reporting in clinical practice.

## 6. Balancing Therapeutic Response with Seizure Safety

### 6.1 Risk-Benefit Framework

The clinical application of rTMS for depression requires systematic consideration of both expected therapeutic benefits and potential risks, with particular attention to seizure probability and consequences. For most patients with treatment-resistant depression, the favorable benefit-risk profile strongly supports rTMS treatment, with response rates of approximately 50% and seizure risks well below those associated with many alternative treatments. The non-invasive nature of rTMS and absence of systemic side effects distinguish it from pharmacological approaches and electroconvulsive therapy, offering advantages for patients concerned about these aspects of alternative treatments.

Quantification of risk-benefit ratios enables informed decision-making at both individual and policy levels. The seizure risk of approximately 0.003% with conventional figure-of-eight coil protocols compares favorably with seizure risks associated with many antidepressant medications, which range from 0.1% to 2% depending on the specific agent and patient population. For patients with TRD who have failed multiple pharmacological trials, the potential for meaningful antidepressant response with rTMS substantially outweighs the low probability of seizure, particularly when appropriate protocols and safety measures are employed.

Individual variation in risk profiles necessitates personalized assessment rather than uniform treatment approaches. Patients with multiple seizure risk factors may warrant modified protocols, such as low-frequency stimulation or conventional rather than deep coils, that reduce seizure probability even if potentially at some cost to theoretical maximum efficacy. Conversely, patients with favorable risk profiles and severe, treatment-resistant depression may appropriately receive more intensive protocols targeting maximum therapeutic response.

### 6.2 Protocol Selection Strategies

Evidence-based protocol selection balances therapeutic objectives with safety considerations, with multiple viable approaches available for different clinical scenarios. For patients with standard risk profiles and treatment-resistant depression, conventional high-frequency left DLPFC rTMS or iTBS represent appropriate first-line options with established efficacy and favorable safety. The choice between these protocols may be influenced by practical considerations including treatment session duration, with iTBS offering substantial time savings without efficacy sacrifice.

For patients with elevated seizure risk, low-frequency rTMS protocols offer the most favorable safety profile with demonstrated antidepressant efficacy. The seizure rate of approximately 0.03 per 1,000 sessions for low-frequency protocols without elevated risk factors represents the lowest risk among established treatment approaches, making this option attractive for risk-averse patients or those with concerning risk factor profiles. The therapeutic equivalence of low-frequency right DLPFC stimulation to left high-frequency protocols in some studies supports its viability as a primary treatment approach.

Deep TMS with H-coils may be indicated when therapeutic targets include deeper brain structures not adequately reached by conventional coils, but the elevated seizure risk (0.43 per 1,000 sessions) warrants careful consideration. This approach may be most appropriate when specific clinical indications support deep targeting and when patient risk profiles are favorable. The availability of alternative protocols with lower seizure risk should inform decisions regarding H-coil utilization.

### 6.3 Maintenance and Relapse Prevention

The durability of rTMS antidepressant effects and strategies for relapse prevention represent important considerations for long-term management. Evidence suggests that rTMS effects, once achieved, are as durable as those of other antidepressant interventions, with maintenance strategies including continuation pharmacotherapy and maintenance rTMS sessions. The Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments guidelines assign level 3 evidence to maintenance rTMS as a relapse prevention strategy, reflecting emerging but not yet definitive evidence.

Maintenance rTMS protocols typically involve less frequent treatment sessions, often weekly or biweekly, following completion of acute treatment courses. These protocols generally employ the same stimulation parameters as acute treatment, with safety considerations similar to those during acute treatment phases. The lower frequency of maintenance sessions reduces cumulative seizure risk even if the per-session risk remains constant, supporting the long-term safety of ongoing rTMS treatment when indicated.

Integration of rTMS with pharmacological treatment may optimize long-term outcomes, with evidence suggesting that continuation of antidepressant medication following successful rTMS treatment reduces relapse risk. The combination of neuromodulation and pharmacotherapy may produce synergistic effects that neither approach achieves alone, though comparative effectiveness data regarding specific combinations remain limited.

## 7. Future Directions and Research Priorities

### 7.1 Protocol Optimization

Ongoing research focuses on optimizing rTMS protocols to maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing adverse effects. Protocol optimization studies examining variations in stimulation parameters, treatment scheduling, and targeting approaches continue to refine understanding of dose-response relationships. The development of personalized stimulation protocols based on individual neurophysiological characteristics represents a promising direction that may improve outcomes while reducing unnecessary exposure to intensive stimulation.

Biomarker development for predicting treatment response and adverse effects represents a priority research area. Neuroimaging markers of cortical excitability, connectivity patterns, and neurochemical status may enable identification of patients most likely to benefit from specific protocols. Electroencephalogram (EEG)-synchronized TMS represents an innovative approach that may enhance targeting precision and therapeutic effects, with preliminary evidence suggesting active stimulation significantly outperforms sham in reducing depression severity.

Comparative effectiveness research examining different protocols, treatment sequences, and combination approaches will inform evidence-based treatment algorithms. The relative merits of different protocols across patient subgroups, including variations in illness chronicity, comorbidities, and treatment history, require systematic investigation to enable truly personalized treatment selection.

### 7.2 Safety Refinement

Continued refinement of safety protocols and risk stratification approaches will further reduce adverse event rates while maintaining therapeutic efficacy. Enhanced screening tools incorporating genetic, neuroimaging, and neurophysiological markers may enable more precise identification of elevated-risk patients. Real-world safety data collection through registries and electronic health records will continue to characterize risk profiles across diverse patient populations and treatment settings.

Novel approaches to seizure risk reduction, including pharmacological prophylaxis in high-risk patients and adaptive stimulation protocols that respond to real-time neurophysiological markers, represent potential future developments. The development of coil designs that maximize therapeutic targeting while minimizing activation of seizure-prone networks may reduce risk without sacrificing efficacy.

Long-term safety monitoring following rTMS treatment, including assessment of cognitive effects, structural brain changes, and quality of life outcomes, will continue to characterize the safety profile beyond acute treatment phases. The accumulation of such data across large patient populations will enable increasingly precise risk-benefit assessment and informed consent processes.

## 8. Conclusion

The clinical application of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation for treatment-resistant depression represents a carefully balanced endeavor that must navigate the interplay between therapeutic efficacy and seizure risk safety boundaries. Contemporary evidence supports rTMS as an effective and evidence-based treatment for major depressive disorder, with response rates of approximately 50% in real-world settings and remission rates of 30-40%, offering meaningful benefit for patients who have failed to respond to conventional pharmacological approaches.

The seizure risk associated with rTMS treatment is low but not negligible, ranging from approximately 0.03 per 1,000 sessions for low-frequency protocols to 0.43 per 1,000 sessions for high-frequency protocols delivered with deep H-coils. This risk hierarchy enables informed protocol selection that matches treatment intensity to individual patient risk profiles. The overwhelming majority of seizures that do occur are self-limited with no lasting consequences, and the overall safety profile compares favorably with alternative treatments including many antidepressant medications.

Evidence-based clinical practice requires systematic pre-treatment assessment to identify patient-specific risk factors, adherence to established safety guidelines regarding stimulation parameters, and ongoing monitoring during treatment sessions. The availability of multiple stimulation protocols, including conventional high-frequency rTMS, theta burst stimulation, and low-frequency approaches, provides clinical flexibility to optimize the therapeutic index for individual patients.

The future of rTMS for depression lies in continued protocol optimization, development of predictive biomarkers for treatment selection, and refinement of safety approaches that further minimize adverse events while preserving therapeutic benefit. As the evidence base continues to expand and clinical experience accumulates, the balance between therapeutic response and seizure safety will become increasingly refined, enabling more precise and personalized application of this valuable neuromodulation technology.
