## 2. Geometric Foundations of Classical Ballet

### 2.1 Historical Origins of Geometric Principles in Ballet

The geometric dimension of ballet has deep historical roots extending to the Renaissance period, when the art form first emerged in the courts of European nobility. The earliest ballet performances, such as the sixteenth-century *Le Balet Comique de la Reine* by Balthazar Beaujoyeulx, incorporated dozens of geometric figures involving triangles, circles, and squares, chosen for their geometric proportions and spatial configurations [1]. These early ballets were heavily influenced by the writings of Pythagoras and Plato, representing the cosmic and heavenly significance of numbers and geometry. The dancers performed elaborate patterns that embodied mathematical harmony, connecting earthly movement to celestial order.

This geometric heritage reflects the broader aesthetic values of the Renaissance period, which emphasized harmony, balance, and form as fundamental principles governing both art and nature. In ballet, these values manifested through what scholars have termed "the geometry of classical ballet," which is expressed through lightness, effortless ease, and control with a state of balance and structural clarity [2]. The aesthetic of classical ballet thus emerged not merely as arbitrary artistic convention but as a deliberate embodiment of mathematical principles understood to reflect universal harmony.

### 2.2 Ballet Positions as Geometric Glyphs

Classical ballet positions and movements can be understood as a systematic vocabulary of geometric configurations. The codified positions of the feet, arms, and body create what one choreographer describes as "something extremely alphabetical about traditional ballet figures and positions—they resemble glyphs" [3]. This characterization illuminates the fundamental nature of classical ballet as a language of spatial relationships, where each position carries specific geometric meaning and can be combined with others according to规则的 rules to create complex expressions.

The geometric precision of classical ballet serves a crucial functional purpose beyond aesthetic appeal. The simplicity of classical ballet positions is precisely what enables them to be reproduced with such ease across generations of dancers [3]. This reproducibility depends on the geometric clarity of each position—the precise angles of the feet in first position, the specific curve of the arms in fifth position, the exact relationship of the body to the supporting leg in an arabesque. Each position constitutes a stable geometric configuration that can be reliably taught, learned, and executed.

The geometric nature of ballet positions becomes particularly evident when analyzed in terms of curves and lines. When examining the basic ballet position where the hands are held over the head, one realizes that there are two curves involved, one on the right and the other on the left [3]. These curves form the foundation for what can serve as a "keyframe" in choreographic development—a starting point from which innumerable transformations can be generated. The geometric analysis of such fundamental positions reveals the underlying mathematical structure that supports the art form's technical demands.

### 2.3 The Role of Turnout and Angular Relationships

One of the most distinctive features of ballet technique—the turnout of the legs—exemplifies the art form's engagement with geometric principles. Turnout involves externally rotating the hip joints so that the feet point outward, creating a specific angular relationship between the lower limbs and the line of progression. This positioning enhances both the aesthetic and technical aspects of performance, creating the characteristic clean lines and open hip angles that distinguish ballet from other dance forms [1].

The geometric implications of turnout extend throughout the body, affecting the alignment of the knees, ankles, and spine. Proper turnout requires sophisticated understanding of joint mechanics and spatial orientation, as the dancer must maintain alignment while rotating from the hip joints rather than forcing rotation at the feet or knees. This requirement demonstrates how geometric principles in ballet are not merely abstract concerns but have direct biomechanical consequences for movement execution and injury prevention.

The angular relationships created by turnout also influence the visual geometry of ballet positions. When a dancer stands in first position with proper turnout, the feet form an angle that creates a stable triangular base of support. In second position, this base widens further, creating a more stable but less compact configuration. These angular relationships between the feet determine the position of the center of mass relative to the base of support, directly affecting the mechanical balance requirements for static and dynamic movements.

### 2.4 Épaulement and Complex Torsional Geometry

The ballet position of épaulement represents perhaps the most sophisticated geometric achievement in classical technique. Described as "the crowning accomplishment of great ballet dancers," épaulement entails "a tremendous number of counter-rotations determined by the relationships among the foot, hand, and head—and even of the eyes" [3]. This position demands the most complex torsion of any ballet element, creating intricate geometric relationships throughout the body.

The mechanics of épaulement provide ballet with what one choreographer identifies as its "inner transitions" [3]. The counter-rotations involved create a dynamic equilibrium between opposing forces, where the head turns one way, the shoulders another, the hips a third, and the feet a fourth. These counter-rotations generate a torsional geometry that gives épaulement its distinctive visual quality—the sense of multidimensional depth and complexity that distinguishes accomplished dancers from beginners.

The geometric complexity of épaulement extends beyond the static position to encompass the transitions into and out of it. The counter-rotations must be coordinated precisely, with each body part arriving at its final position at the appropriate moment to create the characteristic impression of effortless complexity. This temporal dimension of épaulement demonstrates how geometric principles in ballet are not merely spatial but involve the precise timing of spatial relationships.

## 3. Spatial Dynamics and Temporal Transformation

### 3.1 From Fixed Geometry to Dynamic Exploration

Contemporary choreographers have developed approaches that take "what is spatial and fixed in ballet and make it temporal and unfixed" [3]. This transformation represents a fundamental shift in how geometric principles are engaged within choreography—from the creation of stable, reproducible configurations to the exploration of dynamic processes of spatial transformation.

The motivation for this shift relates to the digital nature of contemporary technology. As one choreographer observes, "Since today's technology is digital rather than alphabetical, why shouldn't we go with the flow?" [3]. This perspective suggests that contemporary choreographic practice should engage with the temporal, process-oriented qualities of digital technology rather than remaining bound to the static, codified nature of classical ballet. The result is a choreographic approach that uses ballet's precise spatial environment as a starting point but subjects it to "a series of distorting operations" that transform fixed configurations into dynamic explorations [3].

### 3.2 States of Fold: Multi-Temporal Body Dynamics

A central concept in contemporary choreographic exploration of spatial dynamics is the "state of fold." This approach involves teaching "the body how to fold and unfold again, at various rates and moving through different body parts" [3]. The result is what the choreographer terms a "many-timed body" folding and unfurling towards and against itself. This concept represents a significant departure from classical ballet's relatively synchronized movements, introducing deliberate asynchrony between different body parts.

The geometric implications of folding are substantial. When the body folds at multiple joints at different rates, the spatial relationships between body parts change continuously and complexly. A simple geometric configuration—the straight line of the body in standing position—transforms into a complex curve as different segments fold at different times and rates. The mathematics of these transformations involves the kinematics of multi-segment chains, where each segment's movement affects and is affected by the movements of adjacent segments.

The concept of fold extends beyond the body to encompass the space through which the body moves. When a dancer extends a position "out into space, or lets it move through the body as a natural continuation of the curves," the geometric relationship between body and environment becomes fluid and dynamic [3]. The boundaries between body and space blur as the dancer perceives and enacts relationships between any point on the body and any point in the surrounding space.

### 3.3 Keyframes and Transformational Procedures

Contemporary choreographic practice draws upon concepts from animation and computer graphics, treating ballet positions as "keyframes" from which transformations can be generated. A keyframe in animation represents a starting or ending point of a smooth transition, with intermediate frames calculated through interpolation. In choreography, the classical ballet position serves as a keyframe—a stable geometric configuration that anchors a sequence of movements [3].

From these keyframes, choreographers can generate "innumerable transformations" that extend the position "out into space" or allow it to "move through the body as a natural continuation of the curves" [3]. These transformations can be understood mathematically as operations that preserve some aspects of the original configuration while modifying others. The dancer becomes a geometric manipulator, exploring the space of possible variations on classical forms.

The procedural aspect of these transformations is crucial. Rather than creating fixed movement sequences, choreographers develop methods or algorithms that generate movement in the moment. Dancers "perceive the relationships between any of the points on the curves and any other parts of their bodies," and then "illustrate the presence of these imagined relationships by moving" [3]. This approach transforms choreography from the creation of specific movements to the establishment of relationships that generate movements through perception and decision.

### 3.4 Coordination Patterns and Phase Relationships

Research into the coordination patterns of ballet dancers has revealed that complex movements can be understood in terms of basic coordination modes. Studies using motion capture technology have found that "the complex arm and leg movement by a professional ballet dancer can be captured almost completely by only six basic coordination patterns for both the arms and the legs" [4]. These patterns fall into groups that can be classified as anti-phase or in-phase movements along three different directions in three-dimensional space.

The mathematical characterization of these coordination patterns involves the analysis of relative phase between oscillating components. In-phase movements involve body parts moving together in the same direction at the same time, while anti-phase movements involve body parts moving in opposite directions. These basic coordination modes serve as "elementary building blocks in complex movements" [4], from which more elaborate sequences are constructed through combination and sequencing.

The discovery of these basic coordination patterns has significant implications for understanding the geometric structure of ballet movement. Rather than treating each movement as a unique configuration, this perspective reveals underlying regularities in how dancers coordinate their body parts in space. The geometric complexity of ballet movements emerges from the combination and sequencing of relatively simple coordination patterns, much as complex musical compositions emerge from the combination of simple rhythmic and melodic elements.

## 4. Biomechanical Dimensions of Balance

### 4.1 Center of Mass and Base of Support

Balance in ballet, as in all human movement, fundamentally involves the management of the center of mass relative to the base of support. The center of mass represents the average position of all mass in the body, while the base of support encompasses the area bounded by the points of contact with the supporting surface. For a dancer to maintain static balance, the center of mass must remain within the base of support; for dynamic balance, the center of mass must move in ways that can be controlled within the changing base of support.

Biomechanical studies of ballet have examined the shifting of weight from both legs in first position to a one-legged stand, analyzing the progressive narrowing of the base of support and the corresponding adjustments in body configuration required to maintain equilibrium [5]. These studies reveal that ballet movements systematically explore the boundaries of balance, repeatedly approaching and recovering from positions where the center of mass approaches the edge of the base of support.

The mathematical modeling of ballet has enabled quantitative analysis of these balance requirements. Research has examined "the surfaces that a ballerina creates with her legs in three-dimensional space as she performs ballet choreography" [6]. Using motion capture systems and mathematical analysis, researchers have reconstructed the trajectories of body segments and calculated the changing positions of centers of mass during movement sequences. These analyses reveal the precise geometric and mechanical requirements for maintaining balance during complex ballet movements.

### 4.2 Multi-Planar Movement Analysis

Traditional biomechanical analysis describes motion and orientation in three orthogonal planes: the sagittal plane (dividing left from right), the transverse plane (dividing top from bottom), and the frontal plane (dividing front from back) [5]. Ballet movements typically involve coordinated motion across all three planes, creating complex three-dimensional trajectories that challenge the body's balance systems.

The analysis of ballet movements in these three planes reveals the multi-planar nature of even apparently simple movements. A grand jeté, for example, involves sagittal plane motion (forward/backward movement), frontal plane motion (lateral displacement of the arms and legs), and transverse plane motion (rotation of the hips and shoulders). The successful execution of such movements requires the dancer to coordinate balance control across all three planes simultaneously.

Measurements of angles and positions in biomechanical analysis are traditionally performed by projection onto the respective planes and axes [5]. Typical projections include the position of the center of mass, the position of the center of pressure (the point where the ground reaction force acts), joint angles, and the direction of gravity. While working with projections rather than underlying three-dimensional geometry may seem unnecessarily complicated, this approach allows comparison with the substantial biomechanical literature and enables the application of established analytical methods.

### 4.3 The Mechanics of Turnout and Hip Mechanics

The turnout that characterizes ballet technique has profound biomechanical implications, affecting the mechanics of the hips, knees, and ankles. Proper turnout requires external rotation of the hip joints, which is limited by the anatomical structure of the hip socket and the orientation of the femoral neck. Dancers who develop exceptional turnout do so through a combination of inherent anatomical variation and progressive stretching of the hip external rotators.

The biomechanical consequences of turnout extend throughout the lower extremity. When the hip is externally rotated, the femur rotates internally relative to the tibia during weight-bearing activities, affecting the alignment of the knee joint. This relationship has implications for injury risk, as abnormal knee alignment during landing movements can increase stress on joint structures. Understanding the biomechanics of turnout is therefore essential for both performance optimization and injury prevention.

The geometric analysis of turnout involves understanding the three-dimensional orientation of the hip joint and its relationship to the orientation of the foot. The angle of turnout is typically measured as the angle between the line of progression and the line of the foot, but this measurement represents only the external manifestation of complex internal rotations. The actual biomechanical reality involves coordinated rotation at the hip, knee, and ankle joints, with subtle adjustments throughout the kinetic chain.

### 4.4 Folding Mechanics and Center of Mass Management

The folding and unfolding movements explored in contemporary choreography have specific biomechanical implications for balance. Classical ballet involves "the constant folding and unfurling of just the leg, which the dancer always brings back to one of the prescribed positions" [3]. This folding is typically limited to the knee, with the hip remaining at approximately 90 degrees to the floor.

Contemporary approaches to folding differ from classical ballet in that they involve "not just in the knee but also in the hips, thus affecting the torso as well" [3]. This extended folding means that "instead of remaining at a 90-degree angle to the floor, the torso begins to fold down and become parallel with it." The biomechanical consequence is that "an entirely new set of mechanics then takes over, since the body has achieved a new state of balance."

The management of center of mass during these extended folding movements requires sophisticated proprioceptive awareness and motor control. As the torso folds toward the floor, the center of mass shifts forward and downward, requiring corresponding adjustments in the position of the legs and arms to maintain equilibrium. Dancers must develop sensitivity to these shifting relationships and learn to control their movements within the new balance parameters that emerge.

## 5. Mathematical Modeling and Motion Analysis

### 5.1 Motion Capture Technology in Ballet Research

The application of motion capture technology to ballet analysis has enabled unprecedented precision in measuring the spatial dynamics of dance movements. Research studies have used systems with "eight infrared cameras and 32 infrared markers attached to the dancer's body" to reconstruct "the trajectories of the markers in 3-dimensional space" [4]. These systems can capture movement at high temporal resolution, revealing the detailed geometry of even rapid movements.

The data collection process involves importing the positions of markers from the motion capture system into analysis software. The data is transferred into matrices containing the x, y, and z positions for each marker at each time sample [6]. This structured representation of movement data enables mathematical analysis of spatial and temporal relationships between body parts.

The calculation of segment centroids represents an important step in motion capture analysis. For each body segment, the centroid is calculated from the individual marker locations for each sample, creating representative trajectories for key anatomical landmarks [6]. For the leg, for example, centroids are calculated for the ankle, below the knee, above the knee, and the hip, with the knee centroid determined by averaging the above-knee and below-knee centroids.

### 5.2 Curve Fitting and Polynomial Modeling

Mathematical analysis of ballet movements involves fitting curves to the recorded position data. After data export, the function polyfit is used to find an approximate nth degree polynomial for the x, y, and z positions of each object [6]. The degree of the polynomial is determined through analysis, balancing the need to capture the essential features of the movement against the risk of overfitting to noise in the data.

Once these polynomial equations are determined, the curves for anatomical landmarks can be plotted in three-dimensional space. For the leg, curves are generated for the ankle, knee, and hip, giving three curves that represent the trajectory of the lower extremity during the movement [6]. These visualizations enable qualitative assessment of movement geometry and quantitative analysis of movement parameters.

The graphical approximation of surfaces involves constructing lines between anatomical landmarks at each time sample. For the leg, a straight line is constructed between the ankle and knee, and another between the knee and hip, representing the dancer's leg between object positions [6]. These lines, plotted across time, create a surface representation that captures the three-dimensional geometry of the leg during movement.

### 5.3 Surface Analysis and Geometric Modeling

The mathematical modeling of ballet extends beyond curve fitting to the analysis of surfaces created by body segments. Research has examined "the surfaces that a ballerina creates with her legs in three-dimensional space as she performs ballet choreography" [6]. These surfaces represent the geometric locus of the leg as it moves through space, capturing both the path of individual points and the relationships between points on different segments.

The surface analysis reveals the three-dimensional structure of ballet movements in ways that are not apparent from visual observation alone. While a viewer can perceive the general shape of a movement, motion capture analysis reveals precise geometric details—the exact curvature of trajectories, the three-dimensional orientation of planes, the relationships between movements of different body parts. These details have implications for both the aesthetic quality of movements and their biomechanical demands.

The mathematical characterization of these surfaces enables comparison across dancers, across movements, and across skill levels. By quantifying the geometric features of movements, researchers can identify characteristic patterns associated with different styles, different levels of expertise, or different choreographic approaches. This analytical capability supports both scientific investigation and practical applications in training and performance.

### 5.4 Coordination Dynamics and Phase Space Analysis

The analysis of coordination patterns in ballet movement draws upon the theoretical framework of coordination dynamics. This approach treats coordination between body parts as a dynamical system, with stable coordination patterns representing attractor states and transitions between patterns involving changes in stability and control [4].

The mathematical analysis of coordination patterns involves extracting the relative phase between oscillating components. For leg movements, the analysis has identified modes that correspond to anti-phase and in-phase patterns in different planes [4]. These modes represent fundamental coordination tendencies that emerge from the interaction of neural, muscular, and mechanical systems. The relative strength of these modes, measured by their eigenvalues, indicates the stability of different coordination patterns.

The phase space representation of coordination dynamics provides a framework for understanding both the stability and flexibility of ballet movements. Stable coordination patterns correspond to deep wells in the phase space, while transitions between patterns correspond to movements across barriers separating different attractor states. This perspective illuminates how dancers can maintain consistent movements while also adapting to changing circumstances and exploring new movement possibilities.

## 6. Choreographic Applications of Geometric Principles

### 6.1 Floor Patterns and Spatial Geometry

Choreographers have long recognized the importance of spatial geometry in creating visually compelling dance. Twentieth-century choreographer Frederick Ashton was "inspired by mathematics for its sheer beauty" and specifically "used geometry to create floor patterns and dance movements that could be viewed from any angle" [1]. This approach treats the stage space as a geometric canvas, with dancers' movements creating lines, shapes, and patterns that engage the audience's spatial perception.

The geometric design of floor patterns involves consideration of symmetry, repetition, and transformation. Groups of dancers can create geometric figures—triangles, circles, lines—that transform over time through the movement of individual dancers. The mathematics of these transformations involves concepts from group theory and geometry, with choreographic choices determining which symmetries and transformations are explored.

The viewing angle independence emphasized by Ashton reflects the three-dimensional nature of dance geometry. Unlike visual arts that present a fixed viewpoint, dance unfolds in time and space, with audience members perceiving the geometric patterns from different perspectives. Choreographers who design with this awareness create works that maintain geometric coherence from any viewing angle, enriching the spatial experience for all audience members.

### 6.2 The "Algebra" of Ballet: Balanchine's Geometric Approach

George Balanchine's choreography has been characterized as the "algebra" of ballet art [7]. This characterization reflects the choreographer's systematic approach to geometric relationships in space, treating choreographic composition as an exploration of mathematical possibilities. Balanchine's compositions create "spatial and bodily geometry, visible and readable by the viewer" through the deployment of "certain angles, turns and poses" [7].

The geometric analysis of Balanchine's work reveals sophisticated engagement with mathematical concepts. The classic arabesque pose in *Apollo Musagète*, with successively stretched legs extending to ever greater heights, "fits mathematically exactly into a circle, making one recall Leonardo's man" [7]. This geometric configuration creates a visual image that evokes both mathematical precision and symbolic meaning, connecting the abstract geometry of the circle to the mythological figure of Apollo.

The mathematical characterization of Balanchine's choreography extends beyond static positions to encompass the transitions between positions. The "algebra" of ballet involves not just the elements themselves but the operations that transform one element into another. Balanchine's choreographic innovations often involved finding new ways to connect classical positions, creating transitions that revealed geometric relationships not previously explored.

### 6.3 Contemporary Extensions: Digital and Computational Approaches

Contemporary choreographers have extended geometric exploration through engagement with digital and computational technologies. The idea to "imitate a computer application that can wrap a different quality around an existing event, thus altering its very nature" reflects an approach that uses computational concepts as choreographic inspiration [3]. Rather than simply applying technology to capture or display dance, this approach uses computational thinking to generate new movement possibilities.

The procedural nature of computational applications—where operations are applied to data to produce outputs—translates into choreographic approaches that generate movement through rules and algorithms. Rather than specifying exact movements, choreographers establish relationships and procedures that produce movements in the moment. Dancers become processors of spatial information, perceiving relationships and generating movements that embody those relationships.

The digital nature of contemporary technology offers possibilities for geometric exploration that differ from those available in classical ballet. Where classical ballet positions function as discrete, reproducible configurations like alphabetic characters, digital technology enables continuous variation and transformation. Contemporary choreography can explore the space between positions, generating movements that exist in the mathematical continuum rather than jumping between discrete states.

### 6.4 Discovery Through Movement

A crucial aspect of geometric exploration in choreography is the role of discovery in the creative process. The procedural approaches described above do not merely reproduce predetermined movements but create conditions for discovering "new ways of moving" [3]. This discovery emerges from the interaction between the geometric constraints established by the choreographer and the dancer's perceptual and motor capabilities.

The dancer's role in this discovery process involves "perceiv[ing] the relationships between any of the points on the curves and any other parts of their bodies" and then "illustrat[ing] the presence of these imagined relationships by moving" [3]. This description positions the dancer as an active explorer of geometric space, using movement as a means of investigation rather than merely as a means of expression.

The mathematical nature of this discovery process reflects the nature of mathematical exploration more generally. Mathematical discovery involves perceiving relationships between abstract entities and then exploring the implications of those relationships through reasoning and computation. In choreography, the abstract entities are spatial relationships, and the exploration occurs through bodily movement.
