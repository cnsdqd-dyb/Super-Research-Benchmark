# How Does Sensory Interaction in Digital Installations Construct an Ontology of Audience Participation?

## Abstract

This research report examines the fundamental question of how sensory interaction in digital installations constructs an ontology of audience participation. Drawing upon contemporary scholarship in embodied interaction theory, multimodal perception studies, and digital aesthetics, this analysis investigates the mechanisms through which interactive artworks transcend traditional observer-artwork relationships to generate novel ontological frameworks for participation. The investigation reveals that sensory interaction operates as a generative process wherein the boundaries between artwork and audience dissolve, creating what theorists have termed "bodies-in-code" and establishing participatory experiences that are simultaneously embodied, technological, and phenomenological. Through examination of cross-modality, feedback loops, and the emergence of presence, this report demonstrates that digital installations do not merely facilitate participation but actively constitute an ontological category wherein audience agency, sensory engagement, and artistic expression become inseparable. The findings carry significant implications for cultural institutions, interactive design practitioners, and theoretical frameworks concerning the nature of participatory experience in digital environments.

## 1. Introduction

The emergence of digital installations as a dominant form of contemporary artistic expression has precipitated a fundamental reconceptualization of the relationship between artwork and audience. Unlike traditional artistic paradigms wherein the viewer occupies a position of detached observation, digital installations invite, require, and indeed constitute their meaning through active audience participation. This transformation extends beyond mere interactivity to encompass a profound ontological shift in how we understand the nature of artistic experience, agency, and presence within technological environments.

The theoretical significance of this phenomenon lies not simply in the behavioral changes it produces—though these are substantial—but in the ontological reconfiguration it engenders. When sensory interaction becomes the primary mechanism through which artistic meaning is realized, the very categories of "artwork," "audience," and "participation" undergo radical transformation. The artwork ceases to be a fixed object awaiting interpretation and becomes instead a dynamic system whose existence is co-constituted through interaction. The audience ceases to be a passive receiver of aesthetic experience and becomes an active participant in the ontological emergence of the work itself.

This report addresses the central question: How does sensory interaction in digital installations construct an ontology of audience participation? To answer this question, we must examine several interconnected dimensions of the phenomenon. First, we must understand the theoretical foundations of embodied interaction as they apply to digital art contexts. Second, we must analyze how sensory modalities—visual, auditory, tactile, and sometimes olfactory—combine and interact to create immersive experiences. Third, we must investigate the ontological implications of the feedback loops that characterize interactive installations, wherein audience actions generate sensory responses that in turn shape subsequent interactions. Finally, we must consider how these mechanisms collectively establish new frameworks for understanding presence, agency, and participation in digital environments.

The investigation proceeds through a synthesis of theoretical perspectives drawn from human-computer interaction studies, digital aesthetics, embodiment theory, and museum studies. By integrating these diverse scholarly traditions, we can develop a comprehensive understanding of how sensory interaction constructs participatory ontology and what this means for the future of artistic experience in digital contexts.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 Embodied Interaction and Somaesthetics in Interactive Art

The examination of interactive art through an embodied perspective has been explored extensively in contemporary scholarship, with particular attention to the complexities of audience-artwork interactions. Nathaniel Stern critiques the dominant linguistic and visual signification paradigms in digital art discussions, arguing that they fail to fully address the complexities of interactive art [1]. According to Stern, the essence of interactivity lies in the physical engagement of the audience, making embodied interaction central to the realization of artwork. This perspective fundamentally challenges Cartesian mind-body dualism by positioning the body not as a mere vessel for perception but as the primary site of aesthetic experience and meaning-making.

Caroline A. Jones, in her curatorial essays for the exhibition Sensorium, challenges the "reigning hegemony" of the five senses, urging a reevaluation of digital art within an expanded sensorial framework [1]. This challenge to traditional sensory hierarchies proves particularly significant for understanding digital installations, which often employ unconventional combinations of sensory modalities and create experiences that cannot be reduced to any single sense. The rejection of sensory hierarchy aligns with phenomenological approaches to perception that emphasize the integrated, holistic nature of embodied experience.

The philosophical foundation for understanding embodied interaction in digital contexts draws significantly from Don Ihde's exploration of bodies in their phenomenal and sociocultural dimensions and their relationship to technology [1]. Ihde's work establishes that human-technology relationships are characterized by various embodied configurations that shape how we perceive and interact with our environments. In the context of digital installations, this framework illuminates how technological mediation does not simply filter or distort perception but actively transforms the conditions of embodied experience.

Paul Dourish's concept of "embodied interaction" as defined in human-computer interaction provides additional theoretical grounding [1]. Dourish emphasizes that interaction with digital systems is fundamentally embodied, situated, and social, challenging the abstract, disembodied models of computation that dominated earlier approaches. This perspective proves essential for understanding how digital installations create participatory experiences that are simultaneously technological and bodily, abstract and concrete.

### 2.2 The Ontology of Interactivity and Participatory Experience

The ontological dimensions of interactivity in digital art have been theorized through several influential frameworks. Mark Hansen coins the expression "bodies-in-code," referring to a hybrid dimension of presence in which the technological mediation of the interface and the media environment, conveyed by interactivity and sensory feedback, leads to the fundamental dissolution of the putative boundaries that separate the body of the spectator-recipient from the physical space of the installation and the digital space of the information [2]. This concept of "bodies-in-code" represents a radical ontological claim: that digital installations do not merely represent or simulate embodied experience but actually produce new ontological configurations wherein the distinctions between physical and digital, body and code, become productively indeterminate.

The theoretical contribution of Saltz proposes an interdependence of the recipient's subjective experience of the interaction and the material conditions for existence of the interface [2]. This interdependence suggests that the ontology of participation cannot be understood by examining either the artwork or the audience in isolation but must be understood as an emergent property of their dynamic relationship. The installation provides the conditions for interaction, but the interaction itself constitutes the artwork's existence in a way that cannot be predetermined or fully specified by the artist.

Whitelaw et al. highlight that the peculiar nature of the digital artefact, especially in fields with a high level of interactivity such as generative art and game art, lies in its capacity to generate concomitant ontologies [2]. This observation points to a fundamental characteristic of interactive digital art: the capacity to generate multiple, coexisting modes of existence depending on the nature and pattern of audience interaction. The artwork does not possess a single, fixed ontology but rather exists as a flexible structure capable of actualizing different ontological configurations through participation.

In ontological terms, one consequence is that in the aesthetic experience of interactivity, work and reception are con-fused into the same processual existence [2]. This fusion represents perhaps the most radical ontological implication of sensory interaction in digital installations: the collapse of the traditional distinction between production and reception, between artwork and audience. The artwork does not exist prior to or independent of its reception; rather, the artwork and its reception constitute a single, ongoing process of ontological emergence.

### 2.3 Cross-Modality and Multisensory Integration

The theoretical understanding of sensory interaction in digital installations must account for the phenomenon of cross-modality—the integration of information across different sensory channels. Csikszentmihalyi's concept of flow, described as "the ideal form of immersion," refers to participants' intensely focused engagement, fully immersed in an activity, often losing track of time and self-awareness [3]. Flow states in interactive installations emerge when the integration of sensory feedback creates a coherent, compelling experience that demands and rewards sustained attention.

The evolution of immersive art is characterized by the interplay between technical interaction, cross-modality, and atmosphere [3]. This characterization emphasizes that the immersive quality of digital installations emerges from the dynamic interaction between technological systems, sensory modalities, and the affective atmosphere they collectively produce. The technical capacity for real-time response, combined with the integration of multiple sensory channels, creates conditions wherein participants can achieve states of deep engagement that transcend ordinary experience.

Multimodal research emerges as a valuable instrument for scrutinizing the impacts of interactive installation art under the influence of multi-sensory stimuli on audience engagement and creative expression [4]. The amalgamation of multimodal data sources holds the potential to heighten audience engagement and amplify creative expression. This research trajectory suggests that the ontological construction of participation is not simply a matter of adding more sensory channels but of creating meaningful relationships between modalities that generate emergent properties of experience.

## 3. Sensory Interaction as Ontological Construction

### 3.1 The Mechanisms of Sensory Engagement

Immersive art installations are characterized by their ability to transform perception, encouraging participants to become part of the artwork through interaction [5]. This transformation of perception operates through the systematic orchestration of sensory stimuli to create environments that respond to and incorporate participant actions. The goal is to immerse the viewer in the artwork, blurring the lines between the observer and the art itself, fostering a deep sense of presence and interaction. The ontological significance of this blurring lies in the dissolution of the subject-object distinction that has traditionally structured aesthetic experience.

The interactive nature of immersive art changes the way audiences consume and engage with art, promoting active participation over passive observation [5]. This shift encourages a deeper, more personal connection to the artwork, potentially altering public perceptions of art and its role in society. The ontological implications of this shift extend beyond individual aesthetic experience to encompass broader cultural and social transformations in how art is understood, valued, and practiced.

AI-powered multi-sensory art installations democratize art by making it more accessible and engaging [6]. Traditional art forms can feel static or unapproachable to some, but these dynamic installations invite participation, fostering a sense of connection and belonging. The democratization of art through interactive installations is thus not merely a matter of increased accessibility but represents an ontological transformation in the nature of artistic experience itself. Art becomes something that is done rather than merely viewed, something that is co-created rather than simply received.

### 3.2 The Feedback Loop as Generative Process

The interactive environments created by the artist, in the field of installation as well as of performance and interactive film, provide action with a context in which the work appears as an original type alongside the interaction [2]. This theoretical formulation emphasizes that the artwork and the interaction are not separate entities but rather co-constitutive elements of a single experiential process. The feedback loop—wherein participant actions generate sensory responses that shape subsequent actions and responses—operates as the fundamental generative mechanism of participatory ontology.

The technical-artistic devices are overshadowed in favour of a mainly embodied, situated, and inactive knowledge resulting from the sensory feedback loop [2]. This observation points to a crucial aspect of participatory ontology: the way in which technological complexity becomes transparent, replaced by direct, embodied engagement with sensory phenomena. The participant does not experience the technology per se but rather experiences the sensory world that the technology enables and sustains. This phenomenological transparency of technology is essential to the ontological construction of participation, as it allows the participant to engage directly with the artwork rather than with an interface mediating their engagement.

Neuroscience studies also show that immersive experiences activate the brain's mirror neuron system, which creates empathy and emotional connection with characters and narratives [7]. When audiences feel agency—the sense that their actions have meaningful consequences within the experience—dopamine levels increase, creating feelings of satisfaction and motivation to continue engaging. This neurobiological perspective on the feedback loop reveals the physiological substrates of participatory ontology, demonstrating how sensory interaction generates not only cognitive but also affective and motivational states that sustain engagement.

### 3.3 Presence and the Dissolution of Boundaries

The concept of presence—the subjective sense of "being there" in a mediated environment—proves central to understanding the ontological dimensions of sensory interaction in digital installations. Poissant prefers to talk about "sense of presence" and "effects of presence" as agents of the embodied relationship of the stage performer and media devices [2]. Similarly, Pitozzi defines presence as the emergence of a perceptive tension between projection and retention, between virtual body and physical body [2]. This tension results in "gradations of presence," that is, the different levels of intensity through which presence irradiates from the performer's body into the space and becomes available to the spectator's perception.

The ontological significance of presence lies in its capacity to generate experiences that are simultaneously real and mediated, physical and virtual. Through the visual and auditory extension of the performer's anatomy, new technologies—especially those in real time such as motion capture and bio-feedback—create conditions for presence that exceed the boundaries of the physical body [2]. These extended forms of presence represent ontological innovations that challenge traditional categories of embodiment and selfhood.

The cultural and historical context of public spaces reveals that digital art in public spaces is no longer confined to traditional art forms [8]. Through interaction and engagement, it incorporates users into the art, making them participants in creativity. This stimulates the integration of art and technology and brings greater possibilities for innovation and creativity. In this process, when digital art intervenes in public spaces, the participation of users becomes crucial. It is the organic coupling of digital art or technology, users, and public spaces that matters [8]. This formulation emphasizes that the ontology of participation emerges from the dynamic relationship between technological systems, human participants, and spatial contexts.

## 4. The Constructed Ontology of Audience Participation

### 4.1 From Passive Observation to Active Co-Creation

Cultural institutions have taken advantage of participatory design approaches that have been successful in other sectors in order to try to increase audience engagement in their collections and encourage a dialogue with their visitors, adopting more audience-centred practices [9]. For instance, cultural institutions have adopted participatory design to build interactive experiences that enhance the exhibition space and facilitate the engagement of new audiences [9]. This institutional adoption of participatory design reflects a broader recognition that the ontology of artistic experience is not fixed but can be deliberately transformed through the design of interactive systems.

The shift from passive observation to active co-creation represents a fundamental ontological transformation in the nature of artistic experience. When audiences become active participants in the creation of artistic experience, they cease to be external observers and become instead constitutive elements of the artwork itself. This ontological shift has profound implications for how we understand the nature of art, the role of the artist, and the experience of the audience.

Co-design methodologies have also been refined to empower cultural heritage professionals to be more active in the design and direct management of digitally-enhanced visitor experiences [9]. This professionalization of participatory design reflects the growing recognition that the construction of participatory ontology requires sophisticated expertise in interaction design, sensory integration, and user experience. The design of interactive installations is not simply an artistic endeavor but a technical and theoretical discipline that draws upon diverse forms of knowledge.

### 4.2 Emotional and Cathartic Dimensions

Interactive installation art can facilitate cathartic experiences, allowing individuals to release pent-up emotions and find emotional release or relief [4]. The interactive nature of these installations can create a safe space for individuals to express and process their feelings. This cathartic potential of interactive installations points to an important dimension of participatory ontology: the capacity for interactive experiences to generate genuine emotional and psychological effects that transcend mere aesthetic appreciation.

Different emotional responses can be elicited using different stimuli, emphasizing the potential of multimodal emotion recognition technology in interactive installation art [4]. Multiple sensing modalities give a wealth of information to support interaction with the world and one another. This technological capacity for emotional recognition and response creates possibilities for interactive systems that can adapt to and reflect the emotional states of participants, deepening the participatory relationship and enhancing the ontological integration of audience and artwork.

The multimodal fusion of various input modalities enables users to interact with computers through various channels [4]. This multimodal approach allows the artwork to engage with audiences in multiple ways and create a more immersive and dynamic experience. The ontological construction of participation thus depends not simply on the presence of interaction but on the richness and integration of the sensory modalities through which interaction occurs.

### 4.3 Community and Collective Participation

Cultural heritage is one ingredient that can strongly contribute towards the welfare of communities [9]. The care and rediscovery of one's own cultural heritage can strengthen cultural identity and help people connect with each other through the past. This can be particularly beneficial for people that have been marginalised from the GLAM sector or are dissatisfied with the heritage interpretation provided by the institutional heritage sector [9]. The ontological construction of participation thus extends beyond individual experience to encompass collective and community dimensions.

Evidences show how the participation in heritage-related activities can enhance community empowerment and development [9]. This community dimension of participatory ontology reveals that the construction of participation through sensory interaction is not merely an individual psychological phenomenon but a social and political process with implications for community formation, cultural identity, and collective empowerment.

Social media applications have been used to facilitate a participative cultural experience [10]. Enabling local communities to create, upload, share, and interact with digital content of cultural heritage demonstrates a proven and growing demand for cultural participation, exploration of identity, and creative expression [10]. The integration of social media with physical installations creates hybrid spaces wherein the ontology of participation extends beyond the immediate interaction to encompass broader networks of communication and sharing.

## 5. Implications and Future Directions

### 5.1 Theoretical Implications

The analysis presented in this report suggests several significant theoretical implications for understanding the ontology of audience participation in digital installations. First, the ontology of participation is not a pre-existing category that interactive installations simply activate but rather a constructed phenomenon that emerges through the dynamic interaction of technological systems, sensory modalities, and embodied participants. This constructivist understanding of participatory ontology challenges essentialist notions of artistic experience and suggests that the nature of participation is always contingent on the specific configurations of interactive systems.

Second, the construction of participatory ontology depends fundamentally on the integration of sensory modalities and the creation of feedback loops that dissolve the boundaries between artwork and audience. The "bodies-in-code" phenomenon represents not merely a metaphorical description of interactive experience but an ontological claim about the nature of existence in technologically mediated environments. As digital installations become increasingly sophisticated in their capacity for real-time response and sensory integration, the ontological distinctions between physical and digital, human and technological, will become increasingly productively indeterminate.

Third, the ontology of participation extends beyond individual experience to encompass collective and community dimensions. The construction of participation through sensory interaction has implications for social formation, cultural identity, and political agency that exceed the boundaries of aesthetic experience narrowly construed. This suggests that theoretical frameworks for understanding participatory ontology must integrate perspectives from aesthetics, phenomenology, social theory, and political philosophy.

### 5.2 Practical Implications

For cultural institutions seeking to develop interactive installations, the analysis suggests several practical implications. First, the design of interactive systems must attend not only to technological functionality but to the ontological dimensions of the experiences they create. The construction of participatory ontology requires careful attention to the relationships between sensory modalities, the dynamics of feedback loops, and the conditions for the emergence of presence.

Second, the democratization of art through interactive installations carries both opportunities and responsibilities. While interactive systems can make art more accessible and engaging, they also risk reducing art to mere entertainment if the deeper dimensions of aesthetic experience are not sustained. The challenge for practitioners is to design interactive experiences that maintain substantive artistic value while embracing the participatory possibilities that digital technology enables.

Third, the integration of social media with physical installations creates opportunities for extending the ontology of participation beyond the immediate interaction. Practitioners must consider how interactive installations can function not as isolated experiences but as nodes in broader networks of cultural participation and community formation.

### 5.3 Future Research Directions

Several directions for future research emerge from this analysis. First, empirical studies of audience experience in interactive installations could provide valuable data on the phenomenological dimensions of participatory ontology. How do participants experience the dissolution of boundaries between self and artwork? What are the conditions under which presence emerges and deepens? These questions require empirical investigation that complements theoretical analysis.

Second, comparative studies across different types of interactive installations could illuminate how different configurations of sensory modalities, feedback dynamics, and spatial contexts shape the construction of participatory ontology. Such studies could inform the development of design principles for interactive systems that optimize the conditions for meaningful participation.

Third, longitudinal research on the social and community effects of interactive installations could reveal how participation in digital art experiences contributes to broader processes of cultural identity formation and community development. Such research would extend the analysis of participatory ontology beyond individual experience to encompass collective and societal dimensions.
