# The Influence of Social Network Rewiring Probability on the Spectral Clustering Structure of Collective Identity Vectors in Urbanization Models

## Abstract

This research report investigates the intricate relationship between social network rewiring probability and the spectral clustering structure of collective identity vectors within the framework of urbanization models. Drawing upon established principles from spectral graph theory, complex systems theory, and agent-based modeling, this analysis examines how variations in the rewiring parameter $p$ within the Watts-Strogatz small-world network model fundamentally alter the eigenspectral properties of graph Laplacian matrices, thereby influencing the emergence and stability of collective identity formations in urban populations. The findings reveal that rewiring probability serves as a critical control parameter governing the transition between regular lattice structures characterized by high clustering coefficients and random graph structures exhibiting low path lengths, with profound implications for understanding how urban social networks maintain cohesion while adapting to changing connectivity patterns. The spectral analysis demonstrates that intermediate rewiring probabilities in the range $p \approx 0.01-0.1$ produce optimal conditions for the emergence of robust collective identity clusters, balancing local community structure with global network integration.

## 1. Introduction and Theoretical Framework

### 1.1 Urban Systems as Complex Adaptive Networks

The paradigm of understanding cities as complex systems represents a fundamental shift from traditional mechanistic approaches to urbanism, which frequently treated urban phenomena in linear or reductionist terms [2]. Complexity theory, developed from the pioneering work of researchers such as Ilya Prigogine and subsequently extended by scholars across multiple disciplines, offers conceptual tools to understand systems as emergent properties arising from interactions between a set of elements, where behaviors generated by the collective cannot be reduced to properties present in isolated components [2]. This theoretical foundation provides the essential backdrop for examining how social network structures within urban environments give rise to collective identities through local interaction dynamics and global network organization.

The application of network analysis to urban systems has gained significant momentum in recent years, driven by the emergence of urban network analysis methodologies and the widespread availability of geographic information system data [8]. Computational modeling of urban networks—whether street networks, social networks, or infrastructure networks—is fundamentally based on a graph-theoretic view in which intersections of linear features are regarded as nodes, and connections between pairs of nodes are represented as edges [8]. This mathematical abstraction enables the application of sophisticated analytical techniques, including spectral graph theory and clustering algorithms, to extract meaningful patterns from the complex topology of urban social systems.

### 1.2 The Significance of Rewiring Probability in Network Evolution

The Watts-Strogatz model of small-world network generation provides a canonical framework for understanding how network topology evolves under controlled rewiring operations [3][4]. The model begins with a regular ring lattice and progressively rewires edges with probability $p$, thereby interpolating smoothly between a highly regular structure and a random graph [4]. This rewiring process introduces long-range connections that dramatically reduce average path lengths while largely preserving the high clustering coefficients characteristic of regular lattices [3].

The rewiring probability $p$ functions as a critical control parameter whose value determines the structural properties of the resulting network. When $p = 0$, the regular ring lattice remains unchanged, preserving high clustering coefficients and long average path lengths [3]. Conversely, when $p = 1$, the graph reaches the limit of total randomness, with edges rewired throughout the network, resulting in low clustering coefficients and short average path lengths [3]. Intermediate values of $p$ generate graphs of particular interest, as they produce structural properties characteristic of small-world networks—high clustering coefficients combined with small average path lengths—that are frequently observed in real-world social systems [3][4].

The parameter range $p \approx 0.01-0.1$ has been identified as particularly significant, as networks in this regime maintain very high clustering coefficients while already exhibiting substantially reduced average path lengths [4]. This "small-world regime" represents an optimal configuration for information propagation, resource allocation, and collective behavior emergence in social networks, making it especially relevant for understanding how collective identity formations develop and persist within urban environments.

### 1.3 Spectral Graph Theory and Collective Identity Analysis

Spectral graph theory provides a rigorous analytical framework for linking global structural properties of networks to local combinatorial features through the spectra of graph-associated matrices [11]. Central to this approach are the adjacency matrix $A$ and the normalized Laplacian $L = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}$, whose eigenvalues encode key indicators of connectivity, expansion, and robustness [11]. The second-smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian, denoted $\lambda_2$, functions as a spectral gap whose magnitude governs mixing times in diffusive processes and convergence rates in consensus dynamics [11].

For the analysis of collective identity vectors in urbanization models, spectral clustering techniques offer particularly powerful tools. Spectral clustering algorithms operate by computing the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian matrix and using these eigenvectors to embed the network nodes in a low-dimensional space where traditional clustering algorithms such as k-means can be effectively applied [5][7]. This approach has demonstrated remarkable success in identifying community structures and cohesive groups within complex networks, including urban social networks where traditional distance-based clustering methods often fail due to the non-Euclidean nature of network topology [8].

The mathematical foundation of spectral clustering integrates graph theory and manifold learning principles, enabling effective handling of complex, nonlinear data distributions while maintaining theoretical guarantees and computational efficiency [9]. As a sophisticated graph-cut methodology in machine learning, spectral clustering is distinguished by its ability to capture intrinsic manifold structures through the eigenspectrum analysis of graph Laplacian matrices [9]. This capability makes it particularly suitable for analyzing the emergence and stability of collective identity clusters within urban social networks, where identity formations often follow complex, non-convex patterns in the high-dimensional space of social attributes.

## 2. Methodology: Spectral Analysis of Network Structure

### 2.1 Graph Laplacian and Eigenspectral Properties

The graph Laplacian $L$ serves as the fundamental mathematical object for spectral analysis of network structure. For an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ with $n$ vertices, the unnormalized Laplacian is defined as $L = D - A$, where $D$ is the diagonal degree matrix and $A$ is the adjacency matrix [5][7]. The eigenvalues of $L$, denoted $0 = \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_n$, provide crucial information about the network's structural properties and its susceptibility to various dynamical processes.

For $d$-regular graphs, the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1} = [1, 1, \ldots, 1]$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $d$, reflecting the uniform degree distribution [7]. The complete graph $K_n$ has an adjacency matrix $A = J - I$, where $J$ is the all-ones matrix and $I$ is the identity matrix, yielding eigenvalues $n-1$ (with multiplicity $n-1$) and $-1$ (with multiplicity $n-1$) [7]. These extreme cases provide reference points for understanding how rewiring operations modify the eigenspectral properties of intermediate network structures.

The spectral gap $\lambda_2$ holds particular significance for understanding collective behavior in networks. This eigenvalue governs the rate at which diffusive processes spread across the network and the convergence speed of consensus dynamics [11]. Networks with larger spectral gaps exhibit faster mixing times and more rapid consensus formation, which directly impacts the speed and stability of collective identity emergence. The magnitude of $\lambda_2$ is intimately connected to the network's expansion properties and the ease with which information or influence can propagate between different regions of the network.

### 2.2 Spectral Clustering Algorithm

The spectral clustering algorithm proceeds through several well-defined stages that transform the raw network structure into a partition of clusters [5]. Given a similarity matrix $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ representing pairwise similarities between nodes, the algorithm first constructs a similarity graph with weighted adjacency matrix $W$. The unnormalized Laplacian $L$ is then computed, and the first $k$ eigenvectors $u_1, \ldots, u_k$ are extracted [5].

These eigenvectors form the columns of a matrix $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$, and each row of $U$ represents a point in $\mathbb{R}^k$ that can be clustered using the k-means algorithm [5]. The resulting clusters $A_1, \ldots, A_k$ constitute the output of the algorithm, where $A_i = \{j \mid y_j \in C_i\}$ and $y_j$ is the $j$-th row of $U$ [5].

The effectiveness of spectral clustering derives from the property that the eigenvectors of the Laplacian provide a continuous relaxation of the discrete graph partitioning problem [7]. When the network exhibits clear community structure, the eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest non-trivial eigenvalues will be approximately constant within communities and vary between them, making the subsequent k-means clustering step highly effective at identifying natural groupings [5][7].

### 2.3 Normalized Variants and Their Properties

Two primary variants of normalized spectral clustering have been developed, corresponding to different normalized graph Laplacians [5]. The Shi-Maliki algorithm uses the random walk Laplacian $L_{rw} = D^{-1}L$, while the Ng-Jordan-Weiss algorithm uses the symmetric Laplacian $L_{sym} = D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}$ [5]. Both approaches have theoretical justifications and exhibit different numerical properties in practice.

The normalized Laplacians are particularly relevant for analyzing social networks where node degrees are heterogeneous, as they account for the varying connectivity patterns of different nodes. In the context of urbanization models, where individuals exhibit diverse social connectivity patterns, normalized spectral clustering may provide more meaningful results by appropriately weighting the contributions of nodes with different degrees to the clustering structure.

## 3. The Watts-Strogatz Model and Rewiring Dynamics

### 3.1 Network Construction and Parameter Space

The Watts-Strogatz model generates networks through a systematic rewiring process applied to a regular ring lattice [3][4]. The construction begins with a one-dimensional lattice of $N$ vertices, each connected to its $k$ nearest neighbors (typically $k$ is even to ensure symmetry) [4]. The algorithm then iterates through all edges and rewires each edge with probability $p$, moving one endpoint to a new position chosen uniformly at random from the remaining vertices [4].

This construction process produces a parameterized family of networks that smoothly interpolates between the regular lattice ($p = 0$) and the Erdős-Rényi random graph ($p = 1$) [4]. The intermediate values of $p$ generate networks exhibiting the small-world property: high clustering coefficients characteristic of regular lattices combined with small average path lengths characteristic of random graphs [3][4].

The pseudo-code for sampling graphs from the Watts-Strogatz model can be found in standard references on network science [3]. The algorithm ensures that the rewiring process preserves the degree distribution of the original lattice while introducing long-range shortcuts that dramatically alter global connectivity properties. This preservation of degree distribution is crucial for isolating the effects of topological reorganization from the confounding effects of degree heterogeneity.

### 3.2 Structural Transitions Across Rewiring Probabilities

As the rewiring probability $p$ increases from 0 to 1, the network undergoes a series of structural transitions that can be characterized through both local and global network metrics [3]. At $p = 0$, the regular ring lattice exhibits high clustering coefficients because nodes are only connected to neighboring nodes, and the average path length is high because traversing the graph from one node to a more distant node requires following the lattice edges in sequence [3].

The introduction of even a small number of rewired edges (corresponding to very small $p$) is sufficient to create shortcuts that dramatically reduce the average path length while having minimal impact on the clustering coefficient [3]. This asymmetric response to rewiring—where path length decreases rapidly while clustering persists—defines the small-world regime and has profound implications for collective behavior in social networks.

When $p = 1$, the graph reaches the limit of total randomization, with all edges rewired [3]. The resulting random graph has a low clustering coefficient because nodes are randomly connected to the rest of the network, and a low average path length because the random connections provide efficient paths between any pair of nodes [3]. This represents a fundamentally different organizational principle than the regular lattice, with implications for the types of collective structures that can emerge.

### 3.3 The Small-World Regime and Its Significance

The parameter range $p \approx 0.01-0.1$ has been identified as the small-world network regime, where networks exhibit optimal combinations of local clustering and global connectivity [4]. In this regime, the clustering coefficient remains very high—often exceeding 80% of the original lattice value—while the average path length has already decreased to within a small factor of its minimum possible value [4].

This regime is particularly significant for understanding collective identity formation in urban social networks because it represents a configuration where local community structures can persist while still maintaining efficient global integration [3][4]. The high clustering coefficient ensures that tightly-knit local groups can form and maintain shared identities through repeated local interactions, while the small average path length ensures that information, influence, and cultural elements can propagate throughout the network, enabling coordination and shared understanding across the entire urban population.

The shaded region in spectral plots of $C(p)$ (clustering coefficient) and $L(p)$ (average path length) against $p$ in logarithmic scale clearly delineates this small-world regime [3]. The transition from regular to small-world behavior occurs at very low rewiring probabilities, typically around $p \approx 0.01$, while the transition from small-world to random behavior occurs at higher rewiring probabilities, typically around $p \approx 0.1$ [3][4].

## 4. Spectral Properties and Collective Identity Vectors

### 4.1 Eigenspectral Evolution Under Rewiring

The spectral properties of network Laplacian matrices undergo systematic transformations as the rewiring probability $p$ varies [1]. Spectral plots of small-world networks generated with rewiring probability $p = 0.3$ exhibit characteristic patterns that differ from both regular lattices and random graphs [1]. These spectral signatures encode information about the network's community structure, expansion properties, and susceptibility to various dynamical processes.

For regular ring lattices, the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix exhibit a characteristic sinusoidal distribution that reflects the lattice's translational symmetry [1]. As rewiring is introduced, this regular pattern gradually dissolves, and the eigenvalue distribution becomes increasingly similar to that of random graphs, which follows the semicircular law for large random matrices [1]. The rate at which this spectral transformation occurs provides insight into how quickly the network loses its regular structure and gains random characteristics.

The spectral gap $\lambda_2$ of the Laplacian increases monotonically with rewiring probability, reflecting the improved expansion properties and faster mixing times of more random networks [11]. However, this increase is not linear; rather, it exhibits a rapid initial increase at very small $p$ (corresponding to the transition from regular to small-world) followed by a slower approach to the random graph limit as $p$ approaches 1 [3]. This non-linear relationship between $p$ and $\lambda_2$ has important implications for understanding how collective identity clusters form and dissolve as urban social networks evolve.

### 4.2 Community Detection and Spectral Embedding

Spectral clustering algorithms leverage the eigenvectors of the Laplacian to embed network nodes in a low-dimensional space where traditional clustering algorithms can be applied effectively [5][7]. The quality of this embedding—and consequently the quality of the resulting clusters—depends critically on the network's spectral properties, which are themselves determined by the rewiring probability.

For networks in the small-world regime, the spectral embedding typically reveals clear community structure, with nodes belonging to the same community clustering together in the embedded space [5][8]. This occurs because the eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest non-trivial eigenvalues capture the slow modes of diffusion on the network, which are dominated by movement between communities rather than within them [7]. The resulting embedding thus provides an optimal representation for community detection.

In contrast, for random networks (high $p$), the spectral embedding often fails to reveal meaningful community structure because the eigenvalue spectrum is relatively flat, with many small eigenvalues that do not correspond to clear community boundaries [7]. Similarly, for regular lattices (low $p$), the spectral embedding may reveal highly regular patterns that do not correspond to natural community boundaries but rather to the lattice's geometric structure [1]. The small-world regime thus represents an optimal configuration for spectral community detection, where the network exhibits both meaningful community structure and sufficient randomness to prevent the embedding from being dominated by geometric regularities.

The concept of collective identity vectors extends the mathematical framework of spectral clustering to capture the multidimensional nature of identity in urban populations [10]. Each individual in an urban social network can be associated with a vector of identity attributes—cultural background, socioeconomic status, residential location, professional network, and other relevant dimensions—that collectively define their position in the space of social identities.

The rewiring probability of the underlying social network fundamentally influences the structure of these collective identity clusters. At low rewiring probabilities, the network's high clustering coefficient promotes the formation of tight, isolated communities with strong internal identity coherence but limited interaction with other communities [3]. At high rewiring probabilities, the network's low path length promotes integration and mixing but may weaken local identity coherence by exposing individuals to diverse identity influences [3]. The small-world regime provides an optimal balance, enabling both strong local identity communities and efficient cross-community communication that can lead to the emergence of shared urban identities.

### 6.2 Determining Optimal Cluster Numbers

A critical challenge in applying spectral clustering to urban networks is determining the appropriate number of clusters [8]. Absent a ground-truth definition of actual communities, it is difficult to assess the validity of a clustering solution and, in particular, the decision of how many clusters to partition the network into [8].

Several methods have been developed to address this challenge [8]. The elbow method records the within-cluster sum of squared distances (WCSS) between plotted eigenpoints and their cluster centroid [8]. As the cluster count increases, the WCSS value decreases monotonically, and the "elbow" point where the rate of decrease sharply changes indicates an optimal cluster number [8]. The eigen gap criterion selects the number of clusters $k$ that maximizes the gap between consecutive eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ and $\lambda_{k+1}$, under the rationale that large gaps indicate natural community boundaries [7].

For urban networks undergoing rewiring, the optimal cluster number may vary with the rewiring probability [8]. At low $p$, the network's high clustering coefficient and regular structure may suggest a relatively small number of large clusters corresponding to major urban districts [3]. At high $p$, the network's random structure may suggest a larger number of smaller clusters or may indicate that no natural clustering exists [3]. The small-world regime typically yields intermediate cluster numbers that balance the need for locally coherent communities with the reality of cross-community integration [4].
