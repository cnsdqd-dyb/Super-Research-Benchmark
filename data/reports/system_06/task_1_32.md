# Heparin Dosage Management During ECMO Support: Balancing Thromboembolism and Bleeding Risks

## Abstract

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) represents a critical life-support modality for patients with severe respiratory or cardiac failure. However, the management of anticoagulation during ECMO support remains one of the most challenging aspects of clinical practice, requiring a delicate balance between preventing circuit thrombosis and thromboembolic complications while minimizing the risk of life-threatening hemorrhage. Unfractionated heparin (UFH) remains the most commonly used anticoagulant in ECMO support, yet optimal dosing strategies, monitoring parameters, and target therapeutic ranges continue to be subjects of intense debate and significant institutional variation. This report examines the current state of heparin dosage management during ECMO support, synthesizing evidence regarding anticoagulation targets, monitoring methodologies, and emerging strategies to optimize the balance between thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks. The analysis reveals a clear trend toward lower-intensity anticoagulation protocols, increased adoption of anti-Xa monitoring over traditional activated clotting time and activated partial thromboplastin time measurements, and the integration of personalized dosing algorithms. Despite advances in understanding, substantial gaps in evidence persist, highlighting the need for continued research and the development of standardized, evidence-based guidelines for anticoagulation management in ECMO patients.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Clinical Context and Significance

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation has emerged as an indispensable therapeutic modality for patients experiencing severe respiratory failure, cardiogenic shock, or cardiac arrest refractory to conventional management [1]. The utilization of ECMO has expanded considerably in recent years, with applications extending to cardiopulmonary resuscitation (eCPR), bridge-to-transplantation strategies, rewarming in accidental hypothermia, and trauma resuscitation [2]. This growth in ECMO utilization has correspondingly amplified the clinical importance of optimizing anticoagulation management, as bleeding and thrombotic complications remain among the most prevalent and consequential adverse events during ECMO support.

The fundamental challenge in ECMO anticoagulation stems from the inherent dysregulation of hemostasis that occurs when blood interfaces with the non-biologic surfaces of the extracorporeal circuit. Contact activation initiates both procoagulant and anticoagulant pathways, creating a prothrombotic state that necessitates pharmacologic anticoagulation to prevent circuit thrombosis, oxygenator failure, and systemic thromboembolic events [3]. Simultaneously, the systemic inflammatory response to circuit exposure, combined with the effects of critical illness and invasive cannulation, creates conditions that predispose patients to bleeding complications. This "yin-yang" relationship between thrombosis and hemorrhage defines the central dilemma facing clinicians managing anticoagulation in ECMO patients.

### 1.2 The Heparin Paradigm

Unfractionated heparin has maintained its position as the anticoagulant of choice for ECMO support despite the availability of alternative agents [4]. This preference reflects heparin's favorable pharmacologic properties, including its rapid onset of action, short half-life, and most importantly, the availability of protamine sulfate for complete reversal in cases of life-threatening bleeding. Additionally, UFH offers practical advantages of cost-effectiveness, widespread familiarity among clinicians, and ease of monitoring using standard laboratory assays [5].

However, the use of heparin during ECMO is complicated by several factors that complicate dosing and monitoring. The relationship between heparin dose and anticoagulant effect is highly variable and unpredictable in individual patients, influenced by factors including antithrombin III levels, acute phase reactants, renal function, and the inflammatory milieu of critical illness [6]. Furthermore, patients may develop heparin resistance due to antithrombin III depletion, requiring escalating doses to achieve therapeutic anticoagulation. Perhaps most concerning is the risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), a potentially catastrophic immune-mediated adverse reaction that presents particular diagnostic challenges in the ECMO population where thrombocytopenia is common for multiple reasons [7].

### 1.3 Scope of This Report

This report provides a comprehensive examination of heparin dosage management during ECMO support, addressing the following key questions: What are the current recommendations and practices regarding heparin dosing? How are anticoagulation targets established and monitored? What evidence supports different anticoagulation intensities? How do clinicians balance the competing risks of thrombosis and hemorrhage? What emerging strategies show promise for optimizing anticoagulation management? By synthesizing the available evidence, this analysis aims to provide clinicians with a thorough understanding of the current state of knowledge and practical approaches to this critical aspect of ECMO care.

## 2. Current Recommendations and Guidelines

### 2.1 Extracorporeal Life Support Organization Guidelines

The Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO), the primary professional society governing ECMO practice, recommends the use of antithrombotic therapy during ECMO support to prevent circuit thrombosis and venous and arterial thromboembolism [8]. However, the ELSO anticoagulation guidelines are notably descriptive rather than prescriptive, leaving substantial latitude for individual institutions to develop their own titration strategies. This approach acknowledges the limited high-quality evidence available to support specific recommendations while providing a framework for institutional protocol development.

Current ELSO guidelines recommend an initial heparin infusion rate of 7.5–20.0 units/kg/h, representing a remarkably broad range that reflects the uncertainty surrounding optimal dosing [9]. This wide range, combined with limited guidance regarding which laboratory tests should be used for monitoring, has contributed to the considerable variation in practice observed across ECMO centers worldwide. The guideline's permissive stance toward institutional protocol development has resulted in a heterogeneous landscape of anticoagulation practices, with significant implications for patient outcomes and the ability to conduct comparative effectiveness research.

### 2.2 International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis Guidance

The Scientific and Standardization Committees on Perioperative and Critical Care Haemostasis and Thrombosis of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) have provided guidance on anticoagulation in adult ECMO patients [10]. This guidance acknowledges the persistent uncertainties in the field and emphasizes the need for individualized approaches that consider patient-specific factors including age, underlying conditions, and the nature of ECMO-induced coagulopathies. The ISTH guidance supports the use of lower initial heparin doses to minimize bleeding risks while maintaining efficacy, reflecting the growing evidence base supporting reduced-intensity anticoagulation strategies.

### 2.3 The Gap Between Guidelines and Practice

A notable phenomenon in ECMO anticoagulation is the divergence between formal guideline recommendations and evolving clinical practice. While international society guidelines have largely maintained consistent reference ranges for anticoagulation parameters over many years, the scientific literature and ongoing clinical studies indicate that routine practice has evolved in a more flexible and often lower-intensity direction [11]. This shift is particularly evident in the trend toward lowering reference ranges for activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), anti-factor Xa (anti-Xa) levels, and activated clotting time (ACT), as well as the administration of significantly lower anticoagulation doses than those historically recommended.

This discrepancy between guidelines and practice reflects the accumulating evidence from observational studies and clinical experience suggesting that more conservative anticoagulation strategies may achieve comparable thrombotic protection with reduced bleeding risk. However, the current evidence base is insufficiently robust to formally incorporate these practices into global guidelines, creating a situation where clinical innovation outpaces formal recommendation. This gap highlights the urgent need for high-quality randomized controlled trials to establish evidence-based standards for ECMO anticoagulation.

## 3. Heparin Dosing Strategies and Targets

### 3.1 Traditional Dosing Approaches

Historically, anticoagulation during ECMO was managed using standardized protocols targeting specific clotting times, with activated clotting time (ACT) serving as the primary monitoring parameter. The traditional therapeutic range for ACT during ECMO was established at 180–220 seconds, based largely on experience from cardiac surgery where ACT monitoring was originally validated [12]. This approach assumed a relatively predictable relationship between heparin dosing and the prolongation of clotting times, allowing clinicians to titrate heparin infusion rates to achieve target values.

However, the limitations of this approach have become increasingly apparent. The ACT can be influenced by numerous factors unrelated to heparin effect, including platelet count, urine output, fibrinogen level, body temperature, hemodilution, other coagulation factor deficiencies, and renal replacement therapy [13]. These variables, all of which are commonly present in critically ill ECMO patients, can significantly alter ACT values independent of actual heparin anticoagulation, potentially leading to inappropriate dose adjustments. Recognizing these limitations, many institutions have modified their protocols to avoid relying solely on ACT for coagulation status determination during ECMO.

### 3.2 Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time-Based Strategies

The activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) has emerged as an alternative or complementary monitoring parameter to ACT. The aPTT assesses the intrinsic and common pathways of coagulation and is theoretically more sensitive to the effects of heparin than ACT. Current practice patterns indicate that aPTT is the major monitoring strategy for heparin anticoagulation during ECMO, with average target ranges typically between 40 and 60 seconds [14].

Data from the EOLIA trial, which included adults on venovenous ECMO, demonstrated that targeting an aPTT of 40–55 seconds was associated with low rates of hemorrhagic stroke and acceptable rates of circuit exchange (28%) [15]. These findings suggest that relatively conservative aPTT targets may provide adequate anticoagulation for circuit protection while minimizing bleeding risk. However, the relationship between heparin doses and plasma aPTT levels has been shown to be weak, complicating the use of aPTT as a reliable monitoring tool [16].

A significant finding from recent research is the association between target aPTT levels and hemorrhagic complications in adults on ECMO support. Studies have demonstrated that maintaining PTT targets greater than 70 seconds significantly increases the risk of hemorrhagic events [17]. This observation supports the feasibility of managing ECMO patients with lower PTT targets to reduce hemorrhagic complications without correspondingly increasing thrombotic complications. The evidence suggests that clinicians should consider more conservative aPTT targets, particularly in patients at elevated bleeding risk.

### 3.3 Anti-Factor Xa Monitoring

Anti-factor Xa (anti-Xa) levels have gained increasing recognition as the most specific measure of heparin effect, offering advantages over clotting time-based monitoring. Unlike aPTT and ACT, anti-Xa levels are not affected by other potential changes in the coagulation system, such as decreased synthesis of coagulation proteins due to hepatic dysfunction, factor deficiencies, or the numerous other variables that influence clotting time measurements [18]. This specificity makes anti-Xa monitoring particularly attractive in the complex critical care environment of ECMO patients.

Recent evidence suggests that anti-Xa monitoring correlates better with unfractionated heparin dosing than aPTT, ACT, or aPTT ratio [19]. Studies comparing anti-Xa-based strategies with traditional monitoring approaches have shown fewer bleeding events and lower mortality without an increase in thrombotic events when anti-Xa monitoring is employed [20]. These findings have contributed to the growing adoption of anti-Xa monitoring as a primary or complementary strategy in many ECMO centers.

Target anti-Xa levels vary across institutions and patient populations. Some centers managing pediatric patients have reported benefit from using target anti-Xa levels of 0.3–0.8 units/mL to guide ECMO anticoagulation [21]. In contrast, data from the EOLIA trial in adults on venovenous ECMO indicated that targeting an anti-Xa level of 0.2–0.3 units/mL achieved favorable outcomes with low hemorrhagic stroke rates [22]. More recent comprehensive reviews suggest target ranges of 0.3–0.7 U/mL, with corresponding aPTT targets of 50–90 seconds or ACT targets of 180–220 seconds [23].

### 3.4 Low-Dose Versus Standard-Dose Heparin Strategies

A major development in ECMO anticoagulation practice has been the emergence of evidence supporting low-dose heparin strategies. A recent meta-analysis comparing low-dose versus standard-dose unfractionated heparin in patients requiring ECMO revealed significant findings regarding bleeding outcomes [24]. The low-dose group demonstrated substantially lower incidences of gastrointestinal bleeding (odds ratio: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.20–0.64) and surgical site hemorrhage (odds ratio: 0.43; 95% CI: 0.20–0.94) compared to the standard-dose group. Importantly, there were no statistical differences in mortality, successful ECMO weaning, or thrombotic event incidence between the groups.

These findings have important implications for clinical practice, suggesting that reduced-intensity anticoagulation strategies may achieve comparable circuit protection and thrombotic prevention while substantially reducing bleeding complications. The evidence supports a paradigm shift toward more conservative heparin dosing, particularly in patient populations where bleeding risk is elevated or where circuit characteristics may permit reduced anticoagulation intensity.

Additional studies have shown that using a lower-dose heparin regimen targeting an ACT of 140–160 seconds is associated with fewer major bleeding incidents compared to therapeutic-dose heparin regimens, with no corresponding increase in thrombotic events [25]. This further supports the safety and potential superiority of reduced-intensity anticoagulation in appropriately selected patients.

## 4. Monitoring Methodologies and Their Limitations

### 4.1 Traditional Coagulation Tests

The monitoring of anticoagulation during ECMO relies on a combination of traditional laboratory tests and increasingly, viscoelastic assays. Activated clotting time, activated partial thromboplastin time, and anti-Xa levels each offer distinct advantages and limitations that influence their utility in different clinical scenarios.

Activated clotting time, while historically the standard for ECMO anticoagulation monitoring, suffers from significant variability and lack of specificity for heparin effect. The test measures the time required for whole blood to clot following activation of the extrinsic pathway and is influenced by platelet function, fibrinogen, and numerous other factors [26]. In the ECMO population, where multiple factors may be abnormal simultaneously, ACT values may not reliably reflect the degree of anticoagulation achieved. Despite these limitations, ACT remains widely used, particularly in the operating room and during cannulation, due to its rapid turnaround time and point-of-care availability.

Activated partial thromboplastin time offers greater sensitivity to heparin than ACT and has become the most commonly used monitoring parameter in many ECMO centers. However, aPTT is also subject to numerous preanalytical and analytical variables that can affect results, including reagent variability, sample handling, and patient-specific factors such as elevated factor VIII levels or lupus anticoagulant [27]. The weak association between heparin doses and plasma aPTT levels observed in clinical studies further complicates the use of aPTT as a reliable monitoring tool [28].

### 4.2 Anti-Factor Xa Assays

Anti-factor Xa assays directly measure the ability of heparin to potentiate antithrombin inhibition of factor Xa, providing a more specific assessment of heparin anticoagulant effect. This specificity represents a significant advantage over clotting time-based tests, as anti-Xa levels are not confounded by many of the factors that affect aPTT and ACT [29].

The anti-Xa assay is typically performed using plasma samples and requires calibration against heparin standards. Results are reported in units per milliliter (U/mL) or international units per milliliter (IU/mL), with therapeutic targets established based on clinical outcome data. While anti-Xa monitoring offers theoretical advantages, practical considerations including cost, availability, and turnaround time (typically requiring laboratory processing rather than point-of-care testing) have limited universal adoption.

Recent evidence suggesting superior correlation between anti-Xa levels and clinical outcomes, combined with fewer bleeding complications compared to aPTT-guided strategies, has accelerated the adoption of anti-Xa monitoring in many ECMO programs [30]. However, the optimal target range remains debated, with pediatric and adult populations potentially requiring different targets, and institutional variation in practice patterns continuing.

### 4.3 Viscoelastic Assays

Viscoelastic coagulation testing, including rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) and thromboelastography (TEG), represents an emerging approach to anticoagulation monitoring that offers several potential advantages over traditional tests. These point-of-care assays provide a global assessment of clot formation and stability, measuring the dynamics of coagulation from initial fibrin formation through clot strengthening and eventual lysis [31].

Viscoelastic testing allows for real-time assessment of the overall coagulation status, enabling clinicians to distinguish between hypocoagulable and hypercoagulable states and to guide not only anticoagulation management but also blood product administration. This comprehensive approach may be particularly valuable in ECMO patients, where the complex interplay between circuit-induced coagulopathy, underlying illness, and therapeutic interventions creates a dynamic and often unpredictable hemostatic environment.

The integration of viscoelastic testing into ECMO anticoagulation protocols is still evolving, and dedicated studies comparing viscoelastic-guided strategies with traditional monitoring approaches are needed. However, preliminary evidence suggests that viscoelastic testing may facilitate more personalized anticoagulation management and has emerged as an effective tool for improving therapy in selected centers [32].

### 4.4 Challenges in Monitoring

The persistent reliance on ACT as a principal method for monitoring unfractionated heparin therapy, despite recommendations from international societies supporting alternative approaches, reflects the complex realities of clinical practice [33]. Point-of-care availability, familiarity, and rapid turnaround time continue to make ACT attractive despite its known limitations. Additionally, the absence of high-quality evidence demonstrating superiority of alternative monitoring strategies has prevented a consensus shift away from ACT-based protocols.

A fundamental challenge in ECMO anticoagulation monitoring is the lack of a gold standard test that reliably predicts clinical outcomes. Each available test has limitations, and the relationship between laboratory values and actual thrombotic or bleeding risk remains imperfectly understood. This uncertainty contributes to the institutional variation in practice and highlights the need for continued research to identify optimal monitoring biomarkers and their corresponding therapeutic targets.

## 5. Balancing Thrombotic and Bleeding Risks

### 5.1 The Risk-Benefit Equation

The management of anticoagulation during ECMO requires continuous assessment of the competing risks of thrombosis and hemorrhage. Circuit thrombosis represents a serious complication that can lead to oxygenator failure, embolic events, and potentially fatal circuit dysfunction. Conversely, bleeding complications are associated with increased mortality, transfusion requirements, and prolonged intensive care unit stay [34]. The optimal anticoagulation strategy must navigate these risks, achieving sufficient anticoagulation to prevent circuit thrombosis while avoiding excessive anticoagulation that precipitates bleeding.

The incidence of bleeding events during ECMO support is substantial, with studies reporting that bleeding occurs during approximately 16.5% of ECMO support days [35]. Bleeding events most frequently occur at the cannulation site, though gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and surgical site bleeding are also common. The incidence of bleeding varies over time and gradually increases over the course of ECMO support, suggesting that cumulative exposure to anticoagulation and progressive critical illness contribute to bleeding risk.

Notably, bleeding is accompanied by thrombotic events in approximately 21% of venovenous ECMO-supported patients, highlighting the frequent coexistence of these complications [36]. This observation suggests that the hemostatic dysregulation in ECMO patients creates a complex environment where both thrombosis and bleeding may occur simultaneously, complicating management decisions.

### 5.2 Patient-Specific Risk Factors

The optimal anticoagulation strategy must be individualized based on patient-specific factors that influence both thrombotic and bleeding risk. Patient age, underlying conditions, and ECMO-induced coagulopathies all complicate management and require consideration when establishing anticoagulation targets [37].

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia presents a particular challenge in the ECMO population. HIT may be difficult to diagnose given the multifactorial nature of thrombocytopenia and hypercoagulability in ECMO patients [38]. The incidence of HIT in ECMO patients varies across studies, but the consequences of missed diagnosis can be severe, including catastrophic thrombosis. When HIT is suspected or confirmed, alternative anticoagulants such as direct thrombin inhibitors (bivalirudin or argatroban) are recommended [39].

Heparin resistance, typically defined as the requirement for unusually high heparin doses to achieve target anticoagulation parameters, may occur due to antithrombin III depletion, increased heparin clearance, or elevated levels of heparin-binding proteins [40]. Management of heparin resistance may require antithrombin III supplementation or transition to alternative anticoagulants.

Body habitus also influences anticoagulation management, with elevated BMI associated with more frequent circuit changes and higher heparin dose requirements [41]. Some protocols titrate heparin based on weight, necessitating higher doses in patients with higher BMI to achieve therapeutic targets.

### 5.3 Circuit-Related Considerations

The characteristics of the extracorporeal circuit influence anticoagulation requirements and thrombotic risk. Circuit age, oxygenator type, and pump configuration may all affect the thrombogenicity of the circuit and the intensity of anticoagulation required. Circuit exchange rates provide a clinical indicator of inadequate anticoagulation, with higher exchange rates suggesting insufficient anticoagulation, though the relationship between anticoagulation parameters and circuit longevity is complex.

Data from the EOLIA trial demonstrated acceptable circuit exchange rates (28%) with relatively conservative anticoagulation targets (aPTT 40–55 seconds or anti-Xa 0.2–0.3 U/mL) [42]. This finding suggests that aggressive anticoagulation may not be necessary for circuit preservation in many patients and supports the safety of reduced-intensity strategies.

### 5.4 Temporal Patterns of Risk

The risk of both thrombosis and bleeding varies over the course of ECMO support. Bleeding incidence gradually increases over time, potentially reflecting cumulative anticoagulation exposure, progressive critical illness, and the development of acquired coagulopathies [43]. Thrombotic risk may be highest during specific phases of ECMO support, such as during cannulation or circuit manipulation.

These temporal patterns suggest that anticoagulation strategies may need to be adjusted over time, with more conservative targets during periods of elevated bleeding risk and potentially more aggressive anticoagulation during high-risk thrombotic periods. Dynamic risk assessment and corresponding adjustment of anticoagulation targets may improve outcomes compared to fixed-target approaches.

## 6. Alternative and Adjunctive Anticoagulant Strategies

### 6.1 Direct Thrombin Inhibitors

While unfractionated heparin remains the standard anticoagulant for ECMO, direct thrombin inhibitors (DTIs) have emerged as important alternatives in specific clinical scenarios. Bivalirudin and argatroban are the primary DTIs used in ECMO patients, each offering distinct pharmacologic profiles and monitoring requirements [44].

DTIs are particularly valuable in patients with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, where continued heparin exposure poses unacceptable thrombotic risk. Additionally, DTIs may be preferred in patients with antithrombin III deficiency or heparin resistance, where adequate anticoagulation with heparin may be difficult to achieve [45]. Some centers routinely use DTIs even in non-HIT patients, citing more predictable pharmacokinetics and stable anticoagulant effect.

Bivalirudin is a reversible direct thrombin inhibitor with a relatively short half-life and predictable dose-response relationship. It is monitored using aPTT, with typical target ranges of 46–65 seconds reported in ECMO studies [46]. Argatroban, another direct thrombin inhibitor, is hepatically metabolized and may be preferred in patients with renal impairment.

The use of DTIs in ECMO is limited by cost, availability, and the lack of reversal agents, though the shorter half-lives of these agents compared to heparin provide some inherent safety advantage. Ongoing research continues to define the optimal role of DTIs in ECMO anticoagulation.

### 6.2 Low Molecular Weight Heparin

Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH), most commonly enoxaparin, is occasionally used for anticoagulation during ECMO, particularly in venovenous ECMO configurations [47]. LMWH offers more predictable pharmacokinetics than unfractionated heparin, with less interpatient variability in anticoagulant effect and once- or twice-daily dosing options.

The predictable pharmacokinetics of LMWH reduce the need for frequent laboratory monitoring, potentially simplifying anticoagulation management. However, challenges in dosing and reversal limit its application in the ECMO population, where rapid titration and complete reversibility may be required for bleeding complications [48].

Studies have explored enoxaparin as an alternative to UFH in ECMO patients, with some evidence supporting higher anti-Xa targets for stable anticoagulation [49]. However, the lack of validated reversal agents and limited experience in critically ill ECMO patients has prevented widespread adoption of LMWH as a primary anticoagulant.

### 6.3 Adjunctive Therapies

Prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) has been used adjunctively in ECMO patients for its vasodilatory and antithrombotic properties [50]. PGE1 may be particularly valuable in challenging ECMO cases where standard anticoagulation strategies are insufficient or poorly tolerated. The mechanism of action involves inhibition of platelet aggregation, which may provide additional antithrombotic protection in high-risk patients.

The role of adjunctive therapies in ECMO anticoagulation remains incompletely defined, and their use is typically reserved for specific clinical scenarios rather than routine application. Further research is needed to establish the optimal indications and dosing strategies for adjunctive anticoagulant therapies.

## 7. Emerging Strategies and Future Directions

### 7.1 Personalized Dosing Algorithms

The recognition that standard anticoagulation protocols may not be optimal for all patients has driven interest in personalized dosing algorithms that integrate patient-specific factors to guide anticoagulation management. These approaches leverage pharmacokinetic principles and clinical data to predict individual heparin requirements and therapeutic targets [51].

Personalized anticoagulation protocols may incorporate factors such as patient weight, renal function, hepatic function, antithrombin levels, and inflammatory markers to establish initial dosing and predict dose-response relationships. Machine learning approaches are being explored to identify patterns in clinical and laboratory data that predict anticoagulation requirements and bleeding or thrombotic risk.

The development of institutional guidelines that standardize anticoagulation therapy while allowing for individualization based on patient characteristics represents an important trend in ECMO care [52]. These sophisticated protocols aim to reduce practice variation while maintaining the flexibility to adapt to individual patient needs.

### 7.2 Point-of-Care Viscoelastic Testing

The integration of point-of-care viscoelastic coagulation testing into ECMO anticoagulation protocols represents a significant advance in management strategy. ROTEM and TEG provide rapid, comprehensive assessment of coagulation status, enabling real-time adjustment of anticoagulation and blood product therapy [53].

Viscoelastic testing offers several potential advantages over traditional monitoring approaches. It provides information about the entire coagulation process, from initial fibrin formation through clot stability and lysis, rather than measuring isolated components of the coagulation cascade. This global assessment may be particularly valuable in ECMO patients, where multiple components of the coagulation system may be simultaneously affected.

The use of viscoelastic testing to guide anticoagulation management is still evolving, and dedicated studies comparing viscoelastic-guided strategies with traditional approaches are needed. However, preliminary evidence suggests that these tests may facilitate more personalized and responsive anticoagulation management, potentially improving outcomes by enabling more precise titration.

### 7.3 Risk Prediction Tools

The development of dedicated prediction tools to assess risk for bleeding and thrombosis represents a promising approach to personalized anticoagulation management [54]. These tools could help clinicians identify patients at elevated risk for either complication and adjust anticoagulation strategies accordingly.

Given the variability of bleeding risk across patients and within individual patients over time, such prediction tools would need to be dynamic and capable of updating risk profiles as clinical conditions change. Machine learning approaches may enable the development of adaptive risk models that incorporate real-time clinical data to guide anticoagulation decisions.

The integration of risk prediction tools into clinical decision support systems could facilitate more nuanced anticoagulation management, moving beyond fixed-target approaches toward individualized strategies that balance patient-specific risks and benefits.

### 7.4 No-Anticoagulation Strategies

A growing body of data suggests that certain ECMO patient populations may demonstrate superior outcomes in the absence of anticoagulation [55]. Several retrospective studies report comparable thrombosis rates with fewer hemorrhagic complications and reduced transfusion requirements in patients managed without continuous anticoagulation.

These findings challenge the paradigm of routine therapeutic anticoagulation for all ECMO patients and raise important questions about patient selection for anticoagulation-free management. However, the conclusions from these studies are limited by retrospective design with inconsistent reporting of outcomes, and current guidelines continue to support routine use of therapeutic anticoagulation [56].

The potential for selected patients to be managed without anticoagulation represents an important area for future research. Randomized controlled trials comparing anticoagulated versus non-anticoagulated ECMO management in appropriately selected patients could help define the role of no-anticoagulation strategies.

### 7.5 Research Priorities

The field of ECMO anticoagulation is characterized by significant knowledge gaps that warrant focused research attention. Priority areas include randomized controlled trials comparing different anticoagulation intensities, head-to-head comparisons of monitoring strategies, and studies evaluating the role of viscoelastic testing in guiding anticoagulation management.

A current randomized clinical trial investigating low-molecular-weight heparin and unfractionated heparin with different anticoagulation targets is expected to provide further guidance regarding these important questions [57]. The results of this and similar studies will be essential for updating clinical practice guidelines and establishing evidence-based standards for ECMO anticoagulation.

The development of standardized outcome definitions and reporting frameworks for studies of ECMO anticoagulation would facilitate meta-analysis and evidence synthesis, accelerating the generation of actionable clinical guidance. International collaboration and registry-based research will be essential for generating the sample sizes necessary to detect meaningful differences in rare but important outcomes.

## 8. Practical Recommendations for Clinical Practice

### 8.1 Initial Heparin Dosing

Based on the available evidence, initial heparin dosing during ECMO support should generally target the lower end of recommended ranges to minimize bleeding risk while maintaining adequate anticoagulation for circuit protection. An initial infusion rate of 7.5–10.0 units/kg/h represents a reasonable starting point for most adult patients, with adjustment based on monitoring results and clinical assessment [58].

Lower initial doses may be appropriate for patients at elevated bleeding risk, including those with recent surgery, coagulopathy, or thrombocytopenia. Conversely, patients with identified thrombotic risk factors or circuit-related concerns may require higher initial doses, though this should be balanced against bleeding risk.

### 8.2 Monitoring Strategy

A multimodal monitoring approach incorporating anti-Xa levels as the primary parameter, supplemented by aPTT and clinical assessment, represents current best practice [59]. Anti-Xa targets of 0.3–0.5 U/mL provide a reasonable starting range for most adult patients, with adjustment based on clinical context and bleeding or thrombotic events.

The frequency of monitoring should be individualized based on patient stability and the trajectory of anticoagulation parameters. More frequent monitoring is warranted during the initial stabilization period, after dose changes, or when clinical status changes. Once stable, monitoring frequency may be reduced, though ongoing vigilance for both over-anticoagulation and under-anticoagulation is essential.

### 8.3 Target Adjustment

Anticoagulation targets should be adjusted based on ongoing clinical assessment of bleeding and thrombotic risk. Conservative targets (anti-Xa 0.2–0.3 U/mL or aPTT 40–55 seconds) may be appropriate for patients at elevated bleeding risk or during periods of clinical stability [60]. More aggressive targets (anti-Xa 0.5–0.7 U/mL or aPTT 70–90 seconds) may be considered for patients with documented circuit thrombosis, recurrent thromboembolic events, or other indicators of inadequate anticoagulation.

The maintenance of aPTT targets greater than 70 seconds should be avoided when possible, given the association between elevated aPTT and hemorrhagic complications [61]. If targets above 70 seconds are necessary, careful monitoring for bleeding complications is essential.

### 8.4 Institutional Protocols

The development of institutional protocols that standardize anticoagulation management while allowing for individualization based on patient-specific factors is strongly recommended [62]. These protocols should specify initial dosing, monitoring parameters and targets, dose adjustment algorithms, and criteria for hematology consultation or transition to alternative anticoagulants.

The use of anticoagulation protocols has been associated with decreased hemorrhagic complications compared to non-protocolized management [63]. Institutional protocols should be developed collaboratively with input from ECMO specialists, hematologists, pharmacists, and critical care physicians, and should be regularly reviewed and updated based on emerging evidence and institutional experience.

### 8.5 Special Populations

Special consideration is warranted for specific patient populations. Patients with suspected or confirmed heparin-induced thrombocytopenia should be transitioned to direct thrombin inhibitors [64]. Patients with antithrombin III deficiency may require supplementation or alternative anticoagulants to achieve adequate anticoagulation. Pediatric patients may benefit from different target ranges than adults, with some evidence supporting anti-Xa targets of 0.3–0.8 U/mL in children [65].

Obese patients may require weight-based dosing with careful monitoring to avoid over-anticoagulation. Patients receiving renal replacement therapy present particular challenges due to altered heparin pharmacokinetics and require individualized management strategies.

## 9. Conclusion

The management of heparin dosage during ECMO support represents a complex clinical challenge that requires careful balancing of thrombotic and bleeding risks. Despite the widespread use of ECMO and decades of clinical experience, significant uncertainty persists regarding optimal anticoagulation strategies, with considerable variation in practice across institutions worldwide.

The evidence reviewed in this report supports several key conclusions. First, unfractionated heparin remains the anticoagulant of choice for most ECMO patients, though alternative agents have important roles in specific clinical scenarios. Second, reduced-intensity anticoagulation strategies appear safe and may be preferable to traditional therapeutic-dose approaches, with lower bleeding complications and comparable thrombotic protection. Third, anti-Xa monitoring offers advantages over traditional clotting time-based tests and should be incorporated into monitoring strategies. Fourth, institutional protocols that standardize care while allowing individualization improve outcomes. Fifth, emerging approaches including viscoelastic testing, personalized dosing algorithms, and dynamic risk prediction tools show promise for optimizing anticoagulation management.

The gap between evolving clinical practice and formal guideline recommendations reflects the current state of evidence, which is largely derived from observational studies and expert opinion rather than high-quality randomized trials. This situation underscores the urgent need for rigorous comparative effectiveness research to establish evidence-based standards for ECMO anticoagulation.

In the interim, clinicians should adopt a patient-centered approach that integrates the best available evidence with individual patient assessment and institutional experience. The goal of anticoagulation management during ECMO is not to achieve a specific laboratory value, but rather to prevent circuit thrombosis and systemic thromboembolism while avoiding life-threatening hemorrhage. Achieving this goal requires ongoing vigilance, frequent reassessment, and willingness to adjust strategies based on clinical course and emerging complications.

As ECMO utilization continues to expand, the importance of optimizing anticoagulation management will only increase. The development of standardized, evidence-based approaches to heparin dosing and monitoring has the potential to improve outcomes for the growing population of patients requiring this life-supporting therapy. Continued research, collaboration among ECMO centers, and engagement with professional societies to update guidelines based on emerging evidence will be essential for advancing the field and ensuring that all ECMO patients receive optimal anticoagulation care.
