# How Decision Priorities Are Established in Autonomous Weapon Systems to Protect Civilian Life: A Technical and Legal Analysis

## Executive Summary

The establishment of decision priorities in autonomous weapon systems (AWS) to protect civilian life represents one of the most consequential technical and ethical challenges in contemporary military affairs. This report examines the multifaceted framework through which AWS are designed to make life-and-death decisions in compliance with International Humanitarian Law (IHL), analyzing the competing claims of algorithmic superiority against the persistent limitations of computational systems in replicating human moral judgment. The analysis reveals that while proponents argue that AWS can apply IHL principles with mathematical objectivity unburdened by human psychological frailties, substantial technical, legal, and ethical challenges complicate this thesis. The concept of "meaningful human control" has emerged as the central normative framework governing AWS deployment, requiring human oversight in targeting and engagement decisions. However, the "black-box" nature of targeting algorithms, dataset biases that systematically misclassify civilians as combatants, and the fundamental inability of algorithms to perform qualitative proportionality assessments present significant obstacles to civilian protection. This report concludes that effective civilian protection in AWS operations requires a comprehensive regulatory framework combining clear prohibitions, meaningful human control requirements, robust accountability mechanisms, and technical measures ensuring predictability, reliability, and explainability in autonomous targeting systems.

## 1. Introduction: The Ethical Imperative of Civilian Protection

The protection of civilian life during armed conflict stands as a foundational principle of international humanitarian law, codified across multiple treaties and recognized as customary international law. The emergence of autonomous weapon systems—weapons capable of selecting and engaging targets without human intervention—poses unprecedented challenges to this protective framework. As these systems become increasingly sophisticated, the question of how decision priorities are established to safeguard civilian populations has become a critical concern for policymakers, military strategists, ethicists, and international legal scholars.

The debate surrounding AWS and civilian protection centers on a fundamental tension: can machines be trusted to make the nuanced judgments required to distinguish combatants from civilians and to balance military necessity against collateral harm? Proponents of AWS argue that algorithmic decision-making, freed from human emotional biases such as fear, fatigue, and vengefulness, can apply IHL principles with a consistency and precision that human operators cannot achieve [1]. This "algorithmic superiority thesis" draws rhetorical strength from historical IHL violations attributed to human psychological frailties, suggesting that machines could prevent atrocities such as the My Lai massacre or Abu Ghraib scandal [1].

However, this optimistic assessment faces substantial counterarguments from technical analyses, legal scholars, and human rights organizations. The inherent limitations of current artificial intelligence systems in understanding context, the "black-box" nature of targeting algorithms that prevents meaningful scrutiny of decision-making processes, and the accountability deficits that arise when lethal force is applied by autonomous systems all complicate claims of enhanced civilian protection [1][2]. Furthermore, the absence of a comprehensive international legal framework specifically governing AWS creates what many scholars describe as a "serious legal vacuum" that leaves victims without adequate mechanisms for seeking accountability, guarantees of non-repetition, and compensation for damages [3].

This report provides an in-depth analysis of how decision priorities are established in AWS to protect civilian life, examining the technical capabilities and limitations of autonomous targeting systems, the legal frameworks governing their use, the concept of meaningful human control as a protective mechanism, and the ongoing international efforts to establish binding regulations. The analysis reveals that effective civilian protection requires a multi-faceted approach combining human oversight, technical safeguards, robust accountability mechanisms, and comprehensive international legal frameworks.

## 2. The Algorithmic Superiority Thesis: Promise and Limitations

### 2.1 The Case for Machine-Based Decision-Making

The contention that AWS inherently surpass human soldiers in adhering to IHL by eliminating emotional bias and enhancing targeting precision constitutes a central pillar of their purported ethical legitimacy [1]. Proponents argue that algorithmic decision-making, unburdened by human fatigue, fear, or vengefulness, can apply IHL principles—particularly distinction and proportionality—with mathematical objectivity impossible for fallible human operators [1]. This perspective, articulated most prominently by scholars such as Arkin (2009) and Scharre (2018), suggests that autonomous systems could potentially reduce civilian casualties by applying consistent, unbiased analysis to targeting decisions [1].

The United States government has embraced elements of this argument in its policy framework for autonomous weapons. The March 2018 white paper "Humanitarian Benefits of Emerging Technologies in the Area of Lethal Autonomous Weapons" notes that "automated target identification, tracking, selection, and engagement functions can allow weapons to strike military objectives more accurately and with less risk of collateral damage or civilian casualties" [4]. This position reflects a broader technological optimism that advances in sensor technology, machine learning, and data processing can overcome the limitations that have historically contributed to civilian harm in armed conflict.

The algorithmic superiority thesis draws particular rhetorical strength from documented cases of IHL violations attributed to human psychological factors. Research in military psychology has extensively documented how stress, fear, and group dynamics can impair judgment and lead to violations of the laws of war. By removing human operators from the immediate decision-making loop, AWS proponents argue that these psychological variables can be eliminated from the targeting process, resulting in more consistent application of IHL principles [1].

### 2.2 Technical Limitations and Systematic Trade-offs

Despite these theoretical advantages, empirical evidence reveals systematic trade-offs between computational speed and contextual discernment that significantly complicate claims of algorithmic superiority [1]. Autonomous targeting systems like the U.S. Project Maven prioritize rapid threat identification within massive datasets, inevitably sacrificing nuanced interpretation of ambiguous scenarios—precisely where IHL's distinction principle demands careful judgment [1].

Technical analyses of targeting algorithms reveal that their "black-box" nature inherently obstructs proportionality assessments, as these systems cannot articulate the rationale for civilian risk trade-offs [1]. This represents a fundamental violation of the IHL principle of precaution, which requires that those planning and conducting attacks do everything feasible to verify that targets are military objectives and to minimize civilian harm [1]. When algorithms cannot explain why a particular targeting decision was made, human reviewers cannot assess whether that decision complied with IHL requirements.

The problem of algorithmic bias presents another significant challenge to civilian protection. Algorithms trained to identify "armed males" in Afghanistan systematically misclassified farmers carrying tools as combatants due to dataset biases [1]. Such errors compound in asymmetric conflicts where combatants deliberately blend with civilian populations—a context requiring situational awareness that no sensor array can currently replicate [1]. The fundamental challenge is that machine learning systems can only recognize patterns present in their training data, and when that data reflects biased or incomplete representations of reality, the resulting decisions will perpetuate and potentially amplify those biases.

### 2.3 The Qualitative Dimension of Proportionality Assessment

Perhaps the most fundamental limitation of AWS in protecting civilian life relates to the qualitative nature of proportionality assessments under IHL. As Amoroso and Tamburrini (2021) note, proportionality assessments necessitate qualitative balancing of values—for example, weighing a tactical advantage against the destruction of a school—that algorithms reduce to numerical thresholds, inevitably flattening moral complexity into programmable metrics [1].

The principle of proportionality, as codified in Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions and recognized as customary international law, requires that attacks which may be expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, damage to civilian objects, or a combination thereof, which would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated, are prohibited [5]. This assessment requires judgment about the relative value of different types of harm—military advantage versus civilian casualties, tactical gains versus destruction of civilian infrastructure—that resists quantification.

Human decision-makers can draw on moral intuition, contextual understanding, and ethical training to navigate these difficult trade-offs. Autonomous systems, by contrast, must be programmed with specific thresholds and weighting functions that inevitably simplify complex moral calculations. The question of whether such simplifications can adequately protect civilian life remains a subject of intense scholarly debate, with significant implications for the development and deployment of AWS.

## 3. International Humanitarian Law Framework and Its Application to AWS

### 3.1 The Applicability of Existing IHL Frameworks

International humanitarian law continues to apply fully to all weapons systems, including the potential development and use of lethal autonomous weapons systems [6]. This principle, affirmed by the Group of Governmental Experts on Emerging Technologies in the Area of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (GGE) operating under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), establishes that AWS cannot operate outside the existing legal framework governing armed conflict [6].

IHL imposes obligations on States, parties to armed conflict, and individuals—not machines [6]. This principle has profound implications for AWS development and deployment: while autonomous systems may execute targeting decisions, legal responsibility rests with human actors who plan, authorize, and conduct attacks. The requirement that humans remain responsible for IHL compliance creates both a legal obligation and a practical constraint on the degree of autonomy that can be delegated to weapon systems.

The fundamental principles of IHL that govern targeting decisions include the principle of distinction, which requires parties to distinguish between civilians and combatants and between civilian and military objects; the principle of proportionality, which prohibits attacks expected to cause excessive civilian harm relative to military advantage; and the principle of precaution, which requires all feasible measures to verify targets and minimize civilian harm [5][7]. Each of these principles presents distinct challenges when applied to autonomous weapon systems.

### 3.2 The Principle of Distinction and Target Identification

The principle of distinction consists of two components: parties in an armed conflict must distinguish between civilians and combatants, as well as between civilian and military objects [5]. The target of an attack may only be military objects and combatants. This principle is codified in Article 48 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions and constitutes a customary rule of IHL applicable to all States [5].

For autonomous weapon systems to comply with the principle of distinction, they must be capable of accurately identifying whether potential targets are combatants or civilians. This requires not only recognizing physical characteristics but also understanding the legal concept of "direct participation in hostilities," which defines when civilians lose their protection from attack [5]. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia has provided guidance on behaviors that constitute direct participation in hostilities, but applying these standards in dynamic operational environments requires contextual judgment that current AI systems struggle to replicate [5].

The challenge of distinguishing combatants from civilians is particularly acute in asymmetric conflicts where non-state armed groups deliberately conceal themselves among civilian populations. In such contexts, effective distinction requires not only technological sensors but also intelligence analysis, understanding of local conditions, and the ability to interpret ambiguous behaviors—all capabilities that remain beyond the current state of autonomous systems [1].

### 3.3 The Principle of Proportionality and Value Balancing

The principle of proportionality requires that anticipated civilian harm not be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage expected from an attack [5][7]. This principle requires qualitative judgments about the relative value of different types of harm—judgments that current autonomous systems are ill-equipped to make.

Proportionality assessments must consider multiple factors including the strategic consequences of potential harm, the status of individuals facing potential harm, and the sociocultural and psychological indicators through which intentions and actions are classified as military rather than civilian [5]. For example, distinguishing a civilian holding a large piece of metal from an armed combatant with a rifle in civilian clothing requires contextual interpretation that goes beyond pattern recognition [5].

The reduction of proportionality assessments to numerical thresholds—a necessary step for algorithmic implementation—inevitably loses the moral complexity inherent in these decisions. As scholars have noted, algorithms cannot replicate the qualitative balancing of values that proportionality requires, leading to systematic errors when autonomous systems encounter scenarios not well-represented in their training data [1].

### 3.4 The Principle of Precaution and Verification Requirements

The principle of precaution requires that those planning and conducting attacks take all feasible precautions to verify that targets are military objectives and to minimize civilian harm [1][5]. For autonomous weapon systems, this principle creates significant challenges related to the "black-box" nature of targeting algorithms.

Technical analyses reveal that autonomous targeting systems often cannot articulate the rationale for their decisions, making it impossible for human reviewers to verify whether appropriate precautions were taken [1]. When an algorithm identifies a potential target, the features or patterns it used to reach that conclusion may be opaque even to the system's developers. This opacity prevents the meaningful verification required by the precaution principle and creates accountability gaps when targeting decisions result in civilian harm.

The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) has stated that "human control at the development stage alone—control in design—will not be sufficient to ensure compliance with IHL in the use of an autonomous weapon system for attacks in armed conflict given the inherently variable and unpredictable nature of real-world operational environments" [8]. This assessment underscores the need for ongoing human oversight throughout the targeting process, not merely during system design.

## 4. Meaningful Human Control: The Central Normative Framework

### 4.1 Conceptual Foundations and Elements

The concept of "meaningful human control" has emerged as the central normative framework governing autonomous weapon systems, representing a consensus reached at the UN CCW in Geneva in May 2014 [2]. This framework seeks to ensure that human judgment remains central to decisions involving the use of lethal force, even when autonomous systems perform supporting functions.

Meaningful human control can be decomposed into two essential elements [2]. First, "human control" clarifies that the operator of an autonomous weapon system controls targets and makes strike decisions, consistent with basic humanitarian thinking. This element requires that humans retain the ability to select targets and authorize attacks, even when autonomous systems assist in target identification or tracking. Second, "meaningful" refers to the final decision made by human beings after weighing various factors in compliance with relevant regulations of international humanitarian law [2].

The distinction between human control and meaningful human control is crucial. A system may involve human operators who technically "control" the weapon while lacking the information, time, or capability to exercise meaningful judgment over targeting decisions. Meaningful human control requires not merely human involvement but human judgment that is informed, deliberate, and capable of ensuring IHL compliance [2][9].

### 4.2 Components of Meaningful Human Control

Human rights organizations and academic scholars have developed detailed frameworks for operationalizing meaningful human control over autonomous weapon systems. These frameworks identify intersecting components spanning decision-making, technological, and operational dimensions [9].

The decision-making components require that operators understand how an autonomous system works—its capabilities, limitations, and decision-making processes [9]. Without such understanding, operators cannot meaningfully evaluate system recommendations or intervene when targeting decisions appear problematic. This requirement has significant implications for system design, training, and operational procedures.

Technological components include predictability, reliability, and the ability of the operator to intervene in system operations [9]. Predictability ensures that system behavior can be anticipated and evaluated against IHL requirements. Reliability ensures consistent performance across operational conditions. The ability to intervene requires not only technical capability to override system decisions but sufficient time and information for meaningful human intervention before force is applied.

Operational components include geographical and temporal constraints on areas of operation and limits on types of targets [9]. These constraints recognize that autonomous systems may be appropriate for certain limited applications while being unsuitable for others. By restricting operational parameters, militaries can deploy autonomous systems in contexts where their limitations are less likely to result in civilian harm.

### 4.3 Challenges in Implementing Meaningful Human Control

Despite broad consensus on the importance of meaningful human control, significant challenges exist in implementing this framework in practice. The technical challenges in exercising effective human control through human-machine interaction are substantial [8]. Autonomous systems may operate at speeds that preclude meaningful human intervention, particularly in complex, fast-moving operational environments.

The "black-box" nature of advanced machine learning systems poses particular challenges for meaningful human control. When operators cannot understand why a system has identified a particular target, they cannot meaningfully evaluate whether that identification complies with IHL [1]. This opacity undermines the core purpose of meaningful human control—to ensure that human judgment informs decisions involving the use of lethal force.

Additionally, the distributed nature of responsibility in modern military operations can create accountability gaps that undermine meaningful human control. When targeting decisions result from interactions between multiple human actors, autonomous systems, and algorithmic processes, determining who bears responsibility for compliance failures becomes extremely difficult [2]. This accountability deficit directly amplifies civilian risks despite claims of enhanced precision from autonomous systems [1].

## 5. Technical Challenges in Autonomous Targeting Decisions

### 5.1 Sensor Limitations and Environmental Factors

Current sensor and algorithmic technologies demonstrate both promise and limitations in distinguishing between combatants and civilians [10]. Sensor systems face inherent challenges in operational environments characterized by complex terrain, variable lighting conditions, and the deliberate concealment of combatants among civilian populations.

Decision support systems that use sensor processing and AI to inform targeting decisions have already been developed and are increasingly used in armed conflicts [9]. These tools, which rely on the same processes that automated weapon systems would use for targeting tasks, highlight multiple hurdles that technology faces in distinguishing between combatants and civilians [9]. For example, tools that depend on mobile phone data to locate and identify targets are unreliable because cell tower triangulation data does not provide precise locations and cannot determine with certainty who possesses specific devices [9].

The limitations of current sensing technology mean that autonomous systems will inevitably operate with incomplete or inaccurate information about their environment. When this incomplete information is processed through algorithms trained on potentially biased datasets, the resulting targeting decisions may systematically misidentify civilians as combatants, as documented in cases where farmers carrying tools were classified as armed combatants [1].

### 5.2 Algorithmic Bias and Dataset Limitations

Algorithmic bias represents a significant threat to civilian protection in autonomous weapon systems. Machine learning systems are trained on datasets that inevitably reflect the perspectives, assumptions, and limitations of their developers [9]. When these datasets are incomplete, unrepresentative, or reflect historical biases, the resulting systems will perpetuate and potentially amplify those biases in targeting decisions.

The problem of bias in AWS extends beyond technical limitations to fundamental questions about how categories like "combatant" and "civilian" are defined and operationalized in algorithmic systems. These categories involve legal and moral judgments that resist simple quantification. When developers translate legal standards into algorithmic criteria, they inevitably make interpretive choices that shape system behavior in ways that may not be apparent or intentional [9].

Once an autonomous weapon system using AI is deployed, insufficient understanding of how and why the system makes determinations could prevent a human operator from scrutinizing recommended targets and intervening to correct errors before force is applied [9]. This limitation is particularly concerning given documented cases of algorithmic bias disproportionately affecting certain populations, raising concerns about discrimination in targeting decisions.

### 5.3 The Explainability Problem

The "black-box" nature of targeting algorithms presents fundamental challenges for civilian protection [1]. When autonomous systems cannot explain their decision-making processes, human reviewers cannot assess whether those decisions complied with IHL requirements. This explainability problem has both legal and ethical dimensions.

From a legal perspective, the inability to explain targeting decisions prevents the verification required by the IHL principle of precaution [1]. Human reviewers cannot determine whether appropriate measures were taken to minimize civilian harm if they cannot understand how decisions were reached. This creates a gap between the legal requirement for precaution and the practical capability to verify compliance.

From an ethical perspective, the explainability problem undermines accountability for civilian harm. When autonomous systems cause civilian casualties and cannot explain why, determining responsibility for compliance failures becomes extremely difficult [1]. This accountability gap creates conditions in which civilian protection may be systematically undermined without clear responsibility for addressing failures.

The CCW Group of Experts' rolling text from November 2024 emphasizes that AWS must be "predictable, reliable, traceable, and explainable to maintain their lawful use under IHL" [6]. These requirements reflect growing recognition that technical transparency is essential for both legal compliance and civilian protection.

## 6. Accountability Deficits and Legal Gaps

### 6.1 The Accountability Vacuum

The use of autonomous weapon systems creates serious accountability challenges that directly affect civilian protection. Without meaningful human control, robots may not have the technical capability to make distinctions required by IHL, and the resulting accountability vacuum makes it difficult for victims to demand accountability, guarantees of non-repetition, and compensation for damages [2][3].

Accountability presupposes control [2]. When autonomous systems make targeting decisions without meaningful human oversight, determining who bears responsibility for compliance failures becomes extremely difficult. This problem is compounded by the distributed nature of responsibility in modern military operations, where targeting decisions may result from interactions between multiple human actors, autonomous systems, and algorithmic processes.

The accountability deficit directly amplifies civilian risks despite claims of enhanced precision from autonomous systems [1]. When no individual or institution can be clearly held responsible for civilian harm caused by autonomous weapons, the deterrent effect of accountability mechanisms is lost. This creates conditions in which errors may be repeated and systemic failures may persist without correction.

### 6.2 The Absence of Comprehensive International Regulation

Currently, there is no legally binding international framework specific to autonomous weapon systems that ensures meaningful human control over the use of force [3]. This regulatory gap creates serious problems for civilian protection in two respects: there is no way to prevent the development and use of weapons with autonomy in the critical functions of targeting and engaging, and it makes it difficult for victims to seek accountability and compensation [3].

While existing international humanitarian law, international criminal law, and international human rights law provide foundational rules and principles governing AWS, gaps in specificity and enforcement highlight the pressing need for a dedicated international treaty [6]. Such a treaty could harmonize interpretations, establish clear prohibitions and restrictions, and ensure accountability in the use of these technologies [6].

Approximately 30 countries and 165 nongovernmental organizations have called for a preemptive ban on lethal autonomous weapons systems due to ethical concerns, including concerns about operational risk, accountability for use, and compliance with the proportionality and distinction requirements of the law of war [4]. However, major military powers including the United States have not supported a comprehensive ban, instead advocating for continued development within existing policy frameworks.

### 6.3 Command Responsibility and Individual Accountability

Existing international legal frameworks provide for command responsibility and individual criminal responsibility for grave breaches of IHL, including violations arising from the use of AWS [6]. However, applying these frameworks to autonomous weapons presents significant challenges.

Command responsibility holds commanders liable for failures to prevent or punish violations by subordinates. When autonomous systems make targeting decisions, the relationship between commanders and these systems is fundamentally different from the relationship between commanders and human subordinates. The question of whether and how command responsibility applies to autonomous systems remains unresolved [10].

Individual criminal responsibility for those who plan, authorize, or conduct attacks using autonomous systems may provide a basis for accountability, but identifying the relevant individuals and establishing their culpability presents practical challenges. When targeting decisions result from algorithmic processes that no individual fully understands, determining who bears criminal responsibility for resulting violations becomes extremely difficult [10].

## 7. Proposed Regulatory Frameworks and International Initiatives

### 7.1 The Call for a Legally Binding Instrument

From the perspective of multiple States and civil society organizations, the only credible way to address autonomy in weapons systems is through the adoption of a new legally binding instrument [3]. The fundamental goal would be to regulate the autonomy of weapons systems in keeping with International Humanitarian Law, International Human Rights Law, and International Criminal Law [3].

A legally binding instrument on autonomous weapons must include clear prohibitions and regulations, aim to maintain meaningful human control over force, and include effective implementation, monitoring, and accountability measures [3]. This regulatory approach would address the current legal vacuum while allowing for continued development of beneficial technologies under appropriate safeguards.

The treaty should prohibit those weapons systems that would delegate targeting and attack decisions to autonomous functions, that would target human beings and civilian infrastructure, and that would profile humans based on algorithmic assessments [3]. These prohibitions would establish clear boundaries on acceptable autonomous weapon development while preserving human control over decisions involving lethal force.

### 7.2 Positive Obligations and Regulatory Components

Beyond prohibitions, the treaty should include positive obligations—regulations ensuring that meaningful human control is maintained in the use of all autonomous weapons systems that are not prohibited [9]. These positive obligations would operationalize the concept of meaningful human control through specific technical and procedural requirements.

The intersecting components of meaningful human control on which regulations could be based include decision-making components requiring that operators understand system capabilities and limitations; technological components requiring predictability, reliability, and intervention capability; and operational components including geographical and temporal constraints and limits on target types [9].

Additionally, the treaty should require meaningful, free, and informed participation of civil society in all forums on autonomous weapons, particularly organizations representative of marginalized groups who may be disproportionately affected by these systems [3]. This participatory requirement would ensure that diverse perspectives inform regulatory development and implementation.

### 7.3 Guiding Principles from the CCW Process

The CCW Group of Governmental Experts has developed guiding principles that inform ongoing discussions about autonomous weapons regulation. These principles affirm that international humanitarian law continues to apply fully to all weapons systems, including lethal autonomous weapons systems [6].

Human responsibility for decisions involving the use of force must be preserved, even as autonomous systems take on supporting functions [6]. A weapons system based on emerging technologies must not be used if it is of a nature to cause superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering, if it is inherently indiscriminate, or if it is otherwise incapable of being used in accordance with IHL requirements [6].

The GGE process has emphasized context-appropriate human oversight, particularly in morally and legally significant decisions such as during the identification and/or engagement of targets [6]. This emphasis on context-appropriate oversight recognizes that meaningful human control may require different levels of intervention depending on the operational situation.

## 8. Balancing Military Utility and Civilian Protection

### 8.1 Defensive Applications and Limited Operational Contexts

Scholars and policymakers have proposed that AWS may be appropriately employed in specific settings where their limitations are less likely to result in civilian harm [5]. These proposed applications include exclusively defensive operations and areas where the presence of civilians is highly unlikely [5].

In defensive applications, autonomous systems could protect military installations or personnel from attack without making targeting decisions that affect civilian populations. The narrow focus of such applications limits the potential for civilian harm while potentially providing security benefits. However, even defensive applications raise concerns about escalation and the potential for autonomous systems to make errors with serious consequences.

The limitation of AWS to contexts with minimal civilian presence reflects recognition that current technology cannot reliably distinguish combatants from civilians in complex environments [5]. By restricting operational parameters, militaries can deploy autonomous systems where their technical limitations pose reduced risks to civilian populations.

### 8.2 The Martens Clause and Ethical Considerations

The Martens Clause provides a link between ethical considerations and IHL, making it particularly relevant to the assessment of autonomous weapon systems [7]. It provides that, in cases not covered by existing treaties, civilians and combatants remain protected by customary IHL, the principles of humanity, and the dictates of the public conscience [7].

The principles of humanity serve as a universal reference point, preventing the assumption that anything not explicitly prohibited is permitted, thereby addressing new situations and new means and methods of warfare [7]. This clause has particular relevance for AWS, as existing treaties do not specifically address autonomous weapons.

With respect to the public conscience, there is a sense of deep discomfort with the idea of any weapon system that places the use of force beyond human control [7]. This ethical dimension of the debate extends beyond legal compliance to fundamental questions about the role of humans in decisions involving the taking of human life. The Martens Clause ensures that these ethical considerations inform the assessment of AWS, even in the absence of specific treaty provisions.

### 8.3 Integration with Human Rights Law

Autonomous weapon systems also present serious challenges in the context of human rights law, particularly the right to life [2]. The right to life has two components: prevention of arbitrary harm to life and accountability when that happens. The use of autonomous weapons without meaningful human control may violate both components.

It constitutes a violation of the right to life if the use of force causes loss of life, even if this includes failure to use due diligence to prevent it [2]. This implies that insufficient human control over the release of force may create conditions for arbitrary harm to life that violates human rights obligations. The integration of human rights law with IHL frameworks provides additional legal basis for regulating autonomous weapons and ensuring civilian protection.

## 9. Conclusion: Toward Effective Civilian Protection

The establishment of decision priorities in autonomous weapon systems to protect civilian life requires a comprehensive approach addressing technical, legal, and ethical dimensions. The analysis presented in this report reveals that while autonomous systems offer potential advantages in consistency and precision, significant challenges remain in ensuring that these systems can reliably protect civilian populations during armed conflict.

The algorithmic superiority thesis—that AWS can apply IHL principles with mathematical objectivity unburdened by human psychological frailties—faces substantial empirical and theoretical challenges. The "black-box" nature of targeting algorithms prevents the explainability required for meaningful IHL compliance verification. Dataset biases systematically misclassify civilians as combatants. The qualitative judgments required for proportionality assessments resist algorithmic implementation. And the accountability deficits created by autonomous decision-making undermine the deterrent effect of legal frameworks designed to prevent civilian harm.

The concept of meaningful human control has emerged as the central normative framework for addressing these challenges. By requiring that human operators understand system capabilities, retain intervention capability, and make final decisions after weighing relevant factors, this framework seeks to preserve human judgment at the center of decisions involving lethal force. However, implementing meaningful human control in practice requires addressing technical challenges related to system opacity, operational tempo, and distributed responsibility.

The absence of a comprehensive international legal framework specifically governing autonomous weapons creates a regulatory gap that undermines civilian protection. While existing IHL principles continue to apply, their application to autonomous systems raises novel questions that existing treaties do not address. A dedicated international treaty could harmonize interpretations, establish clear prohibitions and restrictions, and ensure accountability in the use of autonomous weapons.

Effective civilian protection in AWS operations requires combining multiple approaches: clear prohibitions on systems that delegate life-and-death decisions to machines or target protected categories; meaningful human control requirements ensuring human judgment in targeting decisions; technical measures ensuring predictability, reliability, and explainability; and robust accountability mechanisms providing remedies for civilian harm. Only through such a comprehensive approach can the international community ensure that autonomous weapon systems enhance rather than undermine the protection of civilian populations during armed conflict.

The decisions made in the coming years regarding autonomous weapons regulation will have profound implications for civilian protection, international humanitarian law, and the fundamental relationship between humans and machines in decisions involving the use of lethal force. The technical and legal analysis presented in this report underscores the urgency of establishing clear frameworks that preserve human control while permitting beneficial technological development—a balance essential for upholding the humanitarian principles that underpin international law.
