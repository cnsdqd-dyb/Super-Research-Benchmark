# The Mechanism of Satisfaction Evaluation Formation: An Analysis of the Gap Between Planning Expectations and Actual Experience

## Abstract

This research report examines the psychological and cognitive mechanisms through which satisfaction evaluations are formed when discrepancies emerge between pre-existing expectations and actual experiential outcomes. Drawing upon Expectation Confirmation Theory (ECT) and its extensions, this analysis explores the systematic process by which individuals cognitively process the gap between what they anticipate and what they actually experience. The report investigates the four core constructs of expectation formation, perceived performance, disconfirmation, and subsequent satisfaction judgments, while examining the moderating factors that influence the strength and direction of these relationships. Understanding this mechanism provides valuable insights for organizations seeking to manage customer satisfaction and influence post-experience behavioral intentions across diverse contexts including consumer markets, information systems, and service industries.

## 1. Introduction

The formation of satisfaction evaluations represents one of the most fundamental psychological processes underlying human decision-making and behavioral responses. When individuals engage with products, services, or technologies, they do so armed with anticipations and predictions about the likely outcomes of their experience. These anticipations—formed through various information channels, prior experiences, social influences, and marketing communications—establish a cognitive baseline against which actual performance is subsequently evaluated [1]. The relationship between these pre-experience expectations and post-experience satisfaction judgments has been the subject of extensive scholarly investigation over the past several decades, yielding robust theoretical frameworks that illuminate the underlying mechanisms at work.

The central premise of satisfaction formation theory holds that individuals do not evaluate experiences in isolation but rather through a comparative process that involves assessing actual performance against a reference point established by prior expectations. This comparative process, termed "disconfirmation" in the academic literature, generates positive or negative discrepancies that subsequently drive satisfaction or dissatisfaction responses [2]. The elegance of this theoretical framework lies in its recognition that satisfaction is not simply a function of absolute performance levels but rather a relative judgment that depends critically on the gap between what was expected and what was actually received.

The theoretical foundations of this research area were established through the pioneering work of Richard L. Oliver, who developed Expectation Confirmation Theory in a series of influential papers published in 1977 and 1980 [3]. Since its initial formulation, this theoretical framework has been adopted and extended across numerous scientific fields, including consumer research, information systems, psychology, and marketing, demonstrating its remarkable versatility and explanatory power. The theory's enduring relevance stems from its ability to explain not only the formation of satisfaction judgments but also their downstream consequences for behavioral intentions such as repurchase decisions, continuance behavior, and word-of-mouth communication [1].

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the mechanism through which satisfaction evaluations are formed from the gap between planning expectations and actual experience. The analysis proceeds by examining the theoretical constructs underlying this process, tracing the cognitive steps through which expectations are formed and subsequently compared against experiential outcomes, and exploring the various factors that moderate the strength and direction of the relationship between disconfirmation and satisfaction.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of Expectation Confirmation Theory

### 2.1 Historical Development and Core Principles

Expectation Confirmation Theory emerged from the recognition that consumer satisfaction could not be adequately explained through simple performance-satisfaction relationships but required a more nuanced understanding of the comparative processes underlying satisfaction judgments. Oliver's foundational work established that satisfaction is fundamentally a cognitive phenomenon, arising from the mental comparison between pre-established expectations and post-experience performance perceptions [3]. This insight represented a significant advancement over earlier models that treated satisfaction as a direct function of objective performance, recognizing instead the subjective and comparative nature of satisfaction evaluations.

The theoretical framework posits that satisfaction formation follows a systematic causal flow involving four distinct stages [1]. The first stage involves the formation of initial expectations or beliefs about a product, service, or technology artifact prior to actual experience. These expectations are developed through exposure to various information sources, including marketing communications, word-of-mouth recommendations, prior experiences with similar offerings, and general knowledge about product categories. The second stage occurs after the individual has gained direct experience with the offering, during which they develop perceptions of its actual performance across relevant dimensions. The third stage involves a cognitive comparison between these initial expectations and perceived performance, resulting in a determination of confirmation or disconfirmation. The fourth and final stage involves the formation of satisfaction judgments, which are influenced by the direction and magnitude of the disconfirmation experienced.

The theoretical framework distinguishes between two primary variants that have emerged in the literature. The original Expectation Confirmation Theory emphasizes the role of confirmation—the degree to which perceived performance aligns with prior expectations—as the key mediating variable between expectations and satisfaction [2]. An alternative formulation, termed Expectation Disconfirmation Theory (EDT), replaces confirmation with disconfirmation as the central construct, focusing instead on the discrepancy between expected and actual performance [4]. Under this framework, disconfirmation can be positive (when performance exceeds expectations), neutral (when performance matches expectations), or negative (when performance falls short of expectations), with each type of disconfirmation producing distinct satisfaction outcomes.

### 2.2 The Four Core Constructs

The theoretical framework rests upon four interrelated constructs that together explain the formation of satisfaction judgments. Expectations represent the first and foundational construct, encompassing the beliefs or predictions that individuals hold about product or service performance prior to actual experience [2]. These expectations serve as reference points or anchors against which actual performance is subsequently evaluated. The formation of expectations can occur through multiple channels, including mass media advertising, opinion leaders, personal recommendations, and prior experience with similar products or brands [1]. Importantly, expectations can vary in their specificity and confidence, with more specific and confident expectations potentially exerting stronger influences on subsequent satisfaction judgments.

Perceived performance constitutes the second core construct, representing the individual's assessment of how well the product, service, or technology actually performed across relevant dimensions [2]. This construct is inherently subjective, reflecting the individual's interpretation and evaluation of their experiential outcomes rather than any objective measure of performance. Perceived performance is influenced not only by the actual characteristics of the offering but also by the individual's attention, interpretation, and evaluation processes, which can be affected by situational factors, individual differences, and contextual variables.

Disconfirmation, the third construct, emerges from the comparison between expectations and perceived performance [4]. When perceived performance exceeds expectations, positive disconfirmation occurs, generating satisfaction. When perceived performance falls short of expectations, negative disconfirmation occurs, generating dissatisfaction. When perceived performance matches expectations, simple confirmation occurs, which tends to produce moderate satisfaction. The magnitude of disconfirmation—the size of the gap between expectations and performance—also matters, with larger positive gaps producing greater satisfaction and larger negative gaps producing greater dissatisfaction.

Satisfaction represents the final construct in the framework, encompassing the individual's affective or cognitive response to the experienced product or service [2]. Satisfaction is posited to be directly influenced by disconfirmation and perceived performance through a mediational relationship. Positive disconfirmation strengthens attitudes toward the product and generates satisfaction, while negative disconfirmation produces unfavorable attitudes and dissatisfaction. Importantly, satisfaction serves as a key antecedent of subsequent behavioral intentions, including repurchase decisions, continuance intentions, and word-of-mouth communication.

## 3. The Cognitive Mechanism of Satisfaction Formation

### 3.1 The Comparative Evaluation Process

The mechanism through which satisfaction evaluations are formed from the gap between expectations and experience can be understood as a multi-stage cognitive process involving comparison, interpretation, and response generation. At its core, this mechanism relies on the fundamental human tendency to evaluate stimuli relative to reference points rather than in absolute terms [1]. This comparative approach to evaluation has deep roots in psychological theory, drawing upon concepts from adaptation level theory, cognitive dissonance theory, and contrast effects in social psychology.

The comparative process begins with the establishment of an expectation-based reference point. This reference point is formed prior to actual experience through the integration of information from various sources [1]. The individual synthesizes available information about the product or service, including marketing claims, peer recommendations, prior experiences, and general knowledge, to form a set of anticipations about likely performance outcomes. These anticipations become the yardstick against which actual performance will be measured. The formation of expectations is not a passive reception of information but an active cognitive process involving interpretation, integration, and prediction.

Once the expectation-based reference point is established, the individual proceeds through a period of exploration and experience with the product or service. During this phase, the individual develops perceptions of actual performance across multiple dimensions [1]. These perceptions are filtered through the individual's attention, interpretation, and evaluation processes, meaning that the same objective performance can give rise to different perceived performance across individuals. The perception of performance is influenced by factors including the salience of different performance dimensions, the individual's prior expectations, and situational factors affecting attention and interpretation.

The critical cognitive step in satisfaction formation occurs when the individual compares perceived performance against the pre-established expectation reference point. This comparison generates a disconfirmation judgment—the determination of whether, and to what degree, actual performance exceeded, matched, or fell short of expectations [4]. This comparison process can be represented mathematically as:

$$D = P - E$$

where $D$ represents disconfirmation, $P$ represents perceived performance, and $E$ represents expectations. When $D > 0$, positive disconfirmation occurs; when $D = 0$, confirmation occurs; and when $D < 0$, negative disconfirmation occurs.

### 3.2 The Role of Cognitive Adjustment

Following the disconfirmation judgment, individuals engage in a cognitive adjustment process that shapes their ultimate satisfaction response. This process, which has been termed "cognitive adjustment" in the theoretical literature, involves the integration of disconfirmation information with prior beliefs and attitudes to form a coherent satisfaction judgment [1]. The cognitive adjustment process serves important psychological functions, including the reduction of cognitive dissonance and the maintenance of cognitive consistency.

When positive disconfirmation occurs—meaning performance exceeded expectations—individuals experience a pleasant surprise that strengthens their favorable attitudes toward the product or service [1]. This positive disconfirmation validates the individual's decision to engage with the offering and provides positive reinforcement for future repurchase intentions. The cognitive adjustment process in this case involves incorporating the positive experience into the individual's overall evaluation, potentially raising their baseline expectations for future interactions while simultaneously generating current satisfaction.

When negative disconfirmation occurs—meaning performance fell short of expectations—individuals develop unfavorable attitudes that generate dissatisfaction [1]. This negative disconfirmation creates cognitive dissonance, particularly if the individual invested significant resources or made a firm commitment to the product or service. The cognitive adjustment process in this case may involve various strategies for managing the dissonance, including downward revision of expectations, reevaluation of the importance of the product, or rationalization of the negative experience.

The cognitive adjustment process is influenced by attribution patterns, where individuals assign responsibility for the disconfirmation to various causes [5]. When negative disconfirmation is attributed to controllable factors within the organization's purview, dissatisfaction tends to be more severe. When negative disconfirmation is attributed to uncontrollable external factors, such as adverse circumstances or situational constraints, dissatisfaction may be attenuated. This attribution process adds a layer of complexity to the basic disconfirmation-satisfaction relationship, explaining why similar levels of negative disconfirmation can produce different satisfaction outcomes depending on how the cause is interpreted.

### 3.3 The Mediational Structure

The relationship between expectations, performance, and satisfaction operates through a mediational structure that has important implications for understanding the satisfaction formation mechanism. Research has established that disconfirmation mediates the relationship between expectations and satisfaction, while also mediating (along with expectations) the relationship between perceived performance and satisfaction [2]. This mediational structure indicates that expectations and perceived performance do not directly cause satisfaction but rather influence satisfaction through their effect on disconfirmation.

The mediational relationship can be understood through the following causal chain: Expectations influence disconfirmation by establishing the reference point against which performance is evaluated; perceived performance influences disconfirmation by determining where actual performance falls relative to the expectation reference point; and disconfirmation influences satisfaction by generating positive or negative discrepancies that drive the satisfaction response [2]. This causal chain explains why the same level of perceived performance can produce different satisfaction outcomes depending on the level of expectations, and why the same level of expectations can produce different satisfaction outcomes depending on perceived performance.

The mediational structure also implies that the relationship between expectations and satisfaction is not straightforwardly positive or negative but rather depends on the relationship between expectations and perceived performance. When expectations are high but performance is even higher, positive disconfirmation occurs and satisfaction is high. When expectations are high but performance is lower, negative disconfirmation occurs and satisfaction is low. This complexity underscores the importance of considering the full mediational model rather than simple direct effects when attempting to understand and predict satisfaction outcomes.

## 4. Moderating Factors in the Expectation-Satisfaction Relationship

### 4.1 Contextual Moderators

The strength and direction of the relationship between expectations, disconfirmation, and satisfaction are not uniform across all contexts but are subject to significant moderating influences. Meta-analytic research has identified several contextual factors that moderate the expectation-satisfaction relationship, including study setting, study design, and study target [6]. These moderators help explain the variability in findings across different research contexts and provide guidance for understanding when and where the basic theoretical relationships will be strongest.

Study setting, specifically the distinction between field and laboratory contexts, has been found to moderate the expectation-satisfaction relationship [6]. Field studies, which examine satisfaction in naturalistic settings with actual consumer experiences, tend to show stronger expectation-satisfaction relationships than laboratory studies, which examine satisfaction under controlled conditions with potentially artificial experiences. This difference may reflect the greater ecological validity of field settings, where expectations are formed through realistic information channels and experiences involve genuine engagement with products or services.

Study design also moderates the expectation-satisfaction relationship, with cross-sectional surveys, longitudinal surveys, and experiments showing different patterns of results [6]. Cross-sectional designs, which measure expectations and satisfaction at a single point in time, may be subject to recall biases and may not capture the temporal dynamics of the satisfaction formation process. Longitudinal designs, which measure expectations before experience and satisfaction after experience, provide stronger evidence for the causal relationships posited by the theory. Experimental designs, which manipulate expectations through controlled information provision, offer the strongest causal evidence but may lack external validity.

The distinction between goods and services as the target of evaluation represents another important moderator [6]. Goods, which are tangible products with relatively observable and verifiable performance characteristics, may give rise to different expectation-satisfaction dynamics than services, which are intangible experiences with more subjective and variable performance characteristics. The heterogeneity of service delivery means that expectations may be more uncertain and performance perceptions more variable for services than for goods, potentially affecting the strength and consistency of the expectation-satisfaction relationship.

### 4.2 Individual and Cultural Moderators

Beyond contextual factors, individual differences and cultural variables also moderate the expectation-satisfaction relationship. Research has demonstrated that cultural and individual differences determine how disconfirmation and satisfaction are expressed and experienced [7]. These differences reflect variations in how individuals form expectations, interpret performance, and respond to disconfirmation across different cultural and personal contexts.

Cultural values and norms shape the expectation formation process by influencing which information sources are trusted, which performance dimensions are emphasized, and what levels of performance are considered normal or expected [7]. In cultures with strong collectivist orientations, for example, expectations may be heavily influenced by social norms and peer opinions, while in cultures with individualist orientations, expectations may be more strongly influenced by personal preferences and individual assessments. These cultural variations in expectation formation subsequently affect the disconfirmation process and ultimate satisfaction judgments.

Social comparison processes also influence the expectation-satisfaction relationship, particularly for experiential domains where social reference points are salient [8]. Individuals compare their own experiences and outcomes against those of relevant others, and these social comparisons can influence both expectations and satisfaction. Upward social comparison—comparing oneself to those who are better off—can elevate expectations and potentially lead to greater dissatisfaction if one's own experience falls short of the comparison standard. Downward social comparison—comparing oneself to those who are worse off—can lower expectations or provide a benchmark that makes one's own experience seem more satisfactory.

### 4.3 Product and Service Category Moderators

The characteristics of the product or service category being evaluated also moderate the expectation-satisfaction relationship. Information systems products and services present particular challenges for traditional ECT frameworks because users typically enter these products with well-defined expectations, unlike the passive reception of marketing that characterizes some consumer purchases [1]. The pre-purchase expectation formation process for information systems involves technical evaluation, needs assessment, and consideration of fit with existing systems and processes, resulting in expectations that are often more specific and confident than those formed through general marketing exposure.

The distinct characteristics of emerging business models, such as online knowledge products, create additional complexity for expectation-satisfaction dynamics [1]. These products often feature high customizability, perishability, and time sensitivity, which affect both the formation of expectations and the perception of performance. The intangible and experience-based nature of knowledge products may also make performance more difficult to evaluate objectively, increasing the role of subjective interpretation in the satisfaction formation process.

Service characteristics, including intangibility, heterogeneity, inseparability, and perishability, create unique challenges for expectation and satisfaction formation [7]. The heterogeneity of service delivery means that expectations may be more uncertain and performance more variable than for tangible goods. The inseparability of production and consumption means that expectations are formed and confirmed in real-time during the service encounter rather than in discrete pre- and post-purchase phases. These characteristics require adaptation of the basic ECT framework to account for the unique dynamics of service evaluation.

## 5. Implications for Satisfaction Management

### 5.1 Managing Expectations

Understanding the mechanism through which satisfaction is formed from the expectation-experience gap provides valuable guidance for organizations seeking to manage customer satisfaction. The central role of expectations in the satisfaction formation process suggests that expectation management represents a powerful lever for influencing satisfaction outcomes. However, expectation management must be approached carefully, as the relationship between expectations and satisfaction is not simply linear but depends on the interaction between expectations and actual performance.

Organizations can influence customer expectations through their marketing communications, including advertising, pricing, and product positioning [1]. By setting appropriate expectation levels, organizations can create conditions favorable to satisfaction. Setting expectations too low may limit the potential for positive disconfirmation, while setting expectations too high may create conditions where negative disconfirmation is likely if performance fails to meet the elevated standard. The optimal expectation level balances the desire to attract customers with realistic promises against the need to create opportunities for positive surprise and delight.

The timing and framing of expectation information also affects satisfaction outcomes. Expectations formed through gradual experience accumulation may be more resistant to disconfirmation than expectations formed through sudden information exposure. Similarly, expectations framed as typical or average performance standards may produce different disconfirmation dynamics than expectations framed as minimum guarantees or aspirational targets. Organizations must consider not only the level of expectations they create but also the manner in which those expectations are communicated and the sources from which they are derived.

### 5.2 Delivering Superior Performance

While expectation management provides one avenue for influencing satisfaction, the most direct path to customer satisfaction lies in delivering superior actual performance. The mediational structure of the ECT framework indicates that perceived performance influences satisfaction through its effect on disconfirmation, meaning that performance improvements translate into satisfaction improvements to the extent that they exceed customer expectations [2]. This insight suggests that organizations should focus not merely on absolute performance levels but on performance relative to customer expectations.

Performance delivery must be consistent and reliable to generate sustained satisfaction. The heterogeneity inherent in many service contexts means that performance may vary across encounters, customers, and time periods [7]. This variability creates uncertainty in the disconfirmation process, as customers may not know what level of performance to expect from one encounter to the next. Organizations can enhance satisfaction by reducing performance variability and increasing the reliability of their offerings, thereby creating more predictable disconfirmation dynamics.

The dimensions of performance that matter most to satisfaction may vary across customer segments and product categories. Organizations must identify which performance attributes are most salient to their customers and focus resources on delivering excellence in those dimensions [7]. Performance improvements in attributes that customers do not value highly may have limited impact on satisfaction, while deficiencies in highly valued attributes may generate severe dissatisfaction even if overall performance is adequate. Understanding customer value structures is therefore essential for effective performance management.

### 5.3 Creating Positive Disconfirmation

The concept of positive disconfirmation—performance that exceeds expectations—represents the sweet spot for satisfaction generation. Creating positive disconfirmation requires either exceeding high expectations or delivering performance that substantially exceeds moderate expectations. Both approaches have advantages and challenges that organizations must navigate.

Exceeding high expectations is challenging because it requires delivering performance at levels significantly above what customers already anticipate. This approach may require substantial investment in capabilities, resources, or processes and may be difficult to sustain across all customer encounters. However, when successful, exceeding high expectations generates strong positive disconfirmation and can create highly satisfied customers who become enthusiastic advocates for the organization.

Delivering performance that substantially exceeds moderate expectations may be easier to achieve but requires careful expectation management to ensure that expectations are not set too high. This approach involves creating positive surprise by delivering more than customers anticipate, which can generate strong satisfaction even when absolute performance levels are moderate. The key is calibrating expectations downward relative to actual capability, creating headroom for positive disconfirmation.

## 6. Extensions and Alternative Theoretical Perspectives

### 6.1 Integration with Other Theoretical Frameworks

The basic ECT framework has been extended and enriched through integration with other theoretical perspectives that address limitations of the original model. One notable extension involves the integration of Self-Determination Theory with ECT to explain continuance intention in technology contexts [1]. This integrative perspective recognizes that satisfaction is not the only determinant of continued engagement but is complemented by psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The combined framework provides a more complete explanation of post-adoption behavior than either theory alone.

Attribution theory has been incorporated into extended models of satisfaction to explain how the causes of performance outcomes influence satisfaction beyond the simple disconfirmation effect [5]. When performance falls short of expectations, customers engage in attribution processes to determine why the shortfall occurred. Attributions to stable, internal, and controllable causes tend to produce greater dissatisfaction than attributions to unstable, external, or uncontrollable causes. This attribution process adds a cognitive layer to the satisfaction formation mechanism that helps explain variability in satisfaction responses to similar disconfirmation outcomes.

Social comparison perspectives have been integrated with ECT to explain how reference group information influences expectation formation and satisfaction judgment [8]. Rather than evaluating performance against internally generated expectations, customers may evaluate performance against the experiences of others, creating social comparison dynamics that operate alongside or even override the basic disconfirmation process. This integration is particularly relevant for experiential products and services where social comparison information is readily available and salient.

### 6.2 Alternative Models of Expectation Effects

Research has identified alternative models of how expectations influence satisfaction beyond the basic disconfirmation mechanism. The assimilation effect model suggests that expectations directly influence satisfaction judgments through a cognitive process that brings perceived performance into alignment with prior expectations [6]. Under this model, expectations serve as perceptual anchors that bias the interpretation of performance information, such that performance that would otherwise be perceived as neutral is interpreted as positive when expectations are high and negative when expectations are low.

The asymmetry effect model suggests that positive and negative disconfirmation have asymmetric effects on satisfaction, with negative disconfirmation having stronger effects than positive disconfirmation of equivalent magnitude [6]. This asymmetry, sometimes termed "negativity bias," reflects the greater psychological impact of negative events compared to positive events of similar objective magnitude. Understanding this asymmetry has important implications for satisfaction management, suggesting that organizations may benefit more from avoiding negative disconfirmation than from generating equivalent positive disconfirmation.

The contrast effect model suggests that expectations can create psychological contrast that amplifies the perceived magnitude of disconfirmation [6]. Under this model, high expectations create a baseline against which performance appears worse than it would appear without the expectation, while low expectations create a baseline against which performance appears better than it would appear without the expectation. This contrast effect operates in addition to any direct disconfirmation effect, potentially amplifying the relationship between expectations and satisfaction.

## 7. Conclusion

The mechanism through which satisfaction evaluations are formed from the gap between planning expectations and actual experience represents a fundamental cognitive process with far-reaching implications for understanding human decision-making and behavior. Expectation Confirmation Theory and its extensions provide a robust theoretical framework for understanding this mechanism, identifying the key constructs of expectations, perceived performance, disconfirmation, and satisfaction, and tracing the causal relationships among them.

The satisfaction formation process operates through a systematic cognitive mechanism in which individuals compare their actual experiences against pre-established expectation reference points. This comparison generates disconfirmation judgments—positive when performance exceeds expectations, negative when performance falls short—that subsequently drive satisfaction responses. The magnitude and direction of disconfirmation, combined with the cognitive adjustment processes through which individuals interpret and integrate their experiences, determine the ultimate satisfaction judgment.

The strength and direction of the expectation-satisfaction relationship are subject to significant moderating influences from contextual, individual, cultural, and product-related factors. Understanding these moderators is essential for applying the theoretical framework effectively across diverse research and practical contexts. The variability in findings across different settings, designs, and product categories reflects the complexity of the satisfaction formation process and the importance of considering boundary conditions when generalizing from specific studies.

For organizations seeking to enhance customer satisfaction, the theoretical framework provides actionable guidance regarding expectation management, performance delivery, and the creation of positive disconfirmation. By carefully calibrating customer expectations, delivering reliable and superior performance, and creating opportunities for positive surprise, organizations can systematically influence the satisfaction formation process to generate favorable customer responses. The integration of ECT with complementary theoretical perspectives further enriches the toolkit available for understanding and managing satisfaction in complex organizational contexts.

The enduring relevance of Expectation Confirmation Theory reflects its foundation in fundamental principles of human cognition and its remarkable versatility across diverse domains of application. As organizations increasingly recognize the strategic importance of customer satisfaction for competitive advantage and long-term success, the theoretical insights provided by this framework become ever more valuable for guiding evidence-based management practice.
