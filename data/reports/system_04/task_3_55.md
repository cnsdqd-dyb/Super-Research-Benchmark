## 1. Introduction

The integration of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) factors into financial decision-making has transitioned from a niche investment strategy to a fundamental component of global capital markets. Central to this transition is the measurement and reporting of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, commonly referred to as carbon accounting. As climate change poses increasing physical and transition risks to corporate assets, investors require accurate, comparable, and timely data to allocate capital efficiently. However, the historic lack of standardized processes in carbon accounting has created a significant market failure characterized by information asymmetry.

Information asymmetry occurs when corporate managers possess superior information regarding the firm's climate risk exposure and carbon performance than external investors. This imbalance can lead to adverse selection, where capital is misallocated to high-carbon assets due to a lack of transparency, and moral hazard, where managers may engage in "greenwashing" to obscure poor environmental performance.

This report examines whether standardized processes of carbon accounting affect investment decision information symmetry. It synthesizes evidence regarding the evolution from voluntary, heterogeneous reporting frameworks to mandatory, standardized regimes such as the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) S2 and the European Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD). Furthermore, it analyzes the theoretical and practical implications of "transactional carbon accounting" (or E-liability accounting), a novel approach that applies the rigor of financial double-entry bookkeeping to carbon emissions.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: Information Asymmetry in Capital Markets

To understand the impact of carbon accounting on investment decisions, it is necessary to ground the analysis in financial and economic theory.

### 2.1 Agency Theory and the Lemon Problem
Agency theory posits that a conflict of interest exists between agents (managers) and principals (shareholders) due to information asymmetry. In the context of climate change, managers may have incentives to underreport carbon risks to maximize short-term stock prices or executive compensation. Without standardized accounting, investors cannot verify whether a firm’s claims of "low carbon" performance are genuine or merely symbolic [cite: 11, 12].

This dynamic creates a "market for lemons," as described by Akerlof. If investors cannot distinguish between truly low-carbon firms and high-carbon firms disguising themselves (greenwashing), they will discount the value of all firms in the sector. Standardized carbon accounting serves as a mechanism to verify quality, allowing the market to accurately price assets and reducing the cost of capital for genuine low-carbon performers [cite: 13].

### 2.2 Signaling Theory
Signaling theory suggests that high-quality firms will voluntarily disclose information to distinguish themselves from lower-quality peers. However, in the realm of carbon accounting, voluntary disclosure has proven insufficient due to the lack of a common language. When firms use different boundaries, estimation methods, and emission factors, even high-quality disclosures become difficult to compare. Standardization transforms carbon data from a noisy signal into a credible metric, enhancing its utility for reducing information asymmetry [cite: 8, 14].

### 2.3 The Role of Materiality
The concept of materiality is central to information symmetry. Information is material if its omission or misstatement could influence the economic decisions of users.
*   **Financial Materiality:** Focuses on how climate issues impact the company's value (Outside-In). This is the focus of the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) and IFRS S2 [cite: 15].
*   **Double Materiality:** Considers both the impact of climate on the firm and the firm's impact on the climate (Inside-Out). This is the framework adopted by the EU's CSRD [cite: 6].
Standardization clarifies which definition of materiality is being applied, thereby aligning investor expectations with corporate reporting.

## 3. The Current State of Carbon Accounting: The Estimation Problem

The prevailing framework for carbon accounting is the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol. While it has provided a necessary starting point, its flexibility and reliance on estimates have limited its effectiveness in eliminating information asymmetry.

### 3.1 The GHG Protocol and Scope 3 Limitations
The GHG Protocol categorizes emissions into three scopes:
*   **Scope 1:** Direct emissions from owned sources.
*   **Scope 2:** Indirect emissions from the generation of purchased energy.
*   **Scope 3:** All other indirect emissions in the value chain.

Research indicates that Scope 3 emissions often account for the vast majority (over 70%) of a company's total footprint [cite: 16]. However, the current accounting standards for Scope 3 allow for the use of secondary data (industry averages) and spend-based estimates rather than primary, supplier-specific data [cite: 17, 18].

### 3.2 Information Asymmetry Implications of Estimation
The reliance on estimates creates a "brittle" system where data cannot be reliably compared across firms [cite: 19, 20].
*   **Lack of Comparability:** Two firms with identical supply chains may report vastly different Scope 3 numbers depending on the estimation databases they use.
*   **Inability to Reward Decarbonization:** If a firm purchases low-carbon steel, but accounts for it using a global industry average emission factor, the accounting system fails to capture the reduction. This prevents investors from identifying and rewarding efficiency, maintaining high levels of information asymmetry [cite: 1, 21].
*   **Greenwashing Risk:** The flexibility in boundary setting and methodology allows firms to cherry-pick favorable estimation methods, obscuring true risk exposure from investors [cite: 22].

## 4. The Transactional Turn: E-Liabilities and Green Ledgers

To address the deficiencies of estimation-based accounting, scholars and practitioners are advocating for a shift toward "transactional carbon accounting," often referred to as the E-liability method or Green Ledger. This approach is posited to have the most profound impact on reducing information asymmetry.

### 4.1 The E-Liability Framework
Proposed by Kaplan and Ramanna, the E-liability method applies the principles of financial cost accounting to carbon. In this system, carbon is treated as a liability that is attached to a product and transferred down the supply chain [cite: 1, 23].
*   **Mechanism:** When a supplier sells a product, they transfer the physical good and a "carbon invoice" (the E-liability) representing the actual emissions generated to produce it. The buyer records this as an incoming carbon liability [cite: 24, 25].
*   **Elimination of Scope 3 Estimates:** By passing actual data from supplier to buyer, the system eliminates the need for the buyer to estimate upstream Scope 3 emissions. The buyer's upstream emissions are simply the sum of the E-liabilities transferred to them [cite: 4, 26].

### 4.2 Impact on Investment Decision Utility
Transactional carbon accounting fundamentally alters the information environment for investors:
1.  **Granularity:** It provides data at the product and batch level, allowing investors to see exactly which product lines are carbon-intensive and which are efficient [cite: 3, 27].
2.  **Auditability:** Because the system uses double-entry bookkeeping (debits and credits of carbon), it can be audited with the same rigor as financial statements. This moves carbon data from "marketing material" to "audited financial data," significantly reducing the trust gap [cite: 1, 28].
3.  **Connectivity:** It links financial and non-financial information within the Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) system (e.g., SAP Green Ledger). This allows investors to analyze the "carbon return on investment" and other integrated metrics [cite: 29, 30].

### 4.3 Critique and Challenges
While promising, the E-liability approach faces challenges. Critics argue it is "brittle" because it requires every link in the supply chain to participate; if one supplier fails to report, the chain breaks [cite: 19]. However, proponents argue that in such cases, conservative default values can be used to penalize non-reporting, incentivizing compliance [cite: 23]. Additionally, some argue that focusing solely on cradle-to-gate transfers may obscure downstream (use-phase) emissions, which are critical for sectors like fossil fuels [cite: 26].

## 5. Regulatory Standardization: IFRS S2 and CSRD

The shift from voluntary to mandatory standardization is a primary driver in reducing information asymmetry. Two major regimes are currently reshaping the landscape.

### 5.1 IFRS S2: The Global Baseline for Investors
The International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) issued IFRS S2 to create a global baseline for climate disclosure focused on the needs of investors [cite: 5, 15].
*   **Focus:** Financial materiality. It requires disclosure of climate risks that affect cash flows, access to finance, and cost of capital [cite: 31].
*   **Standardization Effect:** By aligning with the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) and requiring specific industry-based metrics (SASB), IFRS S2 reduces the cost of analyzing diverse reports. It mandates the disclosure of Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions, forcing companies to standardize their reporting methodologies [cite: 32, 33].
*   **Investor Impact:** Research suggests that IFRS S2 adoption will improve stock liquidity and lower the cost of capital by providing investors with comparable data on transition risks [cite: 15, 34].

### 5.2 CSRD: The Double Materiality Approach
The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) goes further by mandating reporting on both financial impact and societal impact [cite: 35, 36].
*   **Standardization:** The European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) provide rigid templates and data points (over 1,000) that companies must report. This eliminates the "cherry-picking" common in voluntary reporting [cite: 6].
*   **Information Symmetry:** By forcing the disclosure of detailed strategy, targets, and double materiality assessments, CSRD reduces the ability of management to hide negative externalities. It makes "greenwashing" significantly harder because vague claims must be substantiated with standardized data points [cite: 6, 37].
*   **Interoperability:** Efforts are being made to ensure IFRS S2 and CSRD are interoperable to prevent market fragmentation, which would re-introduce information asymmetry for global investors [cite: 32].

## 6. Empirical Evidence: Does Standardization Affect Capital Markets?

Academic research provides empirical support for the hypothesis that standardized carbon accounting improves market efficiency.

### 6.1 Impact on Cost of Capital
Multiple studies have found a correlation between carbon disclosure quality and the cost of capital.
*   **Cost of Equity:** Firms with high carbon intensity generally face a higher cost of equity, reflecting a risk premium demanded by investors. However, high-quality, standardized disclosure mitigates this penalty by reducing uncertainty [cite: 7, 38].
*   **Cost of Debt:** Research indicates a U-shaped relationship where initial disclosure may raise costs (as risks are revealed), but high-quality, sustained disclosure eventually lowers the cost of debt as lenders gain confidence in the firm's risk management [cite: 39].
*   **Idiosyncratic Risk:** Standardized disclosure reduces idiosyncratic risk (firm-specific risk) by clarifying the firm's exposure to carbon pricing and regulation. This allows investors to diversify more effectively [cite: 40].

### 6.2 Impact on Stock Price Crash Risk
Information asymmetry is a key driver of stock price crash risk (the likelihood of a sudden, sharp decline). Managers may hoard bad news about carbon risks until it reaches a tipping point. Standardized disclosure forces the timely release of this information, smoothing out price adjustments and reducing crash risk [cite: 35, 41].

### 6.3 The Role of Textual vs. Numerical Disclosure
Research distinguishes between numerical data (emissions tons) and textual disclosure (strategy). While numerical standardization is crucial, textual disclosures that explain the *context* of the numbers (e.g., how emission allowances are accounted for) are equally important for reducing asymmetry. The lack of standardized accounting for emission allowances (assets vs. expenses) remains a source of confusion for investors [cite: 2, 42].

## 7. The Role of Assurance in Establishing Trust

Standardization of *reporting* is insufficient without standardization of *verification*.

### 7.1 Limited vs. Reasonable Assurance
Currently, most carbon assurance is "limited" (negative assurance: "nothing came to our attention"). This is significantly less rigorous than the "reasonable" assurance (positive opinion) provided for financial statements [cite: 9, 43].
*   **Information Asymmetry:** Limited assurance leaves a residual gap in trust. Investors cannot be certain that the data is free from material misstatement.
*   **Regulatory Trajectory:** The CSRD mandates a path from limited to reasonable assurance over time [cite: 44]. This transition is expected to be the final step in aligning carbon data reliability with financial data reliability.

### 7.2 The Auditability of Transactional Data
Transactional carbon accounting (E-liability) is inherently more auditable than estimation-based accounting. Because it relies on transactional records (invoices, goods receipts) rather than spreadsheets of estimates, auditors can trace the "carbon trail" just as they trace the money trail. This capability is essential for moving to reasonable assurance [cite: 3, 28].

## 8. Conclusion

The evidence overwhelmingly supports the conclusion that standardized processes of carbon accounting are essential for reducing investment decision information asymmetry. The current landscape, dominated by estimation-based methods and voluntary frameworks, has failed to provide the comparable, reliable data investors need, resulting in mispricing of risk and capital misallocation.

The transition toward standardized, mandatory frameworks (IFRS S2, CSRD) and the potential adoption of transactional accounting (E-liability) represents a paradigm shift. By treating carbon emissions with the same rigor as financial transactions—through double-entry bookkeeping, standardized materiality definitions, and mandatory assurance—these processes transform carbon data from a qualitative signal into a quantitative, decision-useful metric.

While challenges remain—specifically regarding the cost of implementation and the need for supply chain-wide adoption—the trajectory is clear. As carbon accounting standardizes, the information gap between managers and investors narrows, enabling more efficient markets and facilitating the massive capital reallocation required for the low-carbon transition.

## 9. Detailed Analysis of Key Standardization Initiatives

### 9.1 The Greenhouse Gas Protocol (GHG Protocol)
*   **Status:** The de facto global standard, used by the majority of reporting companies.
*   **Strengths:** Established a common language (Scopes 1, 2, 3); flexible adoption encouraged early uptake.
*   **Weaknesses regarding Asymmetry:**
    *   **Flexibility:** Allows companies to choose between operational control, financial control, or equity share approaches for consolidation. This means two identical companies can report different numbers, confusing investors [cite: 1, 19].
    *   **Scope 3 Estimates:** Allows the use of "spend-based" methods (multiplying dollars spent by an industry average emission factor). This fails to capture specific supplier performance, meaning a company buying "green" steel looks the same as one buying "dirty" steel if the spend is the same [cite: 17, 18].
    *   **Lack of Downstream Granularity:** Often relies on lifetime usage estimates that are highly subjective (e.g., an auto manufacturer estimating the lifetime mileage of cars sold) [cite: 45].

### 9.2 IFRS S2 (International Sustainability Standards Board)
*   **Objective:** To provide a global baseline of sustainability disclosures specifically for capital markets.
*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Alignment:** Fully integrates TCFD recommendations (Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, Metrics & Targets) [cite: 31, 46].
    *   **Industry Specificity:** Incorporates SASB standards to ensure metrics are relevant to the specific sector (e.g., cement vs. software) [cite: 31].
    *   **Scope 3 Mandate:** Requires Scope 3 disclosure, removing the option to hide value chain risks [cite: 33].
*   **Impact on Asymmetry:** High. By focusing on *financial* materiality, it speaks directly to the investor's need to assess cash flow risks. It reduces the noise of "social impact" data that may not be financially relevant, sharpening the signal for asset pricing [cite: 15].

### 9.3 Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)
*   **Jurisdiction:** European Union (but with extraterritorial reach to non-EU companies with significant EU operations).
*   **Key Features:**
    *   **Double Materiality:** Companies must report on how sustainability issues impact them (financial) AND how they impact the world (impact) [cite: 6].
    *   **ESRS Standards:** Highly prescriptive standards (ESRS E1 for Climate) requiring hundreds of data points.
    *   **Mandatory Assurance:** Requires limited assurance initially, moving to reasonable assurance [cite: 36].
*   **Impact on Asymmetry:** Very High. The sheer depth of data required makes it difficult to hide poor performance. The mandatory digital tagging (XBRL) will allow investors to machine-read and compare data instantly across thousands of firms [cite: 37].

### 9.4 E-Liability / Transactional Carbon Accounting
*   **Concept:** A management accounting innovation rather than just a reporting standard. It proposes eliminating Scope 3 estimates entirely.
*   **Mechanism:**
    *   **E-Asset:** Carbon embodied in inventory.
    *   **E-Liability:** The obligation to account for emissions.
    *   **Transfer:** When goods move, the E-liability moves.
*   **Impact on Asymmetry:** Transformative. It solves the "allocation problem" (how to allocate factory emissions to specific products) using cost accounting logic. This allows investors to see the carbon intensity of *specific products* and *specific supply chains*, enabling highly granular capital allocation [cite: 1, 3, 4].

## 11. Implications for Corporate Governance and Strategy

Standardized carbon accounting does not only affect external investors; it fundamentally changes internal corporate governance, which in turn affects the information released to the market.

### 11.1 Integrated Thinking
The integration of carbon data into the general ledger (as proposed by transactional accounting and enabled by ERP systems like SAP) forces "integrated thinking." CFOs and sustainability officers must work with the same data set. This internal alignment reduces the risk that the "sustainability report" tells a different story than the "financial report," a common source of information asymmetry [cite: 3, 47].

### 11.2 Executive Compensation
Standardized metrics allow for the inclusion of carbon targets in executive compensation packages with legal rigor. When carbon data is audited and standardized, it becomes a reliable basis for contract enforcement, aligning manager interests with shareholder interests regarding climate risk [cite: 1].

### 11.3 Supply Chain Discipline
Standardized accounting forces firms to engage deeply with their supply chains. Under an E-liability system, a firm cannot simply estimate its supplier's emissions; it must demand an accounting statement. This creates a cascade of transparency down the value chain, illuminating risks that were previously hidden in the "Scope 3 black box" [cite: 23, 25].

## 12. Conclusion: The Path Forward

The question "Do standardized processes of carbon accounting affect investment decision information symmetry?" can be answered with a definitive **yes**, but with the caveat that the *degree* of impact depends on the *type* of standardization.

Standardization of *disclosure formats* (like TCFD) helps, but standardization of *measurement methodologies* (like E-liability) is required to fully resolve the market failure. The current transition from voluntary estimates to mandatory, audited, and transactional accounting represents the maturation of carbon markets.

For investors, this evolution promises a future where carbon risk is priced with the same efficiency as interest rate risk or credit risk. For the broader economy, it implies that capital will increasingly flow away from opaque, high-carbon assets toward transparent, carbon-efficient ones, driven not by altruism but by the rational processing of superior information.
