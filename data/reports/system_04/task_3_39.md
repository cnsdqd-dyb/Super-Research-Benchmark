## 1. Introduction

The efficient allocation of transport capacity—whether road space, transit frequency, or energy infrastructure—relies fundamentally on understanding the demand patterns of users. These patterns are encapsulated in Origin-Destination (OD) matrices, which describe the volume of trips between zonal pairs. However, OD matrices are not static; they exhibit complex **spatiotemporal distributions** that create fluctuating **density gradients** across a network.

The interaction between these gradients and network capacity is non-linear. Classical traffic theory often assumes homogeneous conditions, but recent advancements in the **Macroscopic Fundamental Diagram (MFD)** theory have demonstrated that spatial inhomogeneity—the variance of density across network links—is a primary driver of capacity loss [cite: 1, 12]. When OD flows concentrate in specific corridors, they create steep density gradients (shockwaves or jam fronts) that degrade the aggregate performance of the network [cite: 13].

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of how the spatiotemporal evolution of OD matrices influences capacity allocation. It synthesizes research from traffic flow theory, electric vehicle (EV) infrastructure planning, and urban dynamics to demonstrate that optimal capacity allocation requires minimizing spatial density variance and dynamically matching supply to the shifting centers of demand.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: The Generalized Macroscopic Fundamental Diagram (GMFD)

To understand how OD matrices affect capacity, one must first understand the relationship between density and flow at the network level. The Macroscopic Fundamental Diagram (MFD) provides this link, but its stability is compromised by uneven density distributions derived from complex OD patterns.

### 2.1 The Impact of Spatial Inhomogeneity on Capacity
The standard MFD relates the average network density ($k$) to the average network flow ($q$). Ideally, flow increases with density up to a critical point ($k_c$), after which it declines into congestion. However, research shows that for the same average density, network flow can vary significantly depending on how that density is distributed [cite: 1].

*   **The GMFD Concept:** The Generalized MFD extends the 2D relationship to a 3D surface: $Flow = f(Density, Inhomogeneity)$. Inhomogeneity is often measured as the standard deviation of density across all links in the network [cite: 2].
*   **Capacity Reduction Mechanism:** High spatial inhomogeneity (large density gradients) increases the probability of spillover at intersections. When queues from high-density links block upstream junctions, they starve downstream links of flow. This phenomenon reduces the network's macroscopic capacity, causing the system to operate below its potential maximum flow [cite: 4, 12].
*   **Nucleation Effect:** Congestion rarely appears uniformly. It "nucleates" at bottlenecks defined by the OD matrix (e.g., high demand for a specific CBD zone). This nucleation creates steep density gradients that propagate outward. If capacity allocation (e.g., green light time) does not adapt to smooth these gradients, the network enters a state of reduced production [cite: 14].

### 2.2 Hysteresis Loops and Jam Fronts
The spatiotemporal evolution of OD demand creates distinct phases of loading (congestion onset) and unloading (recovery). This results in **hysteresis loops** in the MFD.

*   **Path-Dependence:** The network performance depends on its history. During the recovery phase, traffic is often more fragmented (higher inhomogeneity) than during the loading phase. Consequently, for the same average density, the flow is lower during recovery than during loading [cite: 2].
*   **Jam Fronts:** Moving density gradients, or "jam fronts," represent the boundaries between free-flow and congested traffic. Accurate modeling of these fronts is essential for capacity allocation. Macroscopic models that allow for arbitrary fundamental diagram shapes can track these gradients, enabling controllers to identify where capacity is effectively being lost [cite: 13].
*   **Implication for Allocation:** Capacity allocation strategies that assume a static MFD will fail during the recovery phase. Controllers must account for the "width" of the hysteresis loop, which is a proxy for the severity of the density gradient [cite: 4].

## 3. Spatiotemporal Dynamics of OD Matrices

The OD matrix is the root cause of the density gradients discussed above. Understanding its spatiotemporal characteristics is a prerequisite for optimizing capacity.

### 3.1 Dynamic OD Estimation
Traditional static OD matrices fail to capture the rapid fluctuations in demand that cause acute density gradients. Modern approaches utilize dynamic OD estimation techniques that integrate real-time data.

*   **Data Sources:** Dynamic matrices are derived from diverse streams, including loop detectors, floating car data (GPS), mobile network data, and smart card records [cite: 15, 16].
*   **Granularity Issues:** As spatial resolution increases (e.g., dividing a city into smaller zones), the OD matrix becomes sparser and more computationally challenging to predict. However, fine-grained matrices are necessary to detect local density gradients that might be smoothed out in coarser models [cite: 17].
*   **Bi-Level Optimization:** Estimating dynamic OD matrices often involves a bi-level problem. The upper level minimizes the error between observed and simulated traffic counts, while the lower level assigns traffic to the network (often using Dynamic User Equilibrium). This process ensures that the estimated OD flows are consistent with the observed propagation of density gradients [cite: 18, 19].

### 3.2 Spatiotemporal Prediction Models
Predicting future OD demand allows for proactive capacity allocation.
*   **Deep Learning Approaches:** Techniques like ConvLSTM (Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory) networks are used to predict spatiotemporal demand distributions. These models can capture the "flow" of demand across the city, predicting where density gradients will emerge hours in advance [cite: 20].
*   **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):** GNNs model the city as a graph, capturing the spatial dependencies between zones. They are particularly effective at predicting how demand in one region will propagate to create density gradients in connected regions [cite: 21, 22].

## 4. Capacity Allocation Strategies in Traffic Networks

Given that spatiotemporal OD variations create density gradients that reduce capacity, allocation strategies must aim to **homogenize** density or **match** supply to dynamic demand.

### 4.1 Perimeter Control and Gating
Perimeter control (or gating) is the most direct application of MFD theory to capacity allocation.
*   **Mechanism:** When the density in a protected region approaches the critical density ($k_c$), traffic signals at the perimeter are adjusted to limit the inflow. This prevents the region from entering the congested regime of the MFD [cite: 2, 23].
*   **Gradient-Based Control:** Advanced gating strategies do not just look at average density; they monitor density gradients. If a specific sub-region shows a rapidly rising density gradient (indicating nucleation of congestion), capacity is throttled specifically for flows heading toward that area. This prevents the inhomogeneity from spreading and degrading the entire network's capacity [cite: 12].

### 4.2 Network Partitioning
To manage density gradients effectively, large heterogeneous networks are often partitioned into smaller, homogeneous subnetworks.
*   **Clustering Algorithms:** Algorithms partition the network such that each subnetwork has a well-defined MFD with low scatter (low variance). This allows for independent capacity allocation policies for each cluster [cite: 5].
*   **Dynamic Partitioning:** Since OD patterns change over time (e.g., morning vs. evening commute), the optimal partition boundaries may shift. Dynamic partitioning algorithms adjust these boundaries in real-time to ensure that density gradients remain contained within controllable clusters [cite: 5].

### 4.3 Signal Optimization
Traffic signals represent the temporal allocation of capacity at intersections.
*   **Cooperative Signal Optimization:** In large-scale networks, signals must be optimized cooperatively to manage density gradients. Surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithms can optimize signal timings to smooth out density variations across the network, thereby maximizing the macroscopic flow [cite: 5].
*   **Impact of Cycle Length:** Poor signal coordination or inappropriate cycle lengths can exacerbate spatial inhomogeneity, effectively reducing the MFD capacity. Adaptive signal control systems use real-time OD data to adjust cycle lengths, minimizing the formation of queues that create sharp density gradients [cite: 14].

## 5. Application: Electric Vehicle (EV) Charging Infrastructure

The principles of spatiotemporal OD distribution and capacity allocation are critically applied in the planning of EV charging stations (EVCS). Here, "capacity" refers to electrical power and charging slots, and "density" refers to the accumulation of EVs with low State of Charge (SoC).

### 5.1 Spatiotemporal Charging Load Prediction
EV charging demand is not merely a function of vehicle location but of the **travel chain**—the sequence of trips defined by the OD matrix.
*   **Travel Chain Simulation:** Monte Carlo simulations based on OD matrices track the SoC of individual vehicles as they move through the network. This reveals the spatiotemporal distribution of charging demand [cite: 7, 8].
*   **Demand Hotspots:** The simulation reveals that charging density gradients shift throughout the day. Residential zones see high demand density at night (19:00–03:00), while commercial and office zones experience peaks during the day. Capacity allocation must mirror these shifts [cite: 24].

### 5.2 Joint Allocation of Charging and Grid Capacity
Allocating EVCS capacity is constrained by the power grid's "hosting capacity."
*   **Grid-Aware Optimization:** High-density charging demand often coincides with grid constraints. A "density gradient" in charging demand can cause voltage drops or transformer overloads if the local grid capacity is insufficient. Optimization models use bi-level programming to allocate charging stations where the grid can support the load, or where Distributed Generation (DG) can be co-located to buffer the demand [cite: 25, 26].
*   **Inaccessibility Rate:** If capacity is not allocated according to the spatiotemporal OD demand, the "inaccessibility rate" increases. This metric quantifies the proportion of vehicles that need charging but cannot access a station due to capacity constraints or distance. Minimizing this rate is a key objective in EVCS planning [cite: 27].

### 5.3 Dynamic Pricing and Navigation
To manage density gradients in charging demand, operators use soft capacity allocation techniques.
*   **Price-Based Demand Response:** Dynamic pricing can smooth spatiotemporal density gradients by incentivizing users to charge in low-density areas or off-peak times. This effectively reallocates the "capacity" of the grid by shifting the demand curve [cite: 7].
*   **Intelligent Navigation:** Systems that route EVs to stations with available capacity (based on real-time CPFE - Charging Power for Future EVs) help homogenize the distribution of charging load, preventing the formation of extreme density gradients at popular stations [cite: 7].

## 6. Application: Public Transport and Ride-Sharing

In public transport and ride-sharing, capacity allocation involves positioning vehicles (supply) to match passenger OD demand.

### 6.1 Ride-Sharing and Dynamic Fleet Management
Ride-sharing platforms deal with highly dynamic OD matrices.
*   **Matching Density to Capacity:** The efficiency of ride-pooling (sharing rides) is non-linearly related to demand density. High demand density allows for higher capacity utilization (more passengers per vehicle) without significant detours. In low-density areas, the "friction" of detours reduces the effective capacity of the fleet [cite: 28, 29].
*   **Anytime Optimal Assignment:** Algorithms for high-capacity ride-sharing (e.g., assigning requests to a fleet of vans) must solve complex optimization problems in real-time. These algorithms dynamically allocate vehicle capacity to OD pairs to minimize wait times and total distance [cite: 30].
*   **Vacant Vehicle Re-allocation:** Accurate prediction of OD demand allows platforms to re-allocate empty vehicles to areas where density gradients (surges in demand) are expected to form, thereby proactively matching supply to demand [cite: 31].

### 6.2 Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) and Urban Density
Long-term capacity allocation involves land-use planning.
*   **Density-Capacity Matching:** Urban planning principles dictate that development density should match transit capacity. Areas within 600-1000m of high-capacity transit stations (Metro/BRT) should have the highest population and job densities. This alignment ensures that the high OD demand generated by density is absorbed by high-capacity modes, preventing road congestion [cite: 32].
*   **Modal Mismatch:** A "modal mismatch" occurs when high OD density exists in areas with low transit capacity. This forces users into private cars, creating severe traffic density gradients. GIS-based analysis identifies these mismatch zones to guide future infrastructure investment [cite: 11].

## 7. Mathematical Modeling of Density and Capacity

The relationship between OD distribution and capacity is formalized through various mathematical models.

### 7.1 Flow Network Algorithms
*   **Max-Flow Min-Cut:** In graph theory, the maximum flow between an origin and destination is limited by the "min-cut" capacity of the edges. In traffic networks, the "capacity" of an edge is dynamic and depends on the density of the target link (due to spillback). Algorithms like Ford-Fulkerson must be adapted to account for these dynamic, density-dependent capacities (residual graphs) [cite: 33, 34].
*   **Advection Dynamics:** Traffic flow can be modeled using advection equations where density propagates through the network. These models capture how OD demand injects mass (vehicles) into the system and how density gradients (shockwaves) travel, interacting with bottlenecks [cite: 35].

### 7.2 Optimization Techniques
*   **Bi-Level Programming:** Used extensively in both OD estimation and capacity allocation. The upper level optimizes a strategic objective (e.g., minimizing total travel time or installation cost), while the lower level models user behavior (User Equilibrium) [cite: 19, 20].
*   **Heuristics:** Genetic Algorithms (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Ant Colony Optimization are employed to solve the NP-hard problems of allocating capacity (e.g., signal timings, bus routes, charging stations) under complex spatiotemporal constraints [cite: 24, 36].

## 8. Conclusion

The spatiotemporal distribution of OD matrices is the primary determinant of how density gradients form and evolve within a network. Research consistently demonstrates that **spatial inhomogeneity**—the uneven distribution of density—is detrimental to network capacity. Whether in traffic flow, EV charging, or public transit, the presence of steep density gradients leads to resource underutilization, hysteresis effects, and service degradation.

Effective capacity allocation therefore requires a two-pronged approach:
1.  **Dynamic Supply Matching:** Using real-time OD estimation and prediction to shift capacity (signal time, power, vehicles) to emerging density hotspots.
2.  **Demand Homogenization:** Implementing control strategies (gating, pricing, navigation) that smooth out density gradients, maintaining the network near its critical density where macroscopic flow is maximized.

Future systems will likely integrate these domains, using the GMFD to manage not just traffic flow, but the coupled dynamics of transportation and energy networks, ensuring that capacity allocation is robust to the inevitable fluctuations of urban life.
