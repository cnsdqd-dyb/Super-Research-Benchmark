# Data Quality and Attribution Uncertainty in Climate Agreement Verification via Remote Sensing

### Key Points
*   **Verification Gap:** Current national inventories (bottom-up) often diverge from atmospheric observations (top-down). Remote sensing offers an independent verification mechanism for the Paris Agreement’s Global Stocktake, but faces significant challenges in distinguishing anthropogenic emissions from natural carbon cycles.
*   **Attribution Strategy:** Because CO2 has a long atmospheric lifetime and high background concentration, attribution relies heavily on "co-emitters" like Nitrogen Dioxide (NO2). NO2 serves as a high-resolution proxy for fossil fuel combustion plumes, allowing satellites to pinpoint industrial sources and urban centers with greater precision than CO2 sensors alone.
*   **Uncertainty vs. Quality:** Academic consensus emphasizes distinguishing between "data quality" (compliance with retrieval assumptions) and "uncertainty" (statistical confidence). High-uncertainty data is not necessarily low-quality; it simply requires rigorous quantification (e.g., per-pixel error estimates) to be useful in inverse modeling.
*   **Technological Evolution:** The transition from scientific missions (OCO-2, TROPOMI) to operational monitoring constellations (Copernicus CO2M) represents a paradigm shift. These future systems integrate multiple imagers (CO2, NO2, aerosols) to reduce attribution errors and support the Enhanced Transparency Framework.

### Executive Summary
The verification of international climate agreements, specifically the Paris Agreement and its Global Stocktake (GST), requires a transition from reliance solely on self-reported national inventories to an integrated system involving independent atmospheric monitoring. Remote sensing satellites provide the necessary global coverage to track Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions. However, the utility of this data is contingent upon rigorous handling of **data quality** and **attribution uncertainty**.

Data quality in this context is not merely a binary flag of validity but a complex characterization of systematic and stochastic errors, requiring international standardization through bodies like the Global Space-based Inter-Calibration System (GSICS). Attribution uncertainty—the difficulty of assigning observed atmospheric GHG enhancements to specific anthropogenic sources versus natural biogenic fluxes—remains the primary scientific hurdle. Advanced methodologies, including the use of short-lived co-emitted gases (NO2) as proxies, machine learning for plume detection, and inverse modeling with detailed priors, are being deployed to disentangle these signals. This report details the mechanisms by which the scientific community quantifies uncertainty and attributes emissions to verify climate action.

## 1. Introduction: The Role of Remote Sensing in Climate Governance

The Paris Agreement established a framework for limiting global warming to well below 2°C, relying on Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and a periodic Global Stocktake (GST) to assess collective progress [cite: 1, 2]. Historically, emissions reporting has been "bottom-up," based on statistical activity data (e.g., fuel consumption) and emission factors [cite: 3, 4]. While transparent, this method is prone to latency, inconsistencies, and large uncertainties, particularly in developing nations or for sectors like agriculture and waste [cite: 5, 6].

Remote sensing offers a "top-down" approach, measuring atmospheric concentrations of GHGs (CO2, CH4) to infer surface fluxes [cite: 7, 8]. This independent verification capacity is critical for the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) of the Paris Agreement [cite: 9, 10]. However, satellites measure the total column-averaged dry air mole fraction (XCO2), which integrates all sources and sinks—anthropogenic and natural—along the optical path. Consequently, the central challenge in using remote sensing for verification is **attribution**: isolating the specific contribution of human activities from the noisy background of the natural carbon cycle, while rigorously quantifying the **uncertainty** of these measurements to ensure legal and scientific robustness [cite: 11, 12].

## 2. Handling Data Quality in Satellite Climate Data Records

To support policy decisions, satellite observations must be processed into Climate Data Records (CDRs) that are stable, consistent, and accompanied by rigorous quality information. The academic and operational communities distinguish sharply between data quality indicators and uncertainty estimates.

### 2.1 Distinction Between Uncertainty, Error, and Quality
Research emphasizes that uncertainty, error, and quality are distinct concepts that must not be conflated in CDRs.
*   **Uncertainty** characterizes the dispersion of values that could reasonably be attributed to the measurand. It is a statistical property.
*   **Error** is the difference between the measured value and the "true" value.
*   **Quality Flags** should indicate whether the retrieval assumptions were met, rather than duplicating uncertainty information [cite: 13].

A critical finding in best-practice guidelines is that high-uncertainty data should not automatically be flagged as "bad" quality. If a measurement has high uncertainty (e.g., due to low solar angle or surface albedo) but that uncertainty is accurately quantified, the data remains valid for assimilation into climate models [cite: 14, 15]. Quality flags are reserved for conditions where the retrieval algorithm fails or assumptions are violated (e.g., undetected cloud contamination), rendering the uncertainty estimate itself unreliable [cite: 15].

### 2.2 Calibration and Inter-Calibration (GSICS)
Data quality assurance begins with sensor calibration. The **Global Space-based Inter-Calibration System (GSICS)**, coordinated by WMO and agencies like EUMETSAT, provides the international standard for ensuring consistency among diverse satellite instruments [cite: 16, 17, 18].
*   **Methodology:** GSICS develops common algorithms to compare operational satellites (e.g., geostationary weather satellites) against high-accuracy reference instruments (e.g., hyperspectral sounders like IASI or CrIS) [cite: 19].
*   **Operational Output:** These comparisons generate calibration corrections that harmonize data records across different satellites and times, which is essential for detecting long-term climate trends that are often smaller than inter-sensor biases [cite: 17, 20].
*   **Traceability:** By tying measurements to absolute SI-traceable references, GSICS ensures that the "top-down" data used for verification is robust against sensor drift over decades [cite: 17].

### 2.3 Validation against Ground Networks
Satellite retrievals are validated against ground-based spectrometers, such as the Total Carbon Column Observing Network (TCCON). This validation provides the "truth" metric for bias correction. However, the sparsity of ground networks introduces its own uncertainty, particularly in regions with few stations, necessitating the use of atmospheric transport models to bridge the gap between satellite footprints and ground stations [cite: 21, 22].

## 3. Attribution Uncertainty: The Signal-to-Noise Problem

Attributing observed CO2 concentrations to specific anthropogenic activities is the most significant challenge in climate verification.

### 3.1 The Background Problem
Carbon dioxide has a long atmospheric lifetime (centuries), resulting in a high and well-mixed background concentration (currently >420 ppm). Anthropogenic plumes from cities or power plants often represent an enhancement of only a few parts per million (ppm) above this background—often less than 1% of the total column [cite: 12, 23].
*   **Quantification:** For example, in 2018, total anthropogenic CO2 concentrations represented only 1.3% of the global atmospheric concentration. This necessitates measurement accuracy better than 0.5 ppm (approx. 0.1%) to resolve emission sources effectively [cite: 12, 23].
*   **Natural Variability:** The biogenic carbon cycle (photosynthesis and respiration) creates seasonal and diurnal fluxes that are often larger in magnitude than fossil fuel emissions, masking the anthropogenic signal [cite: 24, 25, 26].

### 3.2 Distinguishing Anthropogenic vs. Biogenic Fluxes
To verify the Paris Agreement, nations must report anthropogenic emissions, not natural fluxes. Satellites, however, see the net flux.
*   **The Biogenic Confounder:** During the growing season, strong biogenic uptake can offset urban fossil fuel emissions, making a city appear as a net sink or neutral source in total column measurements [cite: 26, 27].
*   **Forests as Sources:** Recent satellite data has revealed that some forests (e.g., parts of the Amazon) have flipped from sinks to sources due to drought and fire. Distinguishing this "natural" degradation from direct anthropogenic deforestation or industrial emissions is critical for the Global Stocktake [cite: 24, 25].

## 4. Methodologies for Reducing Attribution Uncertainty

To overcome the signal-to-noise ratio and attribution challenges, the scientific community employs proxy species, multi-gas analysis, and advanced modeling.

### 4.1 The Nitrogen Dioxide (NO2) Proxy Method
The most prominent method for attributing CO2 emissions to human activity is the use of Nitrogen Dioxide (NO2) as a co-emitted tracer.
*   **Rationale:** NO2 is produced alongside CO2 during high-temperature fossil fuel combustion (power plants, vehicles). Unlike CO2, NO2 has a very short atmospheric lifetime (hours). This results in sharp, distinct plumes that clearly mark the location of combustion sources, with a signal-to-noise ratio orders of magnitude higher than CO2 [cite: 28, 29, 30, 31].
*   **Plume Detection:** Instruments like TROPOMI (on Sentinel-5P) provide high-resolution NO2 imagery. These images are used to locate the plume and define the shape of the emission field. This spatial information is then applied to the lower-resolution or noisier CO2 data to "mask" or filter the anthropogenic signal [cite: 29, 30, 32].
*   **Emission Ratios:** Researchers estimate CO2 emissions by measuring the NO2 flux and applying a NOx-to-CO2 emission ratio. This ratio varies by fuel type and technology (e.g., coal vs. gas plants). While effective, this method introduces parameter uncertainty regarding the stability of the NOx/CO2 ratio and the chemical lifetime of NOx [cite: 29, 31, 33].
*   **Synergy:** Future missions like **CO2M** (Copernicus Anthropogenic Carbon Dioxide Monitoring) will carry both CO2 and NO2 imagers on the same platform to perform this attribution simultaneously [cite: 34, 35].

### 4.2 Multi-Species and Cluster Analysis
Beyond NO2, other gases are used to fingerprint emission sources.
*   **Carbon Monoxide (CO):** CO is a proxy for incomplete combustion. Combining CO and CO2 measurements helps partition emissions between fossil fuel (high combustion efficiency) and biomass burning (lower efficiency) [cite: 27, 36].
*   **K-Means Clustering:** Researchers utilize unsupervised machine learning techniques, such as K-means clustering, on multi-species datasets (NO2, CO, SO2, XCO2 anomalies). This approach groups pixels into clusters representing different source types (e.g., "heavy industry," "traffic," "forest fire," "background"). This effectively distinguishes anthropogenic emissions from forest fires or background noise based on the chemical signature of the plume [cite: 28, 37].

### 4.3 Isotopic Constraints (Radiocarbon)
While currently limited to ground-based or aircraft campaigns, Radiocarbon (14C) analysis is the "gold standard" for attribution. Fossil fuels are devoid of 14C (due to their age), whereas biogenic carbon contains current atmospheric levels. Measuring 14C allows for a precise separation of fossil fuel CO2 (CO2ff) from biogenic CO2 (CO2bio). This data is used to calibrate and validate the "priors" used in satellite inverse modeling [cite: 27, 36, 38, 39].

## 5. Inverse Modeling and Uncertainty Quantification

Satellite data is rarely used directly; it is assimilated into atmospheric transport models via **inverse modeling** to estimate surface fluxes.

### 5.1 The Role of Priors
Inverse models require "prior" estimates of emissions (usually from bottom-up inventories) to constrain the solution. The model adjusts these priors to match the satellite observations.
*   **Uncertainty Propagation:** The uncertainty of the final flux estimate depends heavily on the uncertainty of the priors and the transport model error (e.g., wind speed errors). If the prior uncertainty is not well-characterized, the inversion may attribute model errors to emission fluxes [cite: 10, 11, 12].
*   **Prior Independence:** For verification to be credible, the "top-down" estimate must be sufficiently independent of the "bottom-up" inventory. However, because observations are sparse, priors are often necessary to regularize the problem. Advanced systems (like the European CO2MVS) aim to produce "enhanced priors" with high spatial resolution to improve this process [cite: 10].

### 5.2 Machine Learning in Inversion
Machine learning (ML) is increasingly used to bypass computationally expensive physical models or to refine plume detection.
*   **CNNs for Plume Detection:** Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are trained on satellite imagery (e.g., TROPOMI NO2) to automatically detect and segment plumes. Studies show CNNs can capture >90% of anthropogenic emissions by identifying plumes that align with inventory locations, while also discovering unreported sources [cite: 32, 40].
*   **Bias Correction:** ML is also used to correct biases in satellite retrievals by learning relationships between meteorological variables (albedo, aerosol optical depth) and retrieval errors [cite: 41].

## 6. Institutional Frameworks: From Science to Policy

The technical capability to measure emissions is being formalized into international verification frameworks.

### 6.1 The Global Stocktake (GST)
The GST is the Paris Agreement's mechanism to assess collective progress. Satellite data is explicitly recognized as a tool to improve the accuracy of national inventories, particularly for sectors with high uncertainty like Land Use, Land-Use Change, and Forestry (LULUCF) [cite: 1, 2, 6].
*   **Acceptance:** While the IPCC guidelines (2006 and 2019 Refinement) mention satellite data for verification, they do not yet mandate it as a primary reporting tool due to the technical complexity and the need for capacity building in developing nations [cite: 3, 42, 43].
*   **Complementarity:** The current consensus is that satellite data serves a QA/QC (Quality Assurance/Quality Control) function—flagging discrepancies in national inventories rather than replacing them entirely [cite: 3, 43].

### 6.2 CEOS and CGMS Roadmaps
The Committee on Earth Observation Satellites (CEOS) and the Coordination Group for Meteorological Satellites (CGMS) have developed a roadmap for an operational GHG monitoring system. This roadmap coordinates the transition from "science missions" (one-off satellites) to "operational constellations" (continuous, redundant monitoring) to support the GST [cite: 44, 45, 46, 47].

## 7. Future Directions: The CO2M Era

The European Copernicus **CO2M** mission (launching ~2026) represents the next generation of verification infrastructure.
*   **Integrated Attribution:** Unlike predecessors, CO2M is designed specifically for attribution. It carries a CO2 spectrometer, an NO2 spectrometer, and a multi-angle polarimeter (for aerosols) on the same platform. This allows for simultaneous measurement of the GHG and its combustion tracer, drastically reducing attribution uncertainty [cite: 34, 35, 48].
*   **Constellation Approach:** A constellation of satellites will reduce revisit times, increasing the probability of cloud-free observations and allowing for the monitoring of dynamic emission events [cite: 34, 48].

## 8. Conclusion

Handling data quality and attribution uncertainty is the linchpin of satellite-based climate verification. The scientific community has moved beyond simple concentration measurements to complex systems that integrate **co-emitted tracers (NO2)**, **machine learning**, and **inverse modeling** to isolate anthropogenic signals. While data quality is managed through rigorous international calibration (GSICS) and distinct uncertainty quantification, attribution remains a probabilistic exercise dependent on the precision of transport models and the separation of biogenic fluxes. As operational missions like CO2M come online, the capacity to provide independent, verifiable evidence for the Global Stocktake will shift from experimental to foundational, closing the loop between climate policy and atmospheric reality.
