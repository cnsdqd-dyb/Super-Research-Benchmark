# How Does the Parameterization of Ocean Heat Redistribution Affect Seasonal Prediction Skill Regarding ENSO Amplitude?

## Executive Summary

The accurate prediction of El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) amplitude remains a formidable challenge in seasonal climate forecasting. Research indicates that the parameterization of ocean heat redistribution—specifically through vertical mixing induced by non-breaking surface waves, mesoscale eddies, and Tropical Instability Waves (TIWs)—is a critical determinant of forecast skill. The inclusion of non-breaking surface wave-induced vertical mixing ($B_v$) has been shown to reduce the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of Niño 3.4 index predictions by approximately 20% to 28% in coupled systems, primarily by correcting upper-ocean stratification and mixed layer depth biases [cite: 1, 2, 3]. Furthermore, the parameterization of TIWs is essential for reproducing the observed asymmetry of ENSO amplitudes (stronger El Niños, weaker La Niñas) by simulating the asymmetric negative feedback of nonlinear dynamic heating [cite: 4]. Stochastic parameterizations (e.g., SPPT) also play a vital role in modulating ensemble spread and triggering ENSO onset through the realistic representation of Westerly Wind Bursts (WWBs), thereby improving the reliability of amplitude forecasts [cite: 5, 6].

## 1. Introduction

The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the dominant mode of interannual climate variability, exerting profound impacts on global weather patterns, agriculture, and economies [cite: 7, 8]. While the prediction of ENSO phase (El Niño vs. La Niña) has improved significantly, predicting the *amplitude* (intensity) of these events at seasonal lead times remains difficult. The "spring predictability barrier" (SPB) and systematic model biases continue to limit forecast skill [cite: 9, 10].

Central to ENSO dynamics is the redistribution of ocean heat content (OHC). The Recharge Oscillator (RO) theory posits that the buildup (recharge) and release (discharge) of warm water volume in the equatorial Pacific are precursors to phase transitions [cite: 7, 11]. However, General Circulation Models (GCMs) often rely on parameterizations to represent sub-grid scale processes that drive this heat redistribution, such as turbulence, wave mixing, and mesoscale eddies. Recent advances demonstrate that refining these parameterizations—particularly those governing vertical mixing and eddy-induced transport—can significantly enhance the simulation of the mean state and the dynamical feedbacks that determine ENSO amplitude [cite: 1, 12].

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of how specific parameterization schemes for ocean heat redistribution influence seasonal prediction skill, focusing on wave-induced mixing, TIWs, and stochastic physics.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: Heat Redistribution and ENSO Dynamics

### 2.1 The Recharge Oscillator and Ocean Memory
The predictability of ENSO is largely derived from the "memory" of the tropical Pacific Ocean, represented by the subsurface Ocean Heat Content (OHC). The RO model describes ENSO as a cycle where the discharge of equatorial heat to higher latitudes leads to a cooling of the eastern Pacific (La Niña), while a recharge of heat precedes warming (El Niño) [cite: 7, 11].

The accuracy of seasonal forecasts depends on how well a model preserves this memory and simulates the conversion of subsurface heat anomalies into Sea Surface Temperature (SST) anomalies. Biases in the mean state, such as the "cold tongue bias" (where the equatorial cold tongue extends too far west), can distort the thermocline feedback loop, leading to errors in predicted amplitude [cite: 13, 14].

### 2.2 The Role of Parameterization
Because global climate models cannot resolve all physical processes (e.g., small-scale turbulence, breaking waves), these processes must be parameterized. The parameterization of vertical mixing is particularly consequential for ENSO because it dictates the coupling between the atmosphere and the subsurface ocean. If vertical mixing is too weak, the mixed layer may be too shallow, making SSTs overly sensitive to wind forcing; if too strong, the thermocline feedback may be dampened [cite: 15, 16].

## 3. Impact of Vertical Mixing Parameterizations

Vertical mixing in the upper ocean controls the exchange of heat and momentum between the surface and the thermocline. Recent studies have highlighted the transformative impact of including non-breaking surface wave-induced mixing into coupled models.

### 3.1 Non-Breaking Surface Wave-Induced Mixing ($B_v$)
Traditional models often underestimate vertical mixing in the upper ocean, leading to simulated mixed layers that are too shallow and SSTs that are too warm in summer or distorted in the cold tongue region. The inclusion of non-breaking surface wave-induced vertical mixing ($B_v$) addresses this deficiency.

*   **Mechanism:** The $B_v$ scheme accounts for the turbulence generated by wave orbital motion, which penetrates deeper than the mixing generated solely by wind shear or wave breaking. This enhanced mixing deepens the Mixed Layer Depth (MLD) and stabilizes the upper ocean stratification [cite: 17, 18, 19].
*   **Impact on Mean State:** In the First Institute of Oceanography Earth System Model (FIO-ESM), the introduction of $B_v$ significantly reduced systematic biases. It cooled the simulated SSTs (counteracting warm biases in some regions) and corrected the MLD, bringing the mean state closer to observations [cite: 12, 20].

### 3.2 Quantitative Improvements in Prediction Skill
The implementation of finer parameterization schemes, specifically $B_v$, has yielded measurable improvements in seasonal forecast skill for ENSO amplitude.

*   **RMSE Reduction:** In a comparative study using the FIO-ESM Climate Prediction System (FIO-ESM-CPS), the modified model (including $B_v$ and other physical updates) showed a significant reduction in the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the Niño 3.4 index. The RMSE was reduced by over **20%** at 4- and 5-month lead times compared to the original model [cite: 1].
*   **Anomaly Correlation Coefficient (ACC):** The ACC for the Niño 3.4 index increased significantly in the modified system. For example, at a 6-month lead time, the ACC improved from 0.70 in version 1.0 to over 0.78 in version 2.0 [cite: 2].
*   **Amplitude Fidelity:** The original models tended to significantly overestimate ENSO amplitudes at longer lead times. The modified parameterization schemes dampened this error, resulting in forecast amplitudes that were far closer to observations [cite: 1].
*   **La Niña Simulation:** The inclusion of wave-induced mixing specifically intensified the simulation of La Niña events, correcting a bias where models often produced La Niña events that were too weak. Heat budget analyses attributed this to enhanced wave-induced vertical advection [cite: 12].

### 3.3 Comparison of Mixing Schemes (KPP vs. TKE)
Beyond wave-induced mixing, the choice of the baseline vertical mixing scheme—such as the K-Profile Parameterization (KPP) versus Turbulent Kinetic Energy (TKE) schemes—affects ENSO characteristics.
*   **Sensitivity:** Research indicates that while mean state biases in the tropical Atlantic and Pacific are often more sensitive to the model architecture than the mixing scheme itself, the representation of small-scale variability (e.g., diurnal cycles) differs. TKE schemes can better simulate the penetration of turbulence below the mixed layer compared to KPP, potentially influencing the rectification of intraseasonal variability onto interannual ENSO timescales [cite: 15].
*   **Parameter Tuning:** The critical bulk Richardson number in KPP affects the Equatorial Undercurrent (EUC) strength. Lowering this value can reduce EUC strength, altering the zonal advection of heat, a key feedback in ENSO growth [cite: 15].

## 4. Parameterization of Tropical Instability Waves (TIWs)

Tropical Instability Waves (TIWs) are mesoscale oceanic features that appear in the eastern equatorial Pacific and Atlantic. They play a crucial role in meridional heat transport, mixing cold equatorial waters with warmer off-equatorial waters.

### 4.1 TIW-Induced Heat Transport and ENSO Asymmetry
TIWs act as a negative feedback mechanism for ENSO. They are most active during La Niña (when the meridional temperature gradient is strong) and suppressed during El Niño (when the gradient is weak).

*   **Asymmetric Feedback:** Because TIWs warm the equator (cooling the off-equatorial region), their suppression during El Niño removes a warming source, which might intuitively suggest cooling. However, the *presence* of TIWs during La Niña provides strong dynamical heating that prevents the cold tongue from becoming excessively cold. This state-dependence creates an asymmetry: TIWs dampen La Niña intensity more than they dampen El Niño intensity [cite: 4, 21].
*   **Parameterization Necessity:** Coarse-resolution climate models cannot resolve TIWs and must parameterize their effects. Without this parameterization, models often fail to reproduce the observed asymmetry between strong El Niños and weaker La Niñas [cite: 4].

### 4.2 Impact on Prediction Skill and Amplitude
*   **Amplitude Modulation:** Explicitly resolving or accurately parameterizing TIWs leads to a reduction in the seasonal cycle amplitude of upper-ocean temperatures and stabilizes the Equatorial Undercurrent [cite: 22].
*   **Nonlinear Dynamic Heating (NDH):** Parameterization schemes that account for TIW-induced NDH via nonlinear eddy thermal diffusivity have been shown to capture the direct modulation of TIWs by ENSO. This improves the representation of the cold tongue annual cycle and the associated ENSO nonlinearity [cite: 23].
*   **Forecast Implications:** In hybrid coupled models, filtering out TIW effects resulted in a 27% increase in ENSO amplitude (specifically intensifying La Niña), demonstrating that TIWs are a damping factor. Therefore, accurate parameterization is required to prevent the over-prediction of cold event amplitudes [cite: 21].

## 5. Mesoscale Eddies and Submesoscale Processes

Beyond TIWs, general mesoscale and submesoscale eddies contribute to the energy cascade and heat transport.

*   **Eddy Parameterization (GM):** The Gent-McWilliams (GM) parameterization is standard for representing the adiabatic flattening of isopycnals by eddies. However, recent work suggests that linking mesoscale energy loss to diabatic mixing (rather than just viscous dissipation) is necessary for energetic consistency. This affects the thermal structure of the ocean interior and, consequently, the background state upon which ENSO propagates [cite: 24].
*   **Eddy-Mean Flow Interaction:** ENSO modulates the available potential energy stored in mean currents, which is then released to eddies via baroclinic instability. This interaction suggests that variations in eddy kinetic energy are predictable during strong ENSO events, providing a feedback loop that models must capture to predict the duration and decay of events accurately [cite: 25, 26].

## 6. Stochastic Physics and Ensemble Spread

While deterministic parameterizations aim to represent the mean effect of sub-grid processes, stochastic parameterizations (e.g., Stochastically Perturbed Parameterization Tendencies - SPPT) introduce noise to represent model uncertainty. This has profound implications for probabilistic seasonal forecasting.

### 6.1 Enhancing Signal-to-Noise Ratio
*   **Triggering Events:** Stochastic physics, particularly in the atmosphere, can generate Westerly Wind Bursts (WWBs) that are critical for initiating El Niño events. In models like EC-Earth, activating stochastic physics increased the frequency of WWBs, which in turn triggered more and stronger El Niño events, correcting an excessively weak ENSO bias in the control run [cite: 5, 6].
*   **Spread and Reliability:** In the ocean component, stochastic schemes (like SPPT applied to ocean mixing) increase ensemble spread, particularly in eddy-active regions. While this improves the reliability of the forecast probability distribution, it does not always reduce the deterministic RMSE of the ensemble mean [cite: 27, 28].
*   **Amplitude Correction:** In the CAM4 model, multiplicative SPPT improved the power spectrum of ENSO and *reduced* the magnitude of ENSO toward observations (counter-intuitively, by introducing noise that disrupted excessive regularity), whereas additive noise tended to increase amplitude [cite: 6, 29].

## 7. Decadal Variability and Limits of Predictability

The effectiveness of these parameterizations is modulated by the background climate state. Research highlights a decline in ENSO prediction skill post-2000.

*   **Weakening Precursors:** The relationship between Warm Water Volume (WWV) and subsequent ENSO SST anomalies weakened after 2000. The frequency of ENSO shifted, and the "whitening" of the subsurface temperature spectrum reduced the efficacy of OHC as a predictor [cite: 10, 30, 31].
*   **Implication for Parameterization:** This shift suggests that parameterizations optimized for the 1980-1999 period (dominated by strong, canonical El Niños) might need adjustment for the Central Pacific (Modoki) events more common in the 21st century. Models that better parameterize extratropical precursors and high-frequency atmospheric noise (WWBs) are essential for maintaining skill in this low-predictability regime [cite: 30, 32].

## 8. Conclusion

The parameterization of ocean heat redistribution is a fundamental lever for controlling ENSO amplitude in seasonal forecast models. The evidence leads to three major conclusions:

1.  **Wave-Induced Mixing is Critical for Bias Reduction:** The inclusion of non-breaking surface wave mixing ($B_v$) is one of the most effective methods for reducing the "cold tongue" bias and improving the RMSE of ENSO amplitude forecasts (by ~20-30%) [cite: 1, 2].
2.  **TIW Parameterization Governs Asymmetry:** Accurate representation of TIW-induced heat transport is non-negotiable for capturing the correct asymmetry between El Niño and La Niña amplitudes. Neglecting this leads to the over-intensification of La Niña events [cite: 4, 21].
3.  **Stochasticity Enhances Reliability:** Stochastic parameterizations are necessary to represent the "noise" (WWBs) that triggers ENSO. They prevent models from becoming overly periodic and improve the probabilistic skill of amplitude forecasts [cite: 5, 6].

Future improvements in seasonal prediction skill will likely stem from coupled systems that integrate explicit wave models (to resolve $B_v$ dynamically) and scale-aware parameterizations that can transition seamlessly between parameterized and resolved eddies as model resolution increases.
