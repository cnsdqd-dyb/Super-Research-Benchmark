# Perturbation Analysis of Policy Parameters in Stochastic International Migration Models: Effects on Spectral Radius and System Stability

**Key Points**
*   **Spectral Radius as a Stability Metric:** In migration models, the spectral radius ($\rho$) of the transition matrix typically represents the asymptotic population growth rate or the threshold for dynamic stability. Policy perturbations affecting migration rates directly alter $\rho$, determining whether migrant populations expand, stabilize, or contract.
*   **Sensitivity to Age-Specific Policies:** Research indicates that the spectral radius is most sensitive to perturbations in migration rates involving young adults (typically under age 30). Policies targeting this demographic yield the most significant shifts in long-term population dynamics [cite: 1, 2].
*   **Stochasticity and the Reduction Principle:** Unlike deterministic models where migration between heterogeneous patches often reduces the overall growth rate (the Reduction Principle), stochastic models reveal that migration can increase the stochastic growth rate ($\lambda_s$) by allowing populations to buffer against local environmental or economic variance [cite: 3, 4].
*   **Non-Gaussian Dynamics:** Recent stochastic evolution models (e.g., Zantout & Schewe, 2025) demonstrate that migration flows follow non-Gaussian distributions. Policy parameters in these models act not just on mean flows but on the shape and variance of flow distributions, necessitating higher-order perturbation analyses to predict effects on the spectral radius [cite: 5, 6].

## 1. Introduction

The mathematical modeling of international migration has evolved from deterministic gravity models to complex stochastic systems that account for the inherent uncertainty and volatility of human mobility. Central to the analysis of these systems is the **spectral radius** of the migration or projection matrix, a scalar value that dictates the long-term behavior of the modeled population. In deterministic linear models, the spectral radius ($\rho(\mathbf{A})$) of the projection matrix $\mathbf{A}$ corresponds to the dominant eigenvalue ($\lambda_1$), representing the asymptotic growth rate of the population [cite: 7, 8]. In stochastic settings, where the matrix $\mathbf{A}_t$ varies over time due to economic shocks, environmental changes, or policy shifts, the concept extends to the **stochastic growth rate** ($a = \log \lambda_s$), which aggregates the effects of time-varying matrices [cite: 3, 9].

Policy parameters—ranging from visa quotas and border enforcement to labor mobility agreements—act as perturbations to the entries of these matrices. Understanding how these perturbations propagate through the system to affect the spectral radius is critical for forecasting demographic futures and designing robust migration governance frameworks. This report synthesizes recent theoretical advancements and empirical studies to explain the sensitivity of the spectral radius to policy-induced perturbations within stochastic migration models.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations: Migration Matrices and Spectral Properties

To understand the effect of policy perturbations, one must first define the structure of the matrices governing international flow and the significance of their spectral properties.

### 2.1 The Structure of Migration Matrices
In multiregional demography, international flow is typically modeled using a **block-structured projection matrix** (often a generalized Leslie or Lefkovitch matrix). For a system of $R$ regions (countries) and $K$ age/stage classes, the system state at time $t$, $\mathbf{n}(t)$, is projected to $t+1$ via:
$$ \mathbf{n}(t+1) = \mathbf{A}_t \mathbf{n}(t) $$
Here, $\mathbf{A}_t$ is a stochastic or substochastic matrix composed of sub-matrices describing fertility, mortality, and migration [cite: 10, 11]. The migration components are off-diagonal blocks $\mathbf{M}_{ij}$ representing the transition rates from region $j$ to region $i$.

In pure Markov chain models of migration (where population size is fixed or irrelevant), the matrix $\mathbf{P}$ is row- or column-stochastic (sums to 1). For such matrices, the spectral radius is always $\rho(\mathbf{P}) = 1$ [cite: 12, 13]. However, in the context of *population growth* and *international flow* where stocks change, the matrix is generally non-negative and irreducible but not strictly stochastic, meaning $\rho(\mathbf{A})$ can be greater than or less than 1 [cite: 1, 14].

### 2.2 The Spectral Radius as a Growth and Stability Metric
The spectral radius $\rho(\mathbf{A}) = \max_i |\lambda_i|$ dominates the long-term dynamics.
*   **Deterministic Case:** If $\mathbf{A}$ is constant, the population grows at a rate $\lambda_1 = \rho(\mathbf{A})$ and converges to a stable stage distribution proportional to the right eigenvector $\mathbf{w}$ [cite: 8, 15].
*   **Stochastic Case:** When $\mathbf{A}_t$ is drawn from a distribution (stochastic environment), the **stochastic growth rate** is defined as $\log \lambda_s = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{1}{t} \log ||\mathbf{n}(t)||$. This rate depends not only on the mean matrix $\bar{\mathbf{A}}$ but also on the variance and autocorrelation of the matrix entries [cite: 3, 9].

## 3. Perturbation Analysis of Policy Parameters

Policy interventions can be mathematically formalized as perturbations to the entries of the migration matrix. A policy parameter $\theta$ (e.g., a cap on H-1B visas) affects specific entries $a_{ij}(\theta)$. The sensitivity of the spectral radius to this parameter is derived using the chain rule:
$$ \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial \theta} = \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial a_{ij}} \frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial \theta} $$
The term $\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial a_{ij}}$ is the **sensitivity** of the eigenvalue to the matrix element, while $\frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial \theta}$ represents the **efficacy** of the policy in changing the vital rate [cite: 15, 16].

### 3.1 Eigenvalue Sensitivity Formulas
For a distinct dominant eigenvalue $\lambda$, the sensitivity to a matrix element $a_{ij}$ is given by the product of the $i$-th element of the reproductive value vector ($\mathbf{v}$, left eigenvector) and the $j$-th element of the stable stage distribution vector ($\mathbf{w}$, right eigenvector):
$$ \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial a_{ij}} = \frac{v_i w_j}{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \rangle} $$
This formula, fundamental to matrix population models [cite: 11, 15, 17], implies that policies affecting migration flows ($a_{ij}$) will have the largest impact on the spectral radius if they target transitions *from* stages with high representation in the population ($w_j$ is large) *to* stages with high future reproductive value ($v_i$ is large).

### 3.2 Age-Specific Policy Impacts
Empirical applications of this framework have yielded specific insights into international and interregional migration. A prominent study on population decline in Japan utilized sensitivity analysis on an extended Leslie matrix incorporating interregional migration. The study found that the spectral radius (population growth rate) was most sensitive to migration rates of individuals **under the age of 30** [cite: 1, 2, 11].
*   **Mechanism:** Young migrants have the highest reproductive value ($v_i$) because they have yet to enter or are currently in their peak reproductive years. A policy that restricts the inflow of young migrants (e.g., stricter student visa policies) reduces $a_{ij}$ for these high-sensitivity classes, causing a disproportionately large reduction in $\rho(\mathbf{A})$.
*   **Contrast with Older Cohorts:** Migration of individuals over 30 had a significantly lower impact on the spectral radius, as their remaining reproductive potential is lower. This suggests that policies aiming to boost population growth through migration must prioritize younger demographics to be effective [cite: 11].

## 4. Stochastic Models and Environmental Variance

In reality, migration flows are not constant; they fluctuate due to economic cycles, political instability, and changing policies. Stochastic models incorporate this variability, fundamentally altering how perturbations affect the spectral radius.

### 4.1 Tuljapurkar’s Approximation
For a population in a stochastic environment, the stochastic growth rate $a = \log \lambda_s$ can be approximated for small noise (small fluctuations in matrix entries) as:
$$ \log \lambda_s \approx \log \lambda_0 - \frac{1}{2 \lambda_0^2} \sigma^2_E S^2 $$
where $\lambda_0$ is the dominant eigenvalue of the mean matrix, $\sigma^2_E$ is the environmental variance, and $S^2$ is a term involving the sensitivities of $\lambda_0$ [cite: 3, 9, 18].
*   **Policy Implication:** A policy that introduces uncertainty (increasing $\sigma^2_E$, e.g., unpredictable border closures) generally **depresses** the stochastic growth rate, even if the mean migration rate remains unchanged. This "dampening" effect is due to the concavity of the growth function [cite: 9].
*   **Variance Sensitivity:** The sensitivity of the stochastic growth rate to policy parameters thus includes a term for the sensitivity of the variance. If a policy stabilizes flows (reduces $\sigma^2_E$), it can increase $\lambda_s$ [cite: 19].

### 4.2 The Reduction Principle and Migration
In deterministic spatial models, the **Reduction Principle** states that if spatial locations (countries) differ in quality, mixing them through migration generally lowers the overall growth rate compared to the growth rate of the best location in isolation. The spectral radius of the coupled system is dominated by the best patch but dragged down by the sink patches [cite: 3, 20].

However, in **stochastic models**, this principle does not always hold. Research by Tuljapurkar and colleagues shows that if environmental fluctuations are large and uncorrelated across regions, migration can increase the stochastic growth rate [cite: 3, 4].
*   **Mechanism:** Migration allows lineages to escape regions temporarily suffering from poor conditions (negative shocks) and exploit regions experiencing positive shocks. This "spatial buffering" or "portfolio effect" means that perturbations increasing migration rates (e.g., open border policies) can increase the spectral radius of the metapopulation matrix in highly volatile environments, contrary to deterministic intuition [cite: 4].

## 5. Advanced Stochastic Evolution Models (Zantout & Schewe 2025)

Recent advancements have moved beyond standard matrix projection models to **stochastic evolution models** that explicitly simulate the probability distributions of migration flows. The 2025 model by Zantout and Schewe represents a significant leap in this domain [cite: 5, 6, 21, 22].

### 5.1 Model Architecture
This model combines dynamic accounting of flows (evolution equations) with stochastic sampling. Migration flows are not fixed rates but are sampled from parameterized probability distributions derived from socio-economic covariates (e.g., GDP, diaspora size) [cite: 5, 6].
*   **Non-Gaussian Flows:** The authors highlight that migration flow distributions are **non-Gaussian** and interdependent. This implies that standard approximations (like Tuljapurkar’s, which often assume Gaussian or small noise) might underestimate the complexity of system responses [cite: 5, 21].

### 5.2 Policy Parameters as Distribution Shifters
In this framework, "regional migration policies" are explicit global parameters that can be tuned [cite: 6, 21]. A perturbation in a policy parameter (e.g., a restrictiveness index) does not just scale a single matrix entry; it shifts the entire probability distribution of potential flows.
*   **Spectral Impact:** While the paper focuses on hindcasts and flow accuracy, the theoretical implication for the spectral radius is profound. A restrictive policy shifts the probability mass of the migration matrix $\mathbf{M}$ towards zero. Because the system is non-linear and stochastic, a small perturbation in policy could lead to a bifurcation or a regime shift in the spectral radius, particularly if it pushes the system across a connectivity threshold (e.g., isolating a region) [cite: 23, 24].
*   **Interdependence:** The model captures the interdependence of flows (e.g., return migration, transit migration). A policy perturbation in one country affects the inflows and outflows of connected countries, altering the global migration matrix's eigenvalues through network effects [cite: 6, 25].

## 6. Stability and Bifurcation Analysis

The spectral radius is also a primary indicator of system stability. In the context of migration-driven epidemics or information diffusion, the condition $\rho(\mathbf{M}) < 1$ often ensures the "extinction" of the diffusion process (stability of the disease-free equilibrium) [cite: 7, 14, 26].

### 6.1 Perturbations and Stability Radius
For systems with singular or near-singular migration matrices (common in sparse migration networks), the **stabilizability radius** becomes relevant. This measures the size of the smallest perturbation required to destabilize a stable system (or stabilize an unstable one) [cite: 23, 24].
*   **Switched Systems:** Migration policies often change discretely (regime switching). The stability of such **switched linear systems** is determined by the joint spectral radius of the set of possible matrices. Even if every individual policy regime corresponds to a stable matrix ($\rho(\mathbf{A}_i) < 1$), switching between them can induce instability (growth) if the matrices are non-commuting [cite: 23].

### 6.2 Rank-1 Perturbations
Many policy changes can be modeled as **rank-1 perturbations** (e.g., modifying the inflow to a single country). The Sherman-Morrison formula and related spectral bounds allow for the precise calculation of how such targeted policies affect the spectral radius. Specifically, if a policy adds a matrix $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ to $\mathbf{A}$, the new eigenvalues satisfy a characteristic equation involving the resolvent of $\mathbf{A}$ [cite: 27, 28]. This is computationally efficient for analyzing the "Target Stationary Distribution Problem," where policymakers aim to achieve a specific population distribution by minimally perturbing the migration matrix [cite: 28, 29].

## 7. Conclusion

Perturbations in policy parameters affect the spectral radius of migration matrices through three primary channels:
1.  **Direct Magnitude Effect:** Policies that alter the mean migration rates ($a_{ij}$) change $\rho(\mathbf{A})$ in proportion to the sensitivity product $v_i w_j$. Policies targeting young adults (high reproductive value) have the maximal effect on the spectral radius [cite: 1, 11].
2.  **Variance Effect:** In stochastic models, policies that increase the volatility of migration flows generally reduce the stochastic growth rate ($\log \lambda_s$) due to the "cost of variance," unless the environmental fluctuations are large enough to favor a "spatial hedging" strategy, in which case migration increases the growth rate [cite: 3, 4, 9].
3.  **Distributional Shift:** In advanced stochastic evolution models (Zantout & Schewe), policies reshape the non-Gaussian probability distributions of flows. This captures complex, interdependent dynamics where policy perturbations can trigger non-linear shifts in the system's spectral properties, potentially leading to regime shifts in migration patterns [cite: 5, 6].

Future research, particularly utilizing the Zantout-Schewe framework, is likely to focus on quantifying the "spectral sensitivity" of these non-Gaussian distributions to specific policy indices, providing a more granular tool for predicting the long-term demographic consequences of migration governance.
