## 1. Introduction

The integration of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) criteria into modern portfolio theory represents one of the most significant shifts in asset management in the 21st century. As of 2021, ESG assets surpassed $18.4 trillion globally, driven by regulatory frameworks like the Paris Agreement and the UN Sustainable Development Goals [cite: 1]. However, the transition from normative "socially responsible investing" to quantitative "ESG factor investing" has introduced profound methodological challenges. The central dilemma lies in reconciling the mathematical rigor required for **risk quantification**—the process of measuring exposure to financial externalities—with the normative demands of **responsibility standardization**—the establishment of universal definitions for corporate virtue.

This report examines whether factor model construction can effectively balance these competing demands or if the reliance on Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) inevitably introduces subjective bias through weighting mechanisms. The analysis explores the mechanics of ESG factor construction, the divergence in rating methodologies, and the trade-offs between subjective and objective weighting schemes.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: ESG as a Factor in Asset Pricing

### 2.1 The Evolution from CAPM to ESG Factor Models
Traditional asset pricing, grounded in the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) and later the Fama-French multi-factor models, posits that asset returns are a function of exposure to systematic risk factors such as market risk, size, value, profitability, and investment patterns. The integration of ESG into this framework treats sustainability not merely as a preference but as a distinct risk factor or a quality characteristic that influences the cost of capital and expected returns [cite: 2, 3].

Scholars and practitioners have attempted to augment the Fama-French five-factor model with an ESG factor, often denoted as **GMB (Good minus Bad)**. This factor is constructed by going long on companies with high ESG scores and short on those with low scores, analogous to the HML (High minus Low) value factor [cite: 4, 5, 6]. The theoretical premise is that if ESG represents a systematic risk (e.g., regulatory transition risk, reputation risk), it should command a risk premium. Conversely, if high ESG scores signal lower risk, these assets might offer lower expected returns (a "greenium") but provide insurance against tail risks [cite: 7, 8].

### 2.2 Construction Methodologies and the GMB Factor
The construction of the GMB factor relies heavily on the underlying data quality and the methodology used to sort firms.
*   **The Sorting Process:** Firms are typically ranked based on aggregate ESG scores or specific pillar scores (E, S, or G). Portfolios are formed based on quantiles (e.g., top 20% vs. bottom 20%).
*   **Regression Analysis:** The returns of these portfolios are regressed against standard factors to isolate the "ESG alpha."
*   **Findings on Efficacy:** Research indicates that while integrating ESG scores can mitigate downside risk, it often leads to high turnover and mixed out-of-sample performance [cite: 2]. Some studies suggest that the "Good minus Bad" factor does not consistently generate abnormal returns once traditional factors are controlled for, implying that ESG performance might be subsumed by quality or low-volatility factors [cite: 5, 9].

### 2.3 The Tension Between Risk and Responsibility
The construction of these models highlights the core tension: **Risk Quantification** demands precise, material, and financialized data (e.g., carbon price exposure), whereas **Responsibility Standardization** often relies on broad, normative frameworks (e.g., UN SDGs) that may not directly map to financial variance [cite: 1, 10]. When factor models use broad ESG ratings as a proxy for risk, they inherit the noise and lack of standardization inherent in those ratings, potentially distorting the risk signals [cite: 11, 12].

## 3. The Challenge of Responsibility Standardization

### 3.1 "Aggregate Confusion" and Rating Divergence
A critical impediment to robust ESG factor model construction is the lack of standardization in how "responsibility" is defined and measured. Unlike credit ratings, where the correlation between major agencies (Moody’s and S&P) is approximately **0.99**, the correlation between ESG ratings from major providers (e.g., MSCI, Sustainalytics, Refinitiv) is significantly lower, averaging around **0.61** [cite: 12, 13].

This phenomenon, termed "aggregate confusion," arises from three distinct sources of divergence:
1.  **Scope Divergence:** Agencies measure different sets of attributes (e.g., one includes electromagnetic radiation as a pollutant, another does not).
2.  **Measurement Divergence:** Agencies use different metrics for the same attribute (e.g., labor practices measured by turnover rates vs. unionization presence).
3.  **Weighting Divergence:** Agencies assign different relative importance to attributes (e.g., weighting climate change at 40% vs. 20%) [cite: 12, 13, 14].

### 3.2 The Impact on Factor Models
This lack of standardization compromises the integrity of ESG factor models. If the input variable (the ESG score) varies wildly depending on the data provider, the resulting factor (GMB) is unstable. A stock could be in the "long" basket of one model and the "short" basket of another, leading to contradictory risk quantifications [cite: 11, 15]. This divergence introduces "noise" into the asset pricing model, making it difficult to determine whether a risk premium exists or if results are merely artifacts of the chosen rating agency [cite: 12, 16].

### 3.3 Regulatory and Industry Responses
Efforts to standardize responsibility, such as the EU Taxonomy, the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR), and the ISO 26000 guidance, aim to create a common language [cite: 17, 18, 19]. However, these standards often focus on disclosure and compliance rather than financial materiality, leaving a gap between regulatory "responsibility" and the "risk" inputs needed for quantitative finance [cite: 20, 21].

## 4. Multi-Criteria Analysis (MCDA) and the Weighting Problem

To construct a single ESG score from hundreds of data points, analysts and rating agencies employ Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA). This process is the primary entry point for subjective bias.

### 4.1 The Role of Weighting in MCDA
MCDA aggregates performance across conflicting criteria (e.g., high carbon emissions vs. excellent labor relations) into a single index. The mathematical formulation generally follows a weighted sum model:
\[ S_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j \cdot x_{ij} \]
Where \( S_i \) is the composite score for firm \( i \), \( w_j \) is the weight of criterion \( j \), and \( x_{ij} \) is the normalized performance of firm \( i \) on criterion \( j \) [cite: 22].

The determination of \( w_j \) (weights) is the most contentious aspect of ESG modeling. Weights determine the trade-offs: how much environmental damage is acceptable for a unit of social good?

### 4.2 Subjective Weighting Methods
Subjective methods rely on the preferences of decision-makers or experts.
*   **Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP):** This method uses pairwise comparisons to establish a hierarchy of importance. While it structures the decision process, it is inherently biased by the specific values and priorities of the experts involved [cite: 23, 24, 25].
*   **Bias Introduction:** Subjective weighting introduces cognitive biases. For example, an analyst might overweight "Governance" due to recent high-profile scandals, or overweight "Environment" due to current climate news, regardless of the financial materiality to a specific sector [cite: 26, 27]. This results in ratings that reflect stakeholder values rather than objective risk [cite: 23].

### 4.3 Objective Weighting Methods
To counter subjective bias, objective weighting methods derive weights directly from the statistical properties of the data.
*   **Entropy Weighting:** This method assigns higher weights to criteria with greater data dispersion (variance). The logic is that attributes with little variation among companies offer little information for differentiation [cite: 28, 29, 30].
*   **CRITIC (Criteria Importance Through Intercriteria Correlation):** This method considers both the contrast intensity (standard deviation) and the conflict (correlation) between criteria [cite: 24, 31].
*   **Limitations of Objectivity:** While these methods remove human preference, they introduce a different kind of bias. Entropy weighting might assign a high weight to a trivial metric simply because the data is noisy or highly variable, while assigning a low weight to a critical risk factor (like carbon emissions) if all companies in a sector perform similarly poorly [cite: 28, 31]. Thus, "objective" does not necessarily mean "correct" or "material."

### 4.4 Hybrid Approaches
Recent literature suggests combining subjective and objective methods (e.g., fuzzy AHP combined with Entropy) to balance expert knowledge with statistical rigor [cite: 26, 32]. These hybrid models attempt to mitigate the extremes of both approaches, allowing for "responsibility" (normative values) to guide the hierarchy while "risk quantification" (statistical variance) refines the specific weights [cite: 33, 34].

## 5. Does Weighting Introduce Subjective Bias?

The query asks specifically if weighting introduces subjective bias. The research evidence confirms that **weighting is the primary conduit for subjective bias in ESG portfolios**, but it also highlights that "objective" alternatives have their own flaws.

### 5.1 The Inevitability of Bias
Even in "objective" models, the selection of criteria (which data points to include) is a subjective choice [cite: 27, 35]. Furthermore, the choice of normalization technique (e.g., Min-Max vs. Z-score) can alter the final rankings, influencing the factor model's output [cite: 22, 25].
*   **Green Dilution:** Research by Scientific Beta found that mixing E, S, and G scores often leads to "green dilution," where strong performance in one area cancels out poor performance in another. For instance, weighting schemes that combine unrelated objectives resulted in only 8% of the carbon reduction objective surviving in the final portfolio [cite: 36]. This suggests that multi-criteria weighting can obscure specific responsibility goals.

### 5.2 Industry-Specific vs. Equal Weighting
*   **Equal Weighting:** A "naive" approach that weights E, S, and G equally. While transparent, it ignores the fact that environmental risks are existential for energy companies but less material for tech firms [cite: 37].
*   **Industry-Specific Weighting:** MSCI and others use dynamic weights based on industry materiality (e.g., heavy E weight for Utilities). This approach aligns better with **risk quantification** (financial materiality) but introduces subjectivity in defining what is "material" for each industry [cite: 37, 38]. Research shows that industry-specific weighting schemes tend to outperform equal weighting in long-term financial performance, suggesting that "biased" (or expert-informed) weights may be superior for risk management [cite: 37].

## 6. Balancing Risk and Responsibility: Dynamic and Factor Solutions

### 6.1 Dynamic Weighting
Static weighting schemes fail to capture the changing nature of risk. **Dynamic weighting** adjusts the importance of factors over time based on market conditions or volatility.
*   **Temporal Decay:** Some models apply time-decay functions, giving more weight to recent ESG data to capture short-term correlations while retaining historical data for long-term trends [cite: 39].
*   **Volatility Targeting:** Adjusting weights based on the volatility of the factors can help control risk. Research indicates that dynamic optimization can improve Sharpe ratios and portfolio resilience during market turbulence (e.g., COVID-19), where "Social" factors became temporarily more critical than "Environmental" ones [cite: 40, 41].

### 6.2 The "Green Minus Bad" Factor as a Compromise
The construction of the GMB factor itself attempts to balance these forces. By using a long-short approach, it isolates the *spread* in returns attributable to ESG.
*   **Risk Mitigation:** High ESG-rated portfolios have been shown to exhibit lower idiosyncratic risk and lower exposure to volatility [cite: 42, 43].
*   **Performance:** Evidence is mixed. Some studies find no significant abnormal returns (alpha) for ESG factors, suggesting that markets may already price these risks efficiently or that the "noise" in ratings obscures the signal [cite: 3, 9]. Others find that ESG factors provide a "quality" tilt that protects value during downturns [cite: 2, 8].

### 6.3 Separation of Objectives
To avoid the "subjective bias" of aggregating conflicting goals, some researchers advocate for **separation** rather than integration. Instead of a single weighted ESG score, portfolios should target specific objectives (e.g., a Carbon Factor, a Diversity Factor) independently. This avoids "green dilution" and allows for precise risk quantification of specific variables without the muddiness of a composite score [cite: 36].

## 7. Conclusion

In ESG portfolios, factor model construction faces a difficult trade-off between **risk quantification** and **responsibility standardization**.

1.  **Can they be balanced?** Yes, but imperfectly. Factor models can quantify ESG risk, but only to the extent that the underlying data is material and standardized. The current lack of correlation among rating agencies (0.61) severely hampers the precision of these models.
2.  **Does weighting introduce subjective bias?** Yes. Weighting in Multi-Criteria Analysis is inherently subjective. Even "objective" statistical methods rely on subjective choices regarding data selection and model architecture.
    *   **Subjective weights** (AHP) align better with normative responsibility goals but introduce cognitive bias.
    *   **Objective weights** (Entropy) align better with data properties but may disconnect from financial materiality.
    *   **Industry-specific dynamic weighting** appears to be the most effective method for balancing financial risk (materiality) with responsibility, as it acknowledges that the importance of E, S, and G factors varies by sector and time.

Ultimately, the "bias" in weighting is not merely a statistical nuisance but a reflection of the diverse values inherent in "responsibility." For academic and practical purposes, the most robust approach is likely not to eliminate bias, but to make it explicit through transparent, dynamic, and material-specific weighting schemes, or to separate E, S, and G into distinct factors to avoid the dilution of objectives.
