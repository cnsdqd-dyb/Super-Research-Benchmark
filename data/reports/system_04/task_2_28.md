### Executive Summary
The proliferation of wearable health monitors has necessitated the development of signal processing algorithms capable of extracting accurate vital signs—such as heart rate (HR) and respiration rate—from signals heavily contaminated by motion artifacts (MA). A critical challenge in this domain is the "power-accuracy trade-off." Sophisticated algorithms often require substantial computational resources, draining the small batteries inherent to wearable form factors. Conversely, simple filters often fail to distinguish between complex motion artifacts and physiological signals, particularly when their frequency spectra overlap.

Recent research highlights three distinct algorithmic pathways to address this challenge. First, **adaptive filtering** has evolved from basic Least Mean Squares (LMS) methods to sophisticated variants like DWT-RI, which leverages multi-resolution analysis to separate noise from signal with minimal processing overhead. Second, **Compressive Sensing (CS)** has emerged as a hardware-software co-design strategy that reduces power consumption at the source by lowering the sampling rate and LED duty cycle, reconstructing signals only when necessary or extracting features directly from the compressed domain. Third, **Deep Learning**, specifically lightweight models (TinyML) and Generative Adversarial Networks (CycleGAN), is proving that computationally intensive training can yield efficient inference models that eliminate the need for reference sensors (like accelerometers), thereby reducing overall system power consumption.

## 1. Introduction to Motion Artifacts and Power Constraints

Wearable devices, including smartwatches, fitness trackers, and medical patches, rely primarily on Photoplethysmography (PPG) and Electrocardiography (ECG) to monitor vital signs. While ECG measures the electrical activity of the heart, PPG uses optical sensors to detect volumetric changes in blood circulation [cite: 1, 2]. Both modalities are highly susceptible to Motion Artifacts (MA). In PPG, MAs arise from the relative movement between the sensor and the skin, changes in tissue compression, and ambient light leakage [cite: 1, 3]. These artifacts often manifest in the frequency spectrum within the same band as the heart rate (0.5–4 Hz), making simple band-pass filtering ineffective [cite: 2, 4].

The constraint of power consumption is equally critical. Wearable devices typically operate on small batteries with limited capacity. The power budget is consumed by three main components: sensing (e.g., driving LEDs in PPG), processing (microcontroller usage), and data transmission (Bluetooth/Wi-Fi) [cite: 5, 6]. Algorithms that require high sampling rates or complex, iterative calculations can rapidly deplete the battery. Therefore, the ideal signal processing solution must be **robust** against non-stationary motion noise and **computationally efficient** to enable long-term continuous monitoring [cite: 7, 8].

## 2. Adaptive Filtering and Recursive Algorithms

Adaptive filtering remains a cornerstone of real-time artifact suppression. These algorithms typically employ a reference signal—most commonly from a tri-axial accelerometer (ACC)—to model the motion noise and subtract it from the corrupted vital sign signal.

### 2.1. The DWT-RI Adaptive Algorithm
One of the most promising developments for power-constrained devices is the **Discrete Wavelet Transform Recursive Inverse (DWT-RI)** algorithm. This method addresses the high computational complexity associated with traditional adaptive filters when dealing with non-stationary signals like MA.

*   **Mechanism:** The DWT-RI algorithm combines Multiresolution Analysis (MRA) with an adaptive filter. It decomposes the raw PPG signal and the reference accelerometer signal into different frequency bands using the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT). This decomposition allows the algorithm to isolate specific frequency components where motion energy is concentrated [cite: 9].
*   **Recursive Inverse (RI):** The "Recursive Inverse" component refers to the weight adaptation mechanism. Unlike standard Least Mean Squares (LMS) which can be slow to converge, or standard Recursive Least Squares (RLS) which is computationally heavy, the DWT-RI utilizes a recursive update of the autocorrelation matrix estimation. This provides fast convergence and stability with reduced computational cost compared to full RLS implementations [cite: 10, 11].
*   **Performance:** Research indicates that DWT-RI can achieve an absolute average error of approximately **1.17 Beats Per Minute (BPM)** during physical exercise [cite: 9, 12]. This level of accuracy is competitive with much more complex offline algorithms while remaining suitable for real-time implementation on wearable microcontrollers.
*   **Computational Efficiency:** By operating in the wavelet domain, the algorithm can focus its adaptive filtering resources only on the sub-bands where artifact overlap occurs, rather than processing the entire spectral width. This selective processing contributes to its low power profile [cite: 9, 12].

### 2.2. Traditional Adaptive Noise Cancellation (ANC)
Standard ANC techniques using Normalized Least Mean Squares (NLMS) or RLS algorithms are widely used due to their simplicity.
*   **Operation:** These filters adjust their coefficients iteratively to minimize the error between the primary signal (corrupted PPG) and the reference signal (accelerometer).
*   **Limitations:** While computationally light, basic ANC often fails when the frequency of the motion artifact coincides exactly with the heart rate frequency (spectral overlap). In such cases, the filter may suppress the vital sign itself [cite: 13]. Furthermore, the requirement for an accelerometer increases the hardware power budget and integration complexity [cite: 1, 2].
*   **Forehead vs. Wrist:** Studies suggest that the efficacy of ANC varies by sensor location. Signals from the forehead are generally less susceptible to MA than wrist-based signals, potentially allowing for simpler, lower-power ANC implementations in head-worn wearables [cite: 14].

## 3. Compressive Sensing (CS) for Ultra-Low Power Acquisition

Compressive Sensing (CS) represents a fundamental shift from "sample then compress" to "sample compressively." This approach targets the dominant source of power consumption in PPG systems: the LED driver.

### 3.1. Principles of CS in Wearables
Traditional Nyquist sampling requires signals to be sampled at twice their highest frequency. However, physiological signals like PPG are "sparse" in the frequency domain (i.e., they consist of a few dominant frequencies). CS allows for random, non-uniform sampling at rates significantly below the Nyquist limit, provided the signal can be reconstructed in a sparse domain [cite: 5, 15].

*   **Power Savings:** By reducing the sampling rate, the duty cycle of the LED driver is reduced proportionally. Since the LED can consume milliwatts of power (compared to microwatts for processing), this yields massive system-level energy savings [cite: 6, 16].
*   **Readout ASICs:** Specialized Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) have been developed to implement CS. For example, a reported CS-based PPG readout ASIC consumes only **172 $\mu$W** of power. This chip performs feature extraction directly from the compressively sampled data, avoiding the energy penalty of full signal reconstruction or raw data transmission [cite: 5, 6].

### 3.2. Reconstruction-Free Feature Extraction
A major advantage of modern CS implementations in wearables is the ability to estimate heart rate without fully reconstructing the time-domain signal.
*   **Lomb-Scargle Periodogram (LSP):** Since CS data is non-uniformly sampled, standard Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) cannot be used. The Lomb-Scargle Periodogram is used to estimate the Power Spectral Density (PSD) directly from the sparse samples. This allows the device to track the heart rate peak in the frequency domain with minimal computational overhead [cite: 17, 18].
*   **Sparse Signal Reconstruction (SSR):** When full signal reconstruction is required (e.g., for morphological analysis), algorithms like **TROIKA** and **JOSS** are employed.
    *   **TROIKA:** Decomposes the signal using Singular Spectrum Analysis (SSA) and reconstructs it using sparse Bayesian learning. It is highly accurate but computationally demanding [cite: 19, 20].
    *   **JOSS (Joint Sparse Spectrum):** Improves upon TROIKA by jointly estimating the spectra of the PPG and accelerometer signals using a Multiple Measurement Vector (MMV) model. While JOSS offers superior accuracy by exploiting the common sparsity of motion artifacts across sensors, it is computationally heavier than TROIKA, making it less ideal for ultra-low-power edge devices unless implemented on dedicated hardware accelerators [cite: 19, 21].

## 4. Generative AI and Non-Accelerometer Approaches

A significant recent advancement is the move toward "reference-free" artifact removal using machine learning, specifically Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs).

### 4.1. CycleGAN for Artifact Removal
The integration of accelerometers, while helpful for ANC, adds to the power budget and bill of materials. **CycleGAN** (Cycle-Consistent Generative Adversarial Network) frameworks have been adapted to clean PPG signals without requiring any reference motion data.

*   **Mechanism:** The model treats the denoising problem as an unpaired image-to-image translation task (or signal-to-signal). It learns to map the domain of "noisy PPG" to "clean PPG" using two generators and two discriminators. Crucially, it does not require paired training data (i.e., the exact same signal recorded with and without noise simultaneously, which is physically impossible) [cite: 1, 22].
*   **Energy Efficiency:** By eliminating the need for an accelerometer, the system power consumption can be reduced by approximately **45%**. The computational cost of running the inference model is offset by the hardware savings and the elimination of multi-channel synchronization and processing [cite: 1, 22].
*   **Performance:** Studies report a **9.5-fold improvement** in motion artifact removal accuracy compared to state-of-the-art methods, maintaining signal integrity even during complex daily activities [cite: 1, 23].

### 4.2. Lightweight Deep Learning (TinyML)
To run deep learning models on wearable microcontrollers (Edge AI), architectures must be highly optimized.
*   **Tiny-PPG:** This is a lightweight deep neural network designed for real-time artifact segmentation and removal on edge devices. It utilizes depth-wise separable convolutions to minimize the number of parameters and operations, ensuring it fits within the tight memory and power constraints of wearable SoCs (System on Chips) [cite: 24].
*   **Transformer Models:** Recent work has explored using transformer architectures with attention mechanisms to learn the relationship between contact pressure (measured by force sensors) and PPG morphology. These models can effectively reconstruct signals by focusing on the most relevant temporal features, though optimizing them for edge computing remains an active area of research [cite: 25].

## 5. Hyperdimensional Computing (HDC)

Hyperdimensional Computing (HDC) is an emerging paradigm inspired by the brain's information processing. It encodes data into high-dimensional (e.g., 10,000 bits) holographic vectors.

*   **Robustness and Efficiency:** HDC is inherently robust to noise and hardware faults due to the holographic distribution of information. Operations on these vectors (binding, bundling, permutation) are computationally simple (mostly bitwise XOR and addition), making them extremely energy-efficient and parallelizable [cite: 26, 27].
*   **Application:** In the context of wearables, HDC has been applied to fuse PPG and IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) data for robust activity recognition and user authentication. Its lightweight nature allows for on-device learning and personalization, adapting to the specific motion patterns of the user without sending data to the cloud [cite: 26, 28]. While primarily used for classification, its noise-tolerance properties are being adapted for signal enhancement and artifact suppression.

## 7. Spectral and Decomposition Methods

Beyond adaptive filtering and AI, several decomposition techniques are employed to separate signal from noise based on spectral characteristics.

*   **Singular Spectrum Analysis (SSA):** Used within the TROIKA framework, SSA decomposes the signal into additive components. By identifying and removing components that correlate with motion frequencies (derived from accelerometers), the signal is cleaned. However, SSA is computationally intensive [cite: 20, 29].
*   **Spectral Peak Tracking (SPA):** Algorithms like **SpaMA** utilize time-varying spectral analysis. They calculate the power spectral density of both PPG and accelerometer signals. Peaks in the PPG spectrum that align with accelerometer peaks are discarded. This method is often combined with heuristic tracking to maintain continuity in heart rate estimation [cite: 30, 31].
*   **Independent Component Analysis (ICA):** ICA attempts to separate the PPG signal into statistically independent components (source separation). While effective for multi-channel setups, it assumes that motion and cardiac signals are independent, which is not always true (e.g., cadence-driven blood flow). It also typically requires higher computational power than adaptive filters [cite: 2, 32].

## 8. Conclusion and Future Directions

To effectively suppress motion artifacts while maintaining accurate continuous monitoring within limited power budgets, a hybrid or context-aware approach is often best.

1.  **For Ultra-Low Power Hardware:** **Compressive Sensing** is the superior choice for reducing the analog front-end power (LEDs), coupled with **Lomb-Scargle** estimation for direct feature extraction without reconstruction [cite: 5, 17].
2.  **For Real-Time Processing on Microcontrollers:** The **DWT-RI** algorithm offers the best balance of low computational complexity and high accuracy, making it suitable for standard wearable processors during active user periods [cite: 9].
3.  **For System-Level Optimization:** **CycleGAN** and other non-accelerometer deep learning methods provide a pathway to eliminate auxiliary sensors entirely, reducing the physical footprint and power consumption of the device [cite: 1, 22].
4.  **Future Trends:** The integration of **Hyperdimensional Computing** suggests a move toward cognitive, self-learning wearables that can adapt to user-specific artifacts with negligible power costs [cite: 27]. Additionally, the development of **ASICs** that embed these specific algorithms (like the CS readout chips) will further decouple monitoring accuracy from general-purpose processing power [cite: 6].

In summary, the field is moving away from brute-force filtering toward intelligent, sparse, and generative methods that exploit the specific characteristics of physiological signals to maximize information retrieval per unit of energy consumed.
