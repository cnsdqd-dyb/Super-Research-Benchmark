## Introduction

The formation of satisfaction evaluations is a complex cognitive process central to consumer behavior, social psychology, and organizational management. It is rarely a direct reaction to a product or service's objective quality; rather, it is a comparative judgment. The prevailing academic consensus identifies the mechanism driving this evaluation as the interaction between **planning expectations** (the anticipated standard formed prior to an event) and **actual experience** (the perceived performance during or after the event).

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the mechanisms that translate the gap between expectations and experience into satisfaction judgments. It explores the foundational **Expectancy Disconfirmation Theory (EDT)**, the cognitive biases of **Assimilation and Contrast** that modulate how gaps are perceived, and the specific implications of "planning expectations" in sectors ranging from tourism to strategic management.

## Theoretical Foundation: Expectancy Disconfirmation Theory (EDT)

For over four decades, **Expectancy Disconfirmation Theory (EDT)** has served as the dominant paradigm for explaining satisfaction formation [cite: 1, 2]. Originating from social psychology and consumer research, EDT suggests that satisfaction is a function of the discrepancy between initial expectations and perceived performance [cite: 3, 4].

### The Core Mechanism
The mechanism operates on a temporal sequence:
1.  **Formation of Expectations:** Prior to consumption or implementation, individuals establish a reference point known as "expectations." These can be predictive (what will happen), normative (what should happen), or ideal (what could happen) [cite: 2].
2.  **Perceived Performance:** The individual experiences the event, product, or service and forms a perception of its performance.
3.  **Comparison (The Disconfirmation Process):** The individual compares the perceived performance against the initial expectations.
4.  **Outcome Generation:** This comparison yields a "disconfirmation" value—a gap that determines the emotional and cognitive state of satisfaction [cite: 3, 5].

### The Three Outcomes of Disconfirmation
The gap between planning expectations and actual experience results in three specific evaluative states, which determine the intensity and direction of satisfaction [cite: 3, 5, 6, 7]:

#### 1. Simple Confirmation (The Zero Gap)
Simple confirmation occurs when the **actual experience matches the planning expectations** exactly.
*   **Mechanism:** There is no discrepancy to process. The performance falls within the individual's "latitude of acceptance."
*   **Result:** This typically leads to a baseline level of satisfaction or neutrality. While the customer is not dissatisfied, they are also not "delighted." Some researchers argue this leads to a state of "neither satisfaction nor dissatisfaction," while others view it as a reinforcement of the initial attitude [cite: 5].
*   **Example:** A consumer buys an energy drink expecting a specific boost in alertness and receives exactly that level of energy [cite: 7].

#### 2. Positive Disconfirmation (The Positive Gap)
Positive disconfirmation occurs when **actual experience exceeds planning expectations**.
*   **Mechanism:** The performance surpasses the reference point set during the planning phase.
*   **Result:** This generates high levels of satisfaction, often described as "delight." It strengthens positive attitudes toward the provider and increases the likelihood of repurchase or loyalty [cite: 3, 5].
*   **Example:** Purchasing a camera based on reviews and finding that its efficiency and image quality are significantly better than anticipated [cite: 7].

#### 3. Negative Disconfirmation (The Negative Gap)
Negative disconfirmation occurs when **actual experience falls short of planning expectations**.
*   **Mechanism:** The performance fails to meet the reference standard. This creates a psychological conflict or "dissonance" between what was promised/planned and what was delivered.
*   **Result:** This leads to dissatisfaction, frustration, and unfavorable attitudes. It is the primary driver of complaints and customer churn [cite: 3, 5, 7].
*   **Example:** Visiting a highly-rated restaurant and finding the food quality to be poor [cite: 7].

## Psychological Processing of the Gap: Assimilation and Contrast

While EDT describes *what* happens when a gap exists, it does not fully explain *how* the human mind processes the magnitude of that gap. Two competing yet complementary psychological theories—**Assimilation Theory** and **Contrast Theory**—explain the cognitive adjustments individuals make when facing a discrepancy between planning expectations and actual experience [cite: 1, 8, 9].

### Assimilation Theory: Minimizing the Gap
Assimilation theory posits that individuals have a natural tendency to maintain cognitive consistency. When a discrepancy arises between expectations and experience, individuals experience psychological discomfort (cognitive dissonance). To reduce this discomfort, they may psychologically adjust their perception of the experience to align more closely with their initial expectations [cite: 8, 9].

*   **The Mechanism:** If the gap is small or ambiguous, the consumer "assimilates" the experience toward the expectation.
*   **Effect on Satisfaction:**
    *   If expectations were high and performance is slightly lower, the consumer may rate the performance higher than it objectively was (assimilating toward the high expectation).
    *   If expectations were low and performance is slightly higher, the consumer may rate the performance lower (assimilating toward the low expectation) [cite: 9].
*   **Implication:** High planning expectations can act as a buffer, protecting satisfaction levels against minor service failures because the consumer subconsciously "fixes" the perception to match the plan [cite: 1].

### Contrast Theory: Magnifying the Gap
Contrast theory suggests the opposite effect. When the discrepancy between expectations and experience is sufficiently large, the assimilation mechanism breaks down. Instead of blending the experience with the expectation, the individual magnifies the difference [cite: 8, 9, 10].

*   **The Mechanism:** The experience is perceived as being "farther away" from the expectation than it objectively is.
*   **Effect on Satisfaction:**
    *   **Positive Contrast:** If performance is significantly better than expected, it is rated as exceptionally good (higher than an objective evaluation would suggest).
    *   **Negative Contrast:** If performance is significantly worse than expected, it is rated as exceptionally poor.
*   **Implication:** This explains the "boom or bust" nature of high-stakes planning. If a heavily planned event (like a vacation or a product launch) misses the mark significantly, the dissatisfaction is disproportionately severe due to the contrast effect [cite: 10, 11].

### Assimilation-Contrast Theory: The Integrated Model
The most robust explanation for how satisfaction is formed from these gaps is the **Assimilation-Contrast Theory**, which integrates both mechanisms using the concept of "latitudes" derived from Social Judgment Theory [cite: 8, 11, 12, 13].

1.  **Latitude of Acceptance (Assimilation Zone):** If the gap between planning expectations and actual experience is small enough to fall within the individual's "latitude of acceptance," **assimilation** occurs. The consumer ignores the minor deviation and remains satisfied (or dissatisfied) in accordance with their initial expectation [cite: 11, 12].
2.  **Latitude of Rejection (Contrast Zone):** If the gap is large enough to fall into the "latitude of rejection," **contrast** occurs. The consumer recognizes the discrepancy as significant and exaggerates it. This leads to extreme satisfaction (if positive) or extreme dissatisfaction (if negative) [cite: 10, 11, 12].

**Table 1: The Assimilation-Contrast Mechanism**

| Gap Size | Zone | Psychological Process | Resulting Evaluation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Small** | Latitude of Acceptance | **Assimilation** | Perception shifts *toward* expectation. Discrepancy is minimized. Satisfaction aligns with initial plan. |
| **Large** | Latitude of Rejection | **Contrast** | Perception shifts *away* from expectation. Discrepancy is magnified. Satisfaction is extremely high or low. |

## Planning Expectations: Context-Specific Mechanisms

The query specifically references "planning expectations." Research highlights that the nature of "planning" creates specific types of expectations that influence the satisfaction mechanism differently across various domains.

### 1. Tourism and Experiential Planning
In tourism, the "planning phase" is a distinct part of the consumption experience. The gap between the "imagined" trip (planning expectations) and the "realized" trip (actual experience) is critical [cite: 14, 15, 16].

*   **The Mechanism:** Tourists form elaborate expectations during the pre-consumption phase (searching, planning, anticipating). This phase often involves idealization, creating a high reference point [cite: 15, 16].
*   **Gap Management:** Because the planning phase is often prolonged and emotionally involved, tourists may be more prone to **assimilation** to protect their emotional investment. However, if the destination fails to provide basic amenities (hygiene, safety), the gap widens into the latitude of rejection, triggering **negative contrast** and ruining the experience [cite: 14, 17].
*   **Co-Creation:** Satisfaction in this context is often "co-created." If the tourist actively participates in the planning, they may feel partially responsible for the outcome, potentially moderating negative disconfirmation through self-attribution biases [cite: 14, 16].

### 2. Organizational and Strategic Planning
In business contexts, "planning expectations" refer to strategic forecasts (e.g., workforce capacity, financial targets) versus actual operational results [cite: 18, 19, 20].

*   **The Implementation Gap:** Research identifies a frequent "lag" where actual implementation falls behind planning expectations (e.g., only 70% of a plan is implemented) [cite: 21].
*   **Satisfaction Mechanism:** In this context, satisfaction is often measured by stakeholders (investors, managers). When "actual experience" (e.g., economic downturns, delays) causes a deviation from the "plan," it creates a **Knowledge Gap** or **Execution Gap** [cite: 22].
*   **Feedback Loop:** Unlike consumer satisfaction, which is often a terminal evaluation, satisfaction with organizational planning is cyclical. A gap between planning and experience triggers a "feedback loop" to adjust future planning expectations (e.g., revising Solvency II actuarial functions or workforce models) [cite: 20, 23].

### 3. Public Policy and Urban Planning
In urban planning, satisfaction is derived from the gap between the "planning expectations" (what the government promised or what the master plan depicted) and the "actual experience" of the residents [cite: 18, 21, 24].

*   **Lag Effects:** Planning implementation often lags behind expectations due to external factors (e.g., economic crises). Research shows that even when implementation is incomplete, satisfaction can remain high if the *perceived* progress falls within the latitude of acceptance (Assimilation) [cite: 21].
*   **Mediating Factors:** Satisfaction with public facilities and disaster prevention planning mediates the relationship between general service satisfaction and the intention to stay in a city. Here, "expectations for future development" act as a buffer for current gaps [cite: 24].

## Measuring the Gap: Analytical Frameworks

To manage the mechanism of satisfaction formation, researchers and practitioners utilize specific frameworks to measure the gap between planning and experience.

### The Gap Analysis Model (SERVQUAL)
The **Gap Analysis** method, often associated with the SERVQUAL model, identifies specific fissures between expectations and delivery that lead to dissatisfaction [cite: 22, 25, 26].

*   **The Customer Gap:** The difference between customer expectations and customer perceptions. This is the ultimate measure of satisfaction [cite: 22].
*   **The Knowledge Gap:** The difference between consumer expectations and management's *perception* of those expectations. If management plans for the wrong expectations, satisfaction is impossible regardless of execution [cite: 22].
*   **The Service Performance Gap:** The difference between service quality specifications (the plan) and the actual service delivery (the experience). This is often where "planning expectations" fail due to resource or training constraints [cite: 22].

### The Perception Gap
Recent literature emphasizes the **Perception Gap**—the disconnect between what a business believes it is providing and what the customer perceives they are receiving [cite: 27].
*   **Visual vs. Actual:** In e-commerce, a common source of negative disconfirmation is the gap between the "planned" purchase (based on high-quality photos) and the "actual" product (which may look cheaper in reality). This visual discrepancy immediately triggers the contrast effect [cite: 27].

## Moderating Factors in Satisfaction Formation

The mechanism converting the expectation-experience gap into satisfaction is not uniform; it is moderated by several variables.

### 1. Level of Involvement
*   **High Involvement:** When a consumer is highly involved (e.g., planning a wedding or buying a house), they have a narrower latitude of acceptance. They are more likely to notice gaps, increasing the probability of **contrast effects** (strong dissatisfaction) if expectations are not met [cite: 6, 28].
*   **Low Involvement:** In low-stakes scenarios (e.g., buying a candy bar), consumers have a wider latitude of acceptance. They are more likely to **assimilate** minor deficiencies, leading to "passive" satisfaction [cite: 6, 28].

### 2. Source of Expectations
*   **Predictive Expectations:** What the consumer believes *will* happen. Gaps here are treated as forecast errors.
*   **Normative Expectations:** What the consumer believes *should* happen. Gaps here are treated as moral or performance failures, often leading to stronger negative disconfirmation [cite: 2].
*   **Stated vs. Unstated:** Research suggests that explicitly *stating* expectations prior to an experience (e.g., in a survey) can prime the consumer to be more critical, potentially leading to lower post-experience evaluations due to a heightened focus on the gap (Negativity Enhancement) [cite: 29].

## Conclusion

The mechanism that forms satisfaction evaluations from the gap between planning expectations and actual experience is a dynamic cognitive process defined by **Expectancy Disconfirmation Theory**. It is not a simple subtraction of "Experience minus Expectation." Instead, it is a psychological calculation filtered through **Assimilation-Contrast** biases.

When the gap is small, the mind tends to **assimilate** the experience to match the plan, preserving satisfaction. When the gap is large, the mind **contrasts** the experience, resulting in exaggerated delight or deep dissatisfaction. This mechanism is critical in domains like tourism and strategic management, where "planning expectations" are elaborate and emotionally charged. Ultimately, satisfaction is less about the objective reality of the experience and more about where that experience falls relative to the "latitudes of acceptance" established during the planning phase.
