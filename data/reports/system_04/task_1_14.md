# The Role of Thermal Runaway Propagation Mechanisms in Designing Safety Redundancy for Lithium-Ion Battery Packs: Electrolyte and Separator Stability

### Key Points
*   **Thermal Runaway Propagation (TRP) is the primary catastrophic failure mode in battery packs**, driven by heat transfer (conduction, convection, and radiation) and the combustion of ejected materials.
*   **Electrolyte combustion contributes approximately 60-70% of the total energy released** during a thermal runaway event, making electrolyte stability a critical factor in determining the severity of propagation.
*   **Separators act as the final physical barrier against internal short circuits**; their ability to maintain dimensional integrity (resist shrinkage) at high temperatures is essential for preventing the initial trigger of thermal runaway.
*   **Safety redundancy requires a multi-tiered approach**, integrating intrinsic cell safety (stable electrolytes, ceramic-coated separators) with extrinsic pack protections (thermal barriers, robust venting, and active cooling) to decouple cell failure from system failure.

### Executive Summary
The design of safety redundancy in lithium-ion battery (LIB) packs is fundamentally dictated by the mechanics of thermal runaway propagation. This phenomenon transforms a single-cell failure into a cascading system-wide catastrophe. Research indicates that the propagation mechanism is not merely thermal conduction between cells but is significantly exacerbated by the ejection of flammable electrolytes and gases. Specifically, the combustion of the liquid electrolyte accounts for the majority of the heat energy released during failure, necessitating redundancy designs that focus on containment and thermal isolation. Furthermore, the separator serves as the critical gatekeeper; its thermal stability determines whether a minor thermal event escalates into a full internal short circuit. Consequently, effective safety redundancy is achieved not through a single solution but through a hierarchy of defenses: modifying cell chemistry to reduce flammability, reinforcing separators to prevent internal shorts, and engineering pack architectures that manage thermal and mass exhaust to inhibit cell-to-cell propagation.

## 1. Introduction: The Imperative of Safety Redundancy in High-Energy Density Storage

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have become the ubiquitous power source for electric vehicles (EVs) and stationary energy storage systems (ESS) due to their high energy density and cycle life. However, the electrochemical potential that makes LIBs effective also renders them susceptible to thermal runaway (TR), a self-sustaining exothermic reaction chain that can lead to fire and explosion [cite: 1, 2]. While single-cell failures can occur due to manufacturing defects or abuse, the primary safety objective in battery pack design is preventing **thermal runaway propagation (TRP)**—the "domino effect" where the failure of one cell triggers adjacent cells, leading to the catastrophic loss of the entire pack [cite: 3, 4].

Designing for safety redundancy involves creating multiple overlapping layers of protection that function independently to prevent TR or contain it if it occurs. This report analyzes the mechanistic role of TRP in redundancy design, with a specific focus on the physicochemical stability of two critical components: the **electrolyte** and the **separator**. These components represent the fuel and the firewall of the battery cell, respectively. Their stability limits define the boundary conditions for safe operation and dictate the engineering requirements for thermal management and containment systems at the module and pack levels [cite: 5].

## 2. Mechanisms of Thermal Runaway Propagation

To design effective redundancy, one must first understand the vectors through which thermal runaway spreads. TRP is a complex multiphysics phenomenon involving heat transfer, fluid dynamics, and chemical combustion.

### 2.1 Heat Transfer Vectors
Propagation occurs when the heat generated by a failing cell raises the temperature of neighboring cells above their critical onset threshold (typically 130°C–150°C) [cite: 1, 6]. The transfer mechanisms include:

*   **Conduction:** Direct heat transfer through cell-to-cell contact or shared structural components (busbars, cooling plates, side walls). In tightly packed modules, conduction is often the primary mode of initial heat transfer [cite: 7, 8].
*   **Convection:** The release of hot vent gases and aerosols transfers heat to the surrounding air and adjacent cell surfaces. This is particularly dangerous in packs with poor airflow management, where hot gas pockets can form [cite: 8, 9].
*   **Radiation:** As cell temperatures during TR can exceed 800°C–1000°C, radiative heat transfer becomes significant, preheating adjacent cells and compromising their thermal stability [cite: 10, 11].

### 2.2 The Role of Ejecta and Electrolyte Combustion
A critical and often underestimated factor in propagation is the ejection of battery materials. When a cell vents, it releases a mixture of gases (CO, CO2, H2, hydrocarbons) and atomized liquid electrolyte [cite: 12, 13].
*   **Combustion Energy:** Research indicates that the combustion of the ejected electrolyte and gases accounts for **60% to 70%** of the total energy released during a thermal runaway event [cite: 14, 15]. This external combustion creates a "fire storm" within the pack, subjecting neighboring cells to temperatures far higher than those generated by internal conduction alone.
*   **Flame Propagation:** The ignited ejecta can bypass thermal barriers designed for conduction, directly impinging on the seals and vents of adjacent cells, causing them to fail mechanically before they fail thermally [cite: 16].

### 2.3 The Propagation Timeline
The speed of propagation is a critical design parameter. In poorly designed packs, cell-to-cell propagation can occur within 5–15 minutes, or even seconds in the case of violent side-wall ruptures [cite: 11]. Redundancy design aims to extend this interval to infinity (no propagation) or sufficiently long to allow for occupant evacuation and emergency response [cite: 17].

## 3. The Role of Electrolyte Stability in Safety Redundancy

The electrolyte is the medium for ion transport, but in the context of safety, it is a volatile fuel source. Its stability—or lack thereof—is a primary driver of the severity of a thermal event.

### 3.1 Chemical Decomposition and Gas Generation
Standard LIB electrolytes consist of lithium salts (e.g., LiPF6) dissolved in organic carbonate solvents (e.g., EC, DMC, EMC).
*   **Decomposition Onset:** At temperatures as low as 80°C (depending on State of Charge), the Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) decomposes. By 100°C–120°C, the electrolyte itself begins to break down and react with the anode [cite: 15].
*   **Exothermic Reactions:** The reaction between the electrolyte and the charged anode is exothermic, accelerating the cell's self-heating rate. Further heating leads to the decomposition of the cathode, releasing oxygen which reacts violently with the electrolyte [cite: 1, 18].
*   **Gas Generation:** The decomposition produces massive volumes of gas (1–2.5 L/Ah), pressurizing the cell until it vents or ruptures [cite: 8, 19]. This venting is the mechanism that releases the flammable fuel into the pack enclosure.

### 3.2 Electrolyte Flammability as a Propagation Catalyst
The flammability of the electrolyte is the single largest contributor to the total heat release in a battery fire.
*   **Energy Release:** As noted, up to 70% of the energy in a TR event comes from the chemical burning of the electrolyte outside the cell casing [cite: 15]. This implies that thermal barriers designed only to block conductive heat from the cell's internal reaction (the remaining 30%) may be insufficient if the electrolyte ignites.
*   **Redundancy Implication:** Safety redundancy must include mechanisms to prevent electrolyte ignition (e.g., inert gas environments) or manage the flames (e.g., flame arrestors).

### 3.3 Advanced Electrolyte Designs for Intrinsic Safety
To reduce the reliance on external pack protections, redundancy is increasingly being built into the electrolyte itself:
*   **Flame Retardant Additives:** Additives such as phosphazenes or fluorinated compounds can reduce flammability and scavenge radical species, increasing the onset temperature of thermal runaway and reducing the heat release rate [cite: 20, 21, 22].
*   **Solid-State Electrolytes (SSEs):** Replacing liquid electrolytes with non-flammable solid ceramic or polymer electrolytes eliminates the primary fuel source. SSEs offer superior thermal stability and prevent leakage, theoretically eliminating the risk of propagation driven by electrolyte combustion [cite: 23, 24]. This represents the ultimate form of material-level redundancy.

## 4. The Role of Separator Stability in Safety Redundancy

The separator is a porous membrane placed between the anode and cathode. It is the physical safeguard against internal short circuits (ISC). Its behavior under thermal stress is a decisive factor in whether a cell enters thermal runaway.

### 4.1 Shutdown vs. Breakdown Mechanisms
*   **Shutdown (Safety Feature):** Polyolefin separators (PE/PP) are designed to melt and close their pores at specific temperatures (PE at ~130°C, PP at ~165°C) [cite: 25, 26]. This "shutdown" blocks ion transport, stopping the electrochemical reaction and preventing further current flow. This is a critical intrinsic redundancy feature [cite: 27].
*   **Breakdown (Failure Mode):** If the temperature continues to rise beyond the shutdown point, or if the separator shrinks due to surface tension and thermal stress, the physical barrier is lost. This leads to direct contact between the anode and cathode (ISC), releasing all stored electrical energy as heat instantaneously [cite: 6, 27].

### 4.2 Thermal Shrinkage and Dimensional Stability
Thermal shrinkage is a major vulnerability. Standard polyolefin separators can shrink significantly at temperatures above 150°C, exposing the electrode edges and causing shorts [cite: 28, 29].
*   **Ceramic Coatings:** To enhance redundancy, modern separators are coated with ceramic particles (e.g., alumina). These coatings provide a rigid skeleton that prevents shrinkage even if the polymer matrix melts. Ceramic-coated separators can maintain integrity at temperatures exceeding 200°C, significantly delaying or preventing the ISC that triggers TR [cite: 30, 31, 32].
*   **High-Temperature Materials:** Advanced separators made from materials like polyimide (PI) or non-woven cellulose nanofibers offer thermal stability up to 250°C or higher, providing a robust buffer against thermal abuse [cite: 28, 33].

## 5. Designing Safety Redundancy: A Hierarchical Approach

Effective safety redundancy integrates the behaviors of electrolytes and separators into a cohesive system design. This hierarchy operates at the material, cell, and pack levels.

### 5.1 Level 1: Material and Cell-Level Redundancy
The first line of defense is intrinsic stability.
*   **Separator Selection:** Using ceramic-coated or high-temperature separators ensures that the cell can withstand transient high temperatures without developing an internal short circuit. This prevents a thermal event in one cell from becoming an electrical short that dumps energy into the system [cite: 28, 30].
*   **Electrolyte Modification:** Employing flame-retardant additives or solid-state electrolytes reduces the calorific value of the failure. If a cell does fail, the reduced chemical energy release lowers the probability that adjacent cells will be triggered [cite: 21, 24].
*   **Internal Fusing:** Devices like Positive Temperature Coefficient (PTC) thermistors and Current Interrupt Devices (CID) built into the cell cap provide electrical redundancy, cutting off current during overcharge or overheating before TR can begin [cite: 1].

### 5.2 Level 2: Module-Level Redundancy
If a cell fails, module design must prevent propagation to neighbors.
*   **Thermal Barriers:** Placing insulating materials (aerogels, mica sheets, phase change materials) between cells blocks conductive heat transfer. These barriers must be robust enough to withstand the erosion from high-velocity hot gas ejection [cite: 7, 34, 35].
*   **Spacing and Air Gaps:** Physical separation reduces conductive and radiative transfer. However, this reduces energy density, so it is often combined with thermal barriers [cite: 5, 17].
*   **Interstitial Heat Sinks:** Aluminum or copper plates between cells can absorb transient heat spikes and distribute them to a cooling system, preventing localized hot spots from reaching the TR trigger temperature [cite: 34].

### 5.3 Level 3: Pack-Level Redundancy
The final layer manages the consequences of a multi-cell failure.
*   **Venting and Exhaust Management:** Since 60-70% of the energy is in the ejected fluid, the pack must have a dedicated exhaust path to channel hot gases and flames away from other modules and the vehicle cabin. Rupture discs and directional venting channels are essential [cite: 7, 12].
*   **Active Cooling:** Liquid cooling systems (or immersion cooling) can actively extract heat from a failing module. High-performance thermal management systems (TMS) are designed to operate at maximum capacity when a TR event is detected [cite: 2, 36].
*   **Battery Management System (BMS):** The BMS acts as the "brain" of redundancy, monitoring voltage, current, and temperature. Advanced algorithms can detect the early signs of SEI decomposition or micro-shorts and isolate the affected string or module before runaway occurs [cite: 37, 38].

## 6. Synthesis: The Interplay of Mechanisms and Design

The relationship between propagation mechanisms and redundancy design is dialectical.
*   **Electrolyte Stability vs. Venting Design:** Because liquid electrolytes generate massive gas volumes and heat upon combustion, pack designs must prioritize gas management. If solid-state electrolytes are used, the redundancy focus shifts from gas management to simple thermal conduction management, as the risk of explosive gas generation is minimized [cite: 23].
*   **Separator Stability vs. Thermal Barriers:** A robust separator allows a cell to get hotter without shorting. This provides a wider safety margin for the thermal barriers to work. If the separator fails early (e.g., at 130°C), the thermal barrier has less time to dissipate heat before the neighbor triggers. Therefore, investment in high-stability separators directly reduces the burden on pack-level thermal insulation [cite: 28].

## 7. Future Directions and Emerging Technologies

Research is moving toward "zero-propagation" packs.
*   **Solid-State Batteries (SSBs):** By eliminating the flammable liquid electrolyte, SSBs remove the primary fuel for propagation. This could render heavy physical containment systems redundant, increasing energy density [cite: 23, 39].
*   **Smart Separators:** Separators with functional coatings that can detect dendrites or release flame retardants upon reaching a trigger temperature are under development [cite: 40].
*   **Immersion Cooling:** Submerging cells in a dielectric fluid provides immediate quenching of thermal events, preventing the separator from ever reaching its melting point and suppressing electrolyte ignition [cite: 36].

## 8. Conclusion

The role of the thermal runaway propagation mechanism in designing safety redundancy is foundational. It dictates that redundancy cannot be achieved by a single component but requires a systemic approach. The instability of the **electrolyte** drives the requirement for gas management and fire suppression, as it is the primary source of energy release. The stability of the **separator** dictates the thermal threshold of the system; it is the critical component that prevents the conversion of thermal abuse into catastrophic electrical failure. Effective safety redundancy, therefore, is the engineering of a system where the separator is robust enough to withstand initial heat, the electrolyte is formulated to minimize combustion energy, and the pack architecture is capable of isolating and exhausting the inevitable energy release of a single-cell failure without engaging its neighbors. Achieving this synergy is the key to the safe deployment of high-energy lithium-ion batteries.
