# The Influence of Local Chemical Ordering on Dislocation-Twin Boundary Interactions in High-Entropy Alloys

### Key Points
*   **Local Chemical Ordering (LCO) creates a "rugged" energy landscape:** Unlike ideal random solid solutions, HEAs exhibit short-range ordering (SRO) where specific atomic neighbors are energetically preferred. This creates local fluctuations in lattice friction and stacking fault energy (SFE), fundamentally altering dislocation mobility via a mechanism known as "nanoscale segment detrapping" [cite: 1, 2, 3].
*   **History-Dependent Stacking Fault Energy:** The interaction is dynamic. As a leading dislocation glides, it destroys the local LCO, creating a "diffuse antiphase boundary" (DAPB). This process changes the local SFE for subsequent dislocations, often lowering the barrier for deformation twinning in a phenomenon termed "history-dependent multi-layer generalized stacking fault energy" (HDML-GSFE) [cite: 4, 5, 6].
*   **Modulation of Twin Nucleation and Growth:** LCO influences the unstable stacking fault energy ($\gamma_{usf}$), which is the barrier for dislocation nucleation. In chemically ordered domains, the nucleation of partial dislocations (precursors to twins) may be suppressed or promoted depending on the specific local chemistry (e.g., Cu-rich clusters), thereby controlling the onset of the Twinning-Induced Plasticity (TWIP) effect [cite: 7].
*   **Dislocation Transmission across Twin Boundaries:** When dislocations encounter a twin boundary (TB), LCO fluctuations prevent the simultaneous constriction of Shockley partials. This leads to a unique interaction mode where dislocations must "detrap" segment-by-segment to cross the boundary, significantly enhancing the strain-hardening capability of the alloy compared to pure metals [cite: 8].

## Introduction

High-entropy alloys (HEAs), also known as complex concentrated alloys (CCAs), represent a paradigm shift in metallurgy, moving from single-principal-element systems to multi-principal-element solid solutions stabilized by high configurational entropy. Early theoretical frameworks treated HEAs as ideal random solid solutions (RSS). However, recent advanced characterization and atomistic simulations have revealed that these alloys frequently possess Local Chemical Ordering (LCO) or Short-Range Ordering (SRO)—non-random atomic arrangements spanning a few nanometers [cite: 1, 9, 10].

The presence of LCO fundamentally alters the plastic deformation mechanisms of HEAs. In conventional alloys, plastic deformation is governed by the motion of dislocations and their interaction with obstacles (grain boundaries, precipitates). In HEAs, the LCO itself acts as a pervasive, intrinsic obstacle field. A critical aspect of plasticity in high-performance HEAs is the interaction between dislocation motion and deformation twinning, a mechanism that provides simultaneous strength and ductility (the TWIP effect).

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of how LCO structures influence the interaction between dislocation motion and twin boundary energy. It explores the "rugged" potential energy landscape created by LCO, the concept of history-dependent stacking fault energy, and the specific atomistic mechanisms—such as nanoscale segment detrapping—that govern how dislocations navigate and modify twin boundaries in these chemically complex materials.

## The Nature of Local Chemical Ordering in HEAs

### Defining LCO and SRO
Local Chemical Ordering (LCO) refers to the deviation from a statistically random distribution of atoms in a solid solution. In HEAs, enthalpy-driven interactions between different constituent elements lead to a preference for specific nearest-neighbor bonds (e.g., Co-Cr attraction or Ni-Al ordering) [cite: 11, 12]. While the material maintains a single-phase crystal structure (e.g., FCC or BCC) on a macroscopic scale, the atomic-scale environment is chemically heterogeneous [cite: 9, 10].

This ordering is distinct from long-range order (LRO) found in intermetallic compounds. LCO domains are typically sub-nanometric (< 1-2 nm) and dispersed throughout the matrix [cite: 9]. This structure creates a unique "energy landscape" for defects. Unlike the uniform lattice friction of a pure metal, the energy landscape in an HEA with LCO is "rugged," characterized by spatial fluctuations in bond energies and lattice distortions [cite: 1, 2].

### The Rugged Energy Landscape
The primary consequence of LCO is the variation in local lattice resistance. As a dislocation line moves through an HEA, it encounters regions of varying chemical stability.
*   **Energy Valleys:** Regions where the dislocation core energy is minimized due to favorable local atomic arrangements.
*   **Energy Peaks:** Regions where the dislocation must break favorable bonds (destroying LCO) or navigate severe lattice distortion [cite: 2, 13].

This heterogeneity forces dislocations to adopt wavy configurations and move via thermally activated processes even in FCC alloys, which typically exhibit low lattice friction in their pure forms [cite: 2]. This phenomenon is critical for understanding how dislocations eventually interact with twin boundaries.

## Influence of LCO on Stacking Fault Energy (SFE)

The interaction between dislocation motion and twinning is fundamentally governed by the Stacking Fault Energy (SFE). In HEAs, LCO renders SFE a local, variable, and dynamic property rather than a global constant.

### Spatial Fluctuation of SFE
In conventional metallurgy, SFE is treated as a single value for a given composition. In HEAs, the local chemical environment varies significantly across different slip planes. Research indicates that SFE is not a unique value but follows a distribution depending on the local concentration of elements (e.g., the number of specific solute atoms in the first-nearest neighbor shell of the fault) [cite: 5, 14].
*   **Local Descriptors:** Studies on refractory HEAs (e.g., TiVNbTaMo) and FCC HEAs have shown that local SFE can be predicted using concentration-based descriptors. The fluctuation can be substantial, prohibiting the prediction of local deformation modes based solely on average composition [cite: 14].
*   **Negative SFE:** In some metastable HEAs (e.g., CrCoNi systems), local regions may exhibit negative SFE, indicating a thermodynamic driving force for phase transformation (FCC $\to$ HCP) or twinning, which is modulated by the degree of LCO [cite: 8, 15].

### History-Dependent Multi-Layer Generalized SFE (HDML-GSFE)
A pivotal concept in understanding LCO-dislocation-twin interaction is the "history dependence" of the Generalized Stacking Fault Energy (GSFE).
*   **LCO Destruction:** When a leading partial dislocation glides on a slip plane, it disrupts the pre-existing LCO, effectively creating a "diffuse antiphase boundary" (DAPB) [cite: 4, 16, 17]. This process consumes energy, effectively raising the stress required for the *first* dislocation to move.
*   **Glide Plane Softening:** Once the LCO is destroyed by the leading dislocation, the resistance for subsequent dislocations on the same plane is reduced. This phenomenon, known as "glide plane softening," promotes planar slip, which is a precursor to deformation twinning [cite: 4, 5, 18].
*   **Dynamic SFE Modification:** The SFE is effectively dynamic. The passage of the first partial dislocation changes the chemical environment for the trailing partial. If the destruction of LCO makes the faulted state energetically more favorable (relative to the initial ordered state), it can effectively lower the energy barrier for the nucleation of a second partial dislocation, thereby facilitating twin growth [cite: 5, 6].

## Dislocation Motion Mechanisms in LCO Environments

Before addressing the twin boundary interaction directly, it is necessary to establish how dislocations move through the LCO field, as this dictates the arrival state of the dislocation at the twin boundary.

### Nanoscale Segment Detrapping
In HEAs with LCO, dislocation motion does not occur via the smooth expansion of loops as seen in pure metals. Instead, it proceeds via a "nanoscale segment detrapping" mechanism [cite: 2, 3, 8].
*   **Mechanism:** The dislocation line gets pinned by local chemical domains (LCOs) that act as obstacles. The dislocation advances by the forward bulging of nanometer-scale segments that thermally activate (detrap) from these pinning sites.
*   **Implication for BCC vs. FCC:** In BCC HEAs, this mechanism can replace kink-pair nucleation as the rate-limiting step for screw dislocations, or act as the primary resistance for edge dislocations, equalizing the mobility of both characters [cite: 3]. In FCC HEAs (like NiCoCr), this mechanism increases lattice friction and promotes strain hardening [cite: 2].

### Diffuse Antiphase Boundaries (DAPB)
The motion of dislocations in LCO-containing alloys creates a trail of chemical disorder. This is analogous to the creation of an Antiphase Boundary (APB) in ordered intermetallics, but in HEAs, it is "diffuse" because the order is short-range [cite: 16, 17, 19].
*   **Strengthening Contribution:** The energy penalty associated with creating a DAPB provides a significant strengthening component. This is often termed "order strengthening" or "SRO hardening" [cite: 19].
*   **Planarity:** Because the first dislocation pays the energy penalty to create the DAPB, subsequent dislocations face less resistance. This encourages dislocations to follow the same path, leading to highly planar slip bands. Planar slip is a prerequisite for the coordinated movement of partial dislocations required to form deformation twins [cite: 18, 20].

## Interaction Mechanism: Dislocation Motion and Twin Boundary Energy

The core of the query lies in how LCO influences the interplay between moving dislocations and Twin Boundaries (TBs). This interaction is bi-directional: LCO affects the formation (nucleation) of twins, and the presence of LCO at TBs alters how incoming dislocations interact with them.

### 1. Influence on Twin Nucleation and Unstable SFE
Deformation twinning is initiated by the nucleation of Shockley partial dislocations. The energy barrier for this nucleation is the unstable stacking fault energy ($\gamma_{usf}$).
*   **Chemical Fluctuations and Nucleation Sites:** LCO induces fluctuations in $\gamma_{usf}$. In Cu-containing HEAs (e.g., CuNiCoFe), Cu-rich clusters (a form of LCO/segregation) have been shown to lower the local $\gamma_{usf}$. This creates "soft spots" where dislocations and twins can nucleate more easily than in the random solid solution matrix [cite: 7].
*   **Suppression vs. Promotion:** While some LCO types lower the barrier, general SRO in systems like CoCrNi is often reported to *increase* the unstable stacking fault energy compared to the random state, initially suppressing twinning and promoting slip. However, once slip initiates and destroys SRO, the barrier drops, leading to a transition in deformation modes [cite: 4, 5].

### 2. The "Nanoscale Segment Detrapping" at Twin Boundaries
When a dislocation (specifically a screw dislocation) approaches a Coherent Twin Boundary (CTB), it must constrict into a perfect dislocation to cross (transmit) or absorb into the boundary. LCO fundamentally changes this unit process.
*   **Asynchronous Constriction:** In pure metals (like Ni), Shockley partials constrict simultaneously along the dislocation line at the TB. In HEAs (like CoNiCrFeMn), the statistical fluctuation of chemical ordering causes the partials to constrict *asynchronously*. The dislocation line is pinned at various points by LCO, requiring a "nanoscale segment detrapping" process to bring the partials together at the interface [cite: 8].
*   **Increased Transmission Resistance:** This asynchronous process raises the stress required for dislocation transmission across the TB. The LCO effectively "roughens" the TB interaction, making the twin boundary a more potent barrier to dislocation motion than in conventional alloys. This contributes significantly to the high work-hardening rate of HEAs [cite: 8].

### 3. Dynamic Twin Boundary Energy and Mobility
The energy of the twin boundary itself is not static during deformation in the presence of LCO.
*   **LCO-Pinning of TBs:** Twin boundaries can be pinned by LCO structures. For a TB to migrate (growing the twin), it must move partial dislocations along the boundary plane. If these partials encounter strong LCO domains (e.g., Ni-LCO in twin planes), their motion is restricted [cite: 11].
*   **Twinning-Assisted GB Dynamics:** In some cases, the interaction is dynamic. Grain boundaries (GBs) and TBs can adjust their mobility via twinning. The emission of partials from GBs changes the local misorientation and structure. In HEAs, this is mediated by the local chemical environment, which dictates the ease of partial emission [cite: 21, 22].
*   **Energy Dissipation:** The interaction of dislocations with LCO at the TB dissipates more energy than in RSS lattices due to the complex bond breaking and re-forming required. This enhances the toughness of the material [cite: 19].

### 4. The Role of "History-Dependent" SFE in Twinning
The most sophisticated understanding of this interaction involves the history-dependent nature of the energy landscape.
*   **The Mechanism:**
    1.  **Initial State:** High LCO exists. SFE is relatively high or fluctuating. Resistance to initial slip is high.
    2.  **Slip Event:** A leading partial dislocation glides, destroying LCO across the slip plane.
    3.  **SFE Drop:** The destruction of LCO leaves behind a plane with a different (often lower) SFE relative to the disordered state, or simply removes the "friction" of the LCO.
    4.  **Twinning Activation:** The lowered effective SFE on the slipped plane facilitates the glide of trailing partials or the nucleation of twinning dislocations on adjacent planes. This explains why twinning often activates after a certain degree of plastic strain (once LCO has been sufficiently disturbed to create favorable pathways) [cite: 4, 5, 6].
*   **Interlayer Coupling:** The slip on one layer affects the chemical environment of neighboring layers. The "collapse" of CSRO (Chemical Short Range Order) induces local shear softening, promoting the growth of twins (which requires slip on successive planes) [cite: 5, 6].

## Case Studies and Material Systems

### CoCrNi and Cantor Alloys (FCC)
*   **System:** CoCrNi and FeMnCoCrNi (Cantor).
*   **Observation:** These alloys exhibit exceptional toughness at cryogenic temperatures.
*   **LCO Role:** LCO (e.g., Cr-Co-Ni ordering) increases the lattice friction via the segment detrapping mechanism [cite: 2]. At low temperatures, the SFE decreases, and the "history-dependent" destruction of LCO promotes the formation of nanotwins. The interaction of dislocations with these LCO-hardened twins leads to massive strain hardening [cite: 4, 5, 23].

### Cu-containing HEAs (FCC)
*   **System:** CuNiCoFe and similar alloys.
*   **Observation:** Cu tends to segregate or form clusters (LCO).
*   **LCO Role:** Cu-rich regions have lower unstable stacking fault energy ($\gamma_{usf}$). These regions act as nucleation sites for dislocations and twins. The LCO here acts as a "softener" for nucleation but a "hardener" for propagation in the matrix [cite: 7].

### Refractory HEAs (BCC)
*   **System:** Ti-V-Zr-Nb-Al and Mo-based alloys.
*   **Observation:** High strength but often limited ductility.
*   **LCO Role:** LCO increases the SFE in systems like Ti-V-Zr-Nb-Al as Al content increases [cite: 24, 25]. The dislocation motion is governed by the detrapping of screw dislocations from LCO environments. Twinning is observed as a surface-nucleated or defect-nucleated mechanism when the stress is high enough to overcome the LCO-imposed friction [cite: 3, 26].

## Implications for Mechanical Properties

The interaction between LCO, dislocation motion, and twin boundary energy is the atomistic origin of the superior mechanical properties of HEAs.
1.  **Strength-Ductility Synergy:** The LCO promotes planar slip (via glide plane softening), which delays cross-slip and encourages dislocation pile-ups. These pile-ups eventually trigger twinning (TWIP effect). The twins then act as dynamic obstacles (Hall-Petch effect), sustaining the work-hardening rate and preventing premature necking [cite: 9, 20, 27].
2.  **Strain Hardening:** The "nanoscale segment detrapping" mechanism at TBs ensures that twins remain strong obstacles to dislocation transmission throughout the deformation process, maintaining high strain hardening rates up to large strains [cite: 8].
3.  **Tunability:** By heat treating to adjust the degree of LCO (e.g., aging to increase SRO), materials scientists can tune the SFE and the critical stress for twinning, effectively engineering the yield strength and ductility balance [cite: 9, 20, 28].

## Conclusion

In High-Entropy Alloys, the Local Chemical Ordering (LCO) structure acts as a critical, dynamic modulator of plasticity. It influences the interaction between dislocation motion and twin boundary energy in three distinct ways:
1.  **Dynamic SFE Modulation:** LCO renders the Stacking Fault Energy history-dependent. Dislocation slip destroys LCO, creating Diffuse Antiphase Boundaries (DAPB) and altering the local SFE to facilitate subsequent planar slip and twin growth (HDML-GSFE theory).
2.  **Nucleation Control:** LCO fluctuations create local variations in unstable stacking fault energy ($\gamma_{usf}$), serving as preferential sites for twin nucleation (e.g., in Cu-rich clusters) or suppressing it in strongly ordered domains until sufficient stress is applied.
3.  **Interaction Mechanics:** At the intersection of dislocations and Twin Boundaries, LCO imposes a "nanoscale segment detrapping" mechanism. This prevents the simultaneous constriction of partial dislocations, significantly increasing the energy barrier for transmission and enhancing the barrier strength of twins.

This complex interplay transforms the twin boundary from a static barrier into a dynamic, chemically responsive interface, enabling the exceptional work-hardening and damage tolerance characteristic of advanced high-entropy alloys.
