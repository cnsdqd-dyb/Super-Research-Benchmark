### Key Points
*   **Trade-off Between Frequency and Efficiency:** Optimal capacity configuration often faces a conflict between minimizing emissions per vehicle (favoring smaller, lighter vehicles) and minimizing emissions per passenger/ton (favoring larger vehicles with high load factors). Research indicates that while larger vehicles generally offer better per-seat efficiency, reducing service frequency to fill them can drive demand toward more carbon-intensive modes like private cars, negating the benefits.
*   **Network Topology Implications:** The debate between Hub-and-Spoke (H&S) and Point-to-Point (P2P) networks is central to capacity planning. H&S networks typically allow for the use of larger, more efficient aircraft and higher load factors but incur penalties from longer total travel distances and congestion. Conversely, P2P networks reduce distance but often rely on smaller, less efficient vehicles. Current evidence suggests H&S may offer lower total emissions in specific contexts due to economies of scale, though P2P reduces the "domino effect" of delays.
*   **Policy Efficacy (Caps vs. Taxes):** Structural shifts in capacity configuration (e.g., switching modes or fundamentally changing fleet composition) are more effectively driven by hard emission constraints (caps) rather than carbon taxes. Taxes are often absorbed as operating costs without inducing significant technological or capacity changes unless the rates are prohibitively high.
*   **Stochastic Factors:** Uncertainty in emission parameters (e.g., speed, weather, technology evolution) significantly impacts long-term capacity planning. Stochastic models suggest that incorporating risk-averse strategies against carbon price volatility leads to more diverse capacity portfolios, such as a mix of renewable integration and flexible logistics networks.

The configuration of transport capacity—encompassing decisions on fleet size, vehicle dimensions, service frequency, and network design—is increasingly governed by the distribution of carbon emissions across different operational phases and modes. Traditionally, capacity planning focused on minimizing financial costs and maximizing service levels. However, the integration of carbon constraints has introduced a multi-objective optimization landscape where the "optimal" configuration shifts significantly depending on how emissions are accounted for (e.g., per vehicle vs. per passenger) and regulated (e.g., taxes vs. caps).

Research highlights a critical dependency on **Load Capacity Factors (LCF)**. High load factors generally dilute the carbon intensity of transport, favoring consolidated capacity strategies like mass transit and hub-based aviation networks. However, this consolidation must be balanced against the risk of reduced service frequency, which can inadvertently increase system-wide emissions by pushing demand toward private, lower-occupancy modes. Furthermore, the distribution of emissions is not uniform; it varies stochastically with factors like congestion, technology adoption, and energy grid composition, necessitating robust, probabilistic planning models rather than deterministic ones.

## 1. Introduction: The Decarbonization of Capacity Planning

The transportation sector is a primary contributor to global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, necessitating a fundamental restructuring of how transport capacity is planned and configured. Optimal capacity configuration refers to the strategic determination of fleet size, vehicle specifications (capacity, fuel type), service frequency, and network topology to meet demand constraints while minimizing costs and environmental impact.

Historically, capacity configuration models treated environmental externalities as secondary or ignored them entirely. The modern paradigm, however, requires integrating **carbon emission distributions**—functions that describe how emissions vary with load, distance, speed, and technology—into the core objective functions of logistics and transport planning. This shift impacts decision-making at three levels:
1.  **Strategic:** Network design (e.g., Hub-and-Spoke vs. Point-to-Point).
2.  **Tactical:** Fleet acquisition and sizing (e.g., large vs. small vehicles).
3.  **Operational:** Frequency setting and routing (e.g., load consolidation).

The distribution of carbon emissions is not merely a static parameter but a dynamic function of **Load Capacity Factor (LCF)**, which measures the ratio of actual load to maximum capacity. As LCF increases, the emission per unit of cargo or passenger decreases, creating a strong incentive for capacity consolidation. However, this relationship is complicated by stochastic variables such as demand fluctuation, regulatory uncertainty (carbon taxes vs. caps), and the non-linear nature of emissions relative to vehicle speed and congestion.

## 2. Carbon Emission Characteristics and Load Capacity Factors

Understanding the impact of emissions on capacity requires analyzing how different modes generate and distribute carbon. The "carbon footprint" is rarely a linear function of distance; it is heavily influenced by the vehicle's capacity utilization and the energy mix powering it.

### 2.1. Modal Emission Distributions
Different transport modes exhibit distinct emission profiles that dictate their optimal capacity configurations:
*   **Aviation:** Emissions are heavily concentrated in the take-off and landing phases, making short-haul flights disproportionately carbon-intensive per kilometer. Long-haul flights benefit from cruising efficiency but require heavier fuel loads. The distribution of emissions favors larger aircraft on hub routes where high load factors can be guaranteed [cite: 1, 2, 3].
*   **Road Transport:** For passenger cars, emissions are highly sensitive to occupancy. A single-occupancy vehicle (SOV) is among the most carbon-intensive modes, whereas high-occupancy vehicles (HOVs) or buses can rival rail efficiency. This disparity drives capacity planning toward maximizing occupancy rather than just vehicular efficiency [cite: 1, 4, 5].
*   **Maritime and Rail:** These modes benefit from massive economies of scale. The emission distribution is relatively flat per ton-km once a certain capacity threshold is reached, incentivizing the use of the largest possible vessels or train sets, provided demand is sufficient to fill them [cite: 6, 7].

### 2.2. The Role of Load Capacity Factor (LCF)
The **Load Capacity Factor (LCF)** is a critical metric in sustainable capacity planning. It is defined as the ratio of the actual load (passengers or freight) to the maximum carrying capacity of the vehicle or system.
*   **Inverse Relationship with Emissions:** Research consistently demonstrates a negative relationship between LCF and carbon intensity. Higher LCF values imply that the fixed carbon cost of moving a vehicle is distributed over more units of demand, lowering the per-unit footprint [cite: 8, 9].
*   **Biocapacity Context:** In broader environmental economics, LCF is also used to compare biocapacity (supply of ecological services) with the ecological footprint (demand). An LCF greater than 1 indicates sustainability. In transport, this translates to designing capacity that remains within the "carrying capacity" of the environmental airshed [cite: 10, 11, 12].

### 2.3. Lifecycle vs. Operational Emissions
Optimal capacity configuration must also account for the distribution of emissions across the vehicle's lifecycle.
*   **Manufacturing vs. Operation:** For electric vehicles (EVs), a significant portion of the carbon distribution is front-loaded in battery manufacturing. This impacts fleet replacement cycles; retiring internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles too early for EVs can result in a "carbon debt" that takes years to pay off. Capacity planning models must therefore balance the operational efficiency of new fleets against the embodied carbon of production [cite: 13, 14].
*   **Grid Intensity:** The optimal capacity for electric fleets depends heavily on the carbon intensity of the local electricity grid. In regions with coal-heavy grids, the optimal configuration may still favor efficient ICEs or hybrids over pure EVs to minimize total emissions [cite: 15].

## 3. Impact on Fleet Sizing and Vehicle Capacity

One of the most direct impacts of carbon emission constraints is on the determination of optimal vehicle size and fleet composition. This involves a complex trade-off between the efficiency of large vehicles and the service quality provided by frequent, smaller vehicles.

### 3.1. The Vehicle Size vs. Frequency Trade-off
In public transit and logistics, planners must decide between a large fleet of small vehicles (high frequency) and a small fleet of large vehicles (low frequency).
*   **The Consolidation Argument:** From a purely operational emission perspective, larger vehicles (e.g., articulated buses, wide-body aircraft) are often more efficient per passenger-km *if* they are full. This drives capacity configuration toward fewer, larger vehicles to maximize load consolidation [cite: 16].
*   **The Modal Shift Paradox:** However, minimizing social costs (which include user waiting times and emissions) can lead to counter-intuitive results. If capacity is configured solely to maximize vehicle size and reduce operator costs, frequency drops. This degradation in service quality can force passengers to switch to private cars, which are far more carbon-intensive. Therefore, **optimal capacity under carbon constraints may actually favor a larger fleet of smaller buses** to maintain high frequency and prevent modal shift to high-carbon alternatives [cite: 17, 18, 19].
*   **Crowding Externalities:** When crowding is factored in, the value of comfort becomes a variable. Overcrowded large vehicles may deter usage. Optimization models that account for crowding externalities and stochastic travel times suggest that automated bus systems, for instance, might require higher frequencies (and potentially smaller or medium-sized vehicles) to optimize the balance between operator costs, user costs, and emissions [cite: 20].

### 3.2. Fleet Composition and Technology
Carbon emission distributions also dictate the technological mix of the fleet.
*   **Heterogeneous Fleets:** Research suggests that a mixed fleet (different vehicle sizes) often outperforms a homogeneous fleet. A heterogeneous fleet allows operators to match vehicle capacity to fluctuating demand (peak vs. off-peak), thereby maintaining high LCFs and minimizing the movement of empty seats/space [cite: 16].
*   **Electric Fleet Transition:** The transition to electric fleets is a capacity problem constrained by charging infrastructure and range. Stochastic models indicate that the volatility of investment costs (e.g., battery prices) has a larger impact on optimal capacity expansion than production costs. Furthermore, the "greenness" of the capacity is contingent on the energy source; in coal-dependent regions, electrification may increase the carbon footprint, altering the optimal deployment strategy [cite: 15, 21].

## 4. Impact on Network Design: Hub-and-Spoke vs. Point-to-Point

The structural design of the transportation network—how nodes are connected—is fundamentally influenced by the need to minimize carbon emissions. The two dominant paradigms, Hub-and-Spoke (H&S) and Point-to-Point (P2P), offer different carbon advantages.

### 4.1. The Case for Hub-and-Spoke (H&S)
*   **Economies of Scale:** H&S networks consolidate flows through a central node, allowing the use of larger, more fuel-efficient aircraft or vessels. Studies have shown that H&S networks can reduce total pollutant emissions (CO, HC, NOx) and fuel consumption compared to P2P networks due to these economies of scale and higher load factors [cite: 3, 22].
*   **Emission Efficiency:** By aggregating demand, H&S minimizes the number of empty or partially empty trips. Ideally, this results in lower emissions per passenger-km, provided the detour distance to the hub does not outweigh the efficiency gains of the larger vehicle [cite: 23, 24].

### 4.2. The Case for Point-to-Point (P2P)
*   **Distance Reduction:** P2P networks connect origins and destinations directly, eliminating the extra distance (detour) required to travel through a hub. Some research argues that P2P networks have a lower environmental impact because they avoid the "wasted" mileage of connecting flights and reduce congestion at hubs, which causes idling and holding pattern emissions [cite: 3, 25].
*   **Resilience and Delays:** P2P networks are less prone to the "domino effect" of delays that plague hubs. Since delays and congestion significantly increase fuel consumption (and thus emissions), the operational resilience of P2P can translate into carbon benefits [cite: 25, 26].

### 4.3. Synthesis of Network Impacts
The "optimal" configuration is likely a hybrid or context-dependent solution.
*   **Distance vs. Efficiency:** If the P2P route requires a small, inefficient regional jet, the H&S route on a modern turboprop or narrow-body might be cleaner despite the longer distance. Conversely, for high-demand routes where large aircraft can be used directly, P2P is superior [cite: 22, 27].
*   **Network Classification:** Airline networks are often classified by their degree distribution. Hub networks have a convex degree distribution (few nodes with very high connectivity), while P2P networks have a concave distribution. Understanding these topological properties helps in modeling their respective carbon resilience and efficiency profiles [cite: 26, 27].

## 5. Integrated Logistics and Reverse Capacity

Optimizing capacity is not just about moving goods forward but also about managing the return journey. "Empty running"—vehicles returning without a load—is a major source of inefficient carbon emissions.

### 5.1. Circular Economy and Reverse Logistics
*   **Network Integration:** Integrating forward distribution networks with reverse waste collection networks can significantly reduce emissions. By utilizing the empty return capacity of delivery vehicles to collect waste or recyclable materials, companies can reduce the total distance traveled and the number of vehicles required.
*   **Empirical Evidence:** A case study combining distribution and waste collection networks demonstrated a reduction in CO2 emissions by 26% to 34%, alongside cost reductions. This "continuous cycle" approach optimizes the use of fleet capacity that would otherwise be wasted [cite: 28, 29].

### 5.2. Collaborative Logistics
*   **Shared Capacity:** Similar to the integration of waste and distribution, collaborative logistics (e.g., carpooling, shared freight capacity) increases the LCF. The distribution of emissions per unit drops dramatically when capacity is shared, making "sharing" a key variable in optimal capacity configuration [cite: 1, 4].

## 6. Policy Instruments: Taxes vs. Constraints

The method by which carbon is regulated—price (tax) vs. quantity (constraint)—fundamentally alters how operators configure capacity.

### 6.1. Carbon Taxes (Price Mechanisms)
*   **Limited Structural Impact:** Research indicates that carbon taxes, unless set at extremely high levels, often fail to induce structural changes in transport mode selection or capacity configuration. Operators tend to absorb the tax as a cost of doing business rather than overhauling their fleets or networks. Taxes provide price certainty but do not guarantee a specific reduction in emission quantity [cite: 30].
*   **Operational Adjustments:** While taxes might not force a switch from Air to Rail, they may incentivize operational tweaks like "slow steaming" (reducing speed to save fuel) or minor fleet optimizations. However, they are less effective at driving the adoption of radically new technologies or capacity models in the short term [cite: 21, 31].

### 6.2. Emission Constraints (Cap-and-Trade / Hard Caps)
*   **Driving Structural Change:** Hard constraints on emissions (caps) are identified as a "more powerful tool" for inducing significant changes in transport mode selection and capacity configuration. If a firm faces a hard limit on total emissions, it is forced to optimize capacity (e.g., switch to rail, invest in EVs, redesign networks) to stay within the cap [cite: 30, 32].
*   **Capacity Expansion:** Under cap-and-trade regulations, the optimal capacity expansion strategy is heavily influenced by the volatility of carbon prices and the availability of allowances. Firms may invest in "greener" capacity (even if more expensive) to hedge against the risk of future allowance scarcity [cite: 21, 33].

## 7. Stochasticity and Uncertainty in Capacity Planning

Carbon emissions are not deterministic; they vary with weather, traffic, vehicle age, and driving style. Optimal capacity configuration must account for this uncertainty.

### 7.1. Parameter Uncertainty
*   **Sensitivity Analysis:** Emission estimates are highly sensitive to parameters like trip length, speed, and temperature. For instance, variations in these inputs can alter emission estimates for pollutants like CO and VOCs by -58% to +76%. Capacity planning based on deterministic averages may therefore lead to suboptimal or non-compliant outcomes [cite: 34].
*   **Stochastic Optimization:** Models that incorporate uncertainty (e.g., stochastic programming) show that the volatility of capacity investment costs has a larger impact on expansion decisions than production costs. Risk-averse planning objectives (e.g., minimizing the worst-case cost/emission scenario) tend to produce more diverse generation and fleet portfolios compared to risk-neutral approaches [cite: 21, 35].

### 7.2. Long-Term Uncertainty
*   **Future Scenarios:** Predicting fleet emissions decades into the future involves high uncertainty regarding technology adoption rates (e.g., EV penetration) and grid decarbonization. Stochastic assessments of the US vehicle fleet, for example, show wide probability distributions for future fuel use and emissions, suggesting that capacity policies must be robust to a range of future realities rather than optimized for a single projection [cite: 36, 37].

## 8. Conclusion

The impact of carbon emission distribution on optimal capacity configuration is profound and multifaceted. It forces a departure from simple cost-minimization models toward complex, multi-objective frameworks that balance service quality, operational efficiency, and environmental constraints.

**Summary of Impacts:**
1.  **Fleet Sizing:** Carbon constraints often favor **heterogeneous fleets** and may counter-intuitively support **larger fleets of smaller vehicles** to prevent modal shifts to private cars, despite the unit-efficiency of large vehicles.
2.  **Network Design:** There is a tension between the **consolidation benefits of Hub-and-Spoke** (high LCF, efficient aircraft) and the **directness of Point-to-Point** (lower distance, resilience). The optimal choice depends on specific route distances and demand densities.
3.  **Policy Sensitivity:** **Emission caps** are superior to taxes for forcing structural capacity changes.
4.  **Integration:** **Reverse logistics** and **shared capacity** are essential strategies for maximizing LCF and minimizing empty running emissions.

Ultimately, optimal capacity configuration in a carbon-constrained world is not about simply buying "greener" vehicles; it is about designing resilient, integrated systems that maximize load factors and minimize waste across the entire lifecycle of the transport network.
