# Semiotic Architectures of Affect: Visual Metaphor Systems and Emotional Resonance in Game Art

**Key Points**
*   **Peircean Semiotics in Game Art:** Game visuals rely heavily on Charles Sanders Peirce’s triad of signs—**icons** (resemblance), **indexes** (causal connections), and **symbols** (cultural conventions)—to communicate mechanics and narrative simultaneously.
*   **Visual Metaphor as Procedural Rhetoric:** Visual metaphors in games are not merely aesthetic; they are functional. For example, the "rot" in *Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice* acts as a visual metaphor for psychosis and stigma, creating anxiety through a bluffing mechanic that threatens permadeath.
*   **Architectural Semiotics:** Environments in games like *Control* utilize Brutalist architecture not just for setting, but as a semiotic system representing bureaucracy, order, and the oppression of the "Oldest House," contrasting with the chaotic visual language of the Hiss.
*   **Chromatic and Atmospheric Signifiers:** Games like *Gris* and *Journey* utilize color progression and lighting shifts as primary narrative devices, mapping emotional arcs (such as the five stages of grief) directly onto the visual spectrum to guide player affect without dialogue.
*   **Affordance Theory and Visual Cues:** The relationship between visual art and player agency is governed by affordances (perceived action possibilities). Effective game art aligns **perceived affordances** (what looks interactable) with **real affordances** (what is coded to be interactable) to maintain immersion and flow.

## 1. Introduction: The Semiotic Bridge Between Art and Affect

The construction of meaning in video games is a complex interplay between ludic systems (mechanics) and aesthetic representations (visuals/audio). Unlike static media, game art must fulfill a dual function: it must be evocative, contributing to the narrative atmosphere, and it must be functional, communicating the rules of the simulation to the player. This report explores how visual metaphor systems in game art construct semiotic frameworks that facilitate emotional resonance. By leveraging the semiotic theories of Charles Sanders Peirce, Ferdinand de Saussure, and contemporary game scholars, we examine how visual elements—from character design and environmental architecture to color palettes and user interface (UI) elements—act as signifiers that bridge the gap between the digital abstraction of code and the visceral emotional experience of the player.

The analysis posits that emotional resonance in games is often achieved when visual metaphors transcend mere decoration and become embedded in the gameplay loop itself—a concept closely related to Ian Bogost’s "procedural rhetoric" [cite: 1]. When the visual representation of a game element (e.g., a crumbling statue in *Gris* or a darkening arm in *Hellblade*) aligns perfectly with the mechanical constraints and narrative themes, it creates a "semiotic loop" [cite: 2] that deepens immersion. Conversely, when these visual systems fail to align with player agency, they result in ludonarrative dissonance, breaking the emotional bond. Through detailed case studies of titles such as *Journey*, *Gris*, *Control*, and *Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice*, this report delineates the mechanisms by which game art transforms pixels into pathos.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of Game Semiotics

To understand how visual metaphors function in games, one must first establish the semiotic frameworks that govern how players interpret visual data. The application of semiotics to game design, often termed "Gameotics" [cite: 3], provides a structured methodology for analyzing audio-visual interaction.

### 2.1 The Peircean Triad in Ludic Spaces
Charles Sanders Peirce’s classification of signs is foundational to understanding game art. Peirce categorized signs into three types based on their relationship to the object they represent: **Icon**, **Index**, and **Symbol** [cite: 3, 4, 5, 6].

*   **Icons (Firstness):** Icons bear a physical resemblance to the object they represent. In game art, this is the most direct form of representation. A digital 3D model of a gun is an icon of a gun; a character avatar is an icon of a person. In *SimCity 2000*, the buildings are icons because they visually resemble the real-world structures they simulate [cite: 3]. Icons operate on the level of "Firstness" or quality/feeling, establishing the immediate visual reality of the game world.
*   **Indexes (Secondness):** Indexes have a direct, causal, or physical connection to their object. In the physical world, smoke is an index of fire. In game design, indexes are crucial for feedback. A health bar lowering is an index of damage received; a footprint in the snow is an index of a character's recent presence. In *Journey*, the length of the player's scarf is an index of their collection of symbols and, by extension, their experience level and flight capability [cite: 7]. Indexes operate on the level of "Secondness," representing reaction and causality.
*   **Symbols (Thirdness):** Symbols rely on social convention or arbitrary association to convey meaning. There is no physical resemblance or causal link; the meaning must be learned. In games, a red barrel signifying "explosive" is a learned symbol specific to the gaming literacy [cite: 8]. The faction logos in *Control* or the specific rune shapes in *Hellblade* function as symbols that carry narrative weight through convention. Symbols operate on the level of "Thirdness," representing law, rule, and habit.

### 2.2 The Interpretant and the Player
Peirce’s model also includes the **Interpretant**—the mental effect or concept produced by the sign in the mind of the observer [cite: 4, 9]. In video games, the player is the active generator of the interpretant. Unlike a film viewer who interprets a fixed frame, a game player interprets a sign and then *acts* upon it. This creates a cycle of semiosis where the player’s action generates new signs (e.g., the player sees a ledge [sign], interprets it as climbable [interpretant], climbs it, and the camera shifts [new sign]).

This interaction is described as a "semiotic loop" of signs, signals, cues, and reactions [cite: 2]. The emotional resonance occurs when this loop flows without friction (immersion) or when it is disrupted in a meaningful way (tension). For instance, in *Hellblade*, the visual hallucinations are signs that the player must interpret, but because they are unreliable (false indexes), they generate feelings of anxiety and distrust, mirroring the protagonist's psychosis [cite: 10].

### 2.3 Greimas and the Actant Model
Beyond Peirce, the structuralist approach of Algirdas Greimas is also applied to game analysis, particularly in how visual characters function as "actants" (narrative units defined by action) [cite: 1]. In visual metaphor systems, an object or environmental feature can become an actant. In *Gris*, the environment itself shifts from an opponent (red winds of anger) to a helper (green vines of bargaining), visually transforming the setting into an active participant in the narrative of grief [cite: 11, 12].

## 3. Visual Metaphor as Procedural Rhetoric

Visual metaphors in games differ from those in static art because they are tied to **procedural rhetoric**—the art of persuasion through rule-based representations [cite: 1, 13]. A visual metaphor in a game is not just an image; it is a system.

### 3.1 The Metaphor-Simulation Paradox
Scholars have noted a "metaphor-simulation paradox" in game studies, where the distinction between a simulation (a model of a system) and a metaphor (a symbolic representation) blurs [cite: 14, 15]. However, in "art games" or "empathy games," the simulation often *is* the metaphor.
*   **Internal Metaphors:** These refer to the game system itself (e.g., floating hearts representing health).
*   **External Metaphors:** These integrate symbolic meaning that transcends the game (e.g., the mechanics of *Passage*, where moving forward visually correlates with aging and the narrowing of possibilities) [cite: 13].

### 3.2 Case Study: *Gris* and the Chromatic Metaphor of Grief
*Gris* (Nomada Studio) is a quintessential example of a game where the visual metaphor system constitutes the entire narrative framework. The game explores the five stages of grief (Kübler-Ross model) through a strictly visual and mechanical progression, devoid of dialogue [cite: 11, 16, 17].

*   **Denial (Grey/Monochrome):** The game begins in a colorless, shattered world. The protagonist, Gris, has lost her voice (a mechanic removed). The visual emptiness signifies the numbness of denial. The environment is static and fragmented, representing a broken psyche [cite: 11, 18].
*   **Anger (Red):** As the player progresses, the color red is introduced. This is not just a palette swap; it changes the environment's behavior. Red manifests as violent sandstorms and aggressive winds that push the player back. The visual metaphor (Red = Anger) becomes a mechanical obstacle (Wind = Resistance), forcing the player to "brace" against the emotion [cite: 11, 12].
*   **Bargaining (Green):** The introduction of green brings flora and fauna. The mechanics shift to interaction and cooperation with forest creatures. The visual metaphor of growth represents the attempt to negotiate a return to normalcy or life [cite: 17].
*   **Depression (Blue):** The world floods, turning blue. The mechanics shift to swimming and sinking. The visual density of the water and the sluggish movement affordances simulate the crushing weight of depression. The player must dive deep into the darkness to find the light, a spatial metaphor for introspection [cite: 11].
*   **Acceptance (Yellow):** The final stage introduces warm, golden light. The mechanics allow for singing (voice restored) and flight/floating. The visual system unifies all previous colors, representing emotional integration. The shattered statue of the mother figure is reassembled, not perfectly, but functionally, symbolizing healing [cite: 16, 19].

In *Gris*, the semiotic framework is "synesthetic"—color is not just seen; it is felt through the mechanics it enables or restricts. The emotional resonance arises because the player physically enacts the struggle of each stage through the controller, guided by the visual cues.

## 4. The Semiotics of Space and Architecture

Game spaces are not passive backdrops; they are "spatial indexes" that communicate narrative and history [cite: 20, 21]. Virtual architecture functions as a central signifier, influencing gameplay and emotional tone.

### 4.1 Brutalism and Bureaucracy in *Control*
Remedy Entertainment’s *Control* utilizes Brutalist architecture as a primary semiotic system to convey themes of bureaucracy, secrecy, and cosmic horror [cite: 22, 23, 24].
*   **The Oldest House as Signifier:** The setting, "The Oldest House," is a shifting, concrete skyscraper that defies Euclidean geometry. The choice of Brutalism (raw concrete, massive geometric forms, lack of ornamentation) serves as a visual metaphor for the Federal Bureau of Control (FBC). It signifies permanence, authority, coldness, and utilitarianism [cite: 25, 26].
*   **Concrete as Tabula Rasa:** The grey concrete walls act as a canvas for the "Hiss"—an interdimensional resonance that corrupts the space. The Hiss is represented by a chaotic red light and floating bodies, creating a violent visual contrast with the rigid, grey order of the Brutalist architecture. This visual conflict (Grey Order vs. Red Chaos) creates immediate semiotic tension [cite: 24].
*   **Ritualistic Geometry:** The game uses repetitive geometric shapes and symmetrical one-point perspective to create a sense of unease. This "ritualistic" architecture implies that the bureaucracy itself is a form of occult ritual. The visual repetition becomes a symbol of the infinite loop of administrative red tape [cite: 24].
*   **Lighting and Typography:** The use of massive, sans-serif typography floating in 3D space (diegetic UI) integrates the bureaucratic labeling directly into the architecture. The building labels itself, reinforcing the semiotic dominance of the Bureau over the individual [cite: 26].

### 4.2 Non-Euclidean Semiotics in *Manifold Garden*
In *Manifold Garden*, the architecture repeats infinitely in all directions (toroidal space). Gravity is a manipulable mechanic. The visual metaphor here is one of "infinity" and "perspective." The architecture uses familiar signs (stairs, windows) but strips them of their usual affordances (stairs can be walked on from any angle). This forces the player to abandon their "cartesian" understanding of space, creating a sense of awe and intellectual vertigo [cite: 20, 21]. The visual system constructs a framework where "down" is a relative concept, metaphorically suggesting that perspective defines reality [cite: 27].

## 5. The Avatar as Semiotic Vessel and the Index of Agency

The player's avatar is the primary point of contact with the game world. The visual evolution of the avatar often serves as an index of the player's journey, agency, or emotional state.

### 5.1 The "Rot" in *Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice*
*Hellblade* presents a profound case of using visual semiotics to represent mental illness and generate player anxiety.
*   **The Visual Signifier:** Early in the game, Senua’s arm is infected with a dark "rot." The game tells the player (via text/voice) that if the rot reaches her head, the game will end and all progress will be erased (permadeath) [cite: 28, 29].
*   **The Semiotic Bluff:** This visual index (the growing rot) acts as a "sanity meter." However, it is a lie. The game does not actually delete save data upon repeated deaths; the rot grows at scripted narrative moments regardless of player performance [cite: 29, 30].
*   **Emotional Resonance:** By creating a *false index* of failure, the developers induce a genuine fear of death and anxiety in the player. This aligns the player's emotional state with Senua’s psychosis—she believes she is cursed and dying, and the player is tricked into believing it too. The visual metaphor (Rot = Failure/Death) successfully bridges the gap between the avatar’s delusion and the player’s reality [cite: 31].
*   **Body as Text:** Senua’s body becomes a text of her trauma. The rot, the mud, the blood, and the scars are visual symbols of her internal battle. The camera stays close (over-the-shoulder), restricting the field of view and forcing an intimate, claustrophobic perspective that enhances the emotional weight of these visual signs [cite: 32].

### 5.2 The Scarf in *Journey*
In *Journey*, the protagonist is a robed figure with no face, arms, or dialogue. The primary visual index of power and experience is the scarf.
*   **Scarf Length as Index:** The scarf grows longer as the player collects glowing symbols. A longer scarf allows for longer flight. Thus, the visual length of the scarf is a direct index of player agency and exploration [cite: 7, 33].
*   **Visual Flow and Connection:** The movement of the scarf (fluid dynamics) visually rhymes with the sand and the wind, creating a metaphor of "flow." The scarf also serves as a connection point between two players (co-op). Players can recharge each other's scarves by touching, a mechanic that creates a visual and mechanical bond. The visual act of huddling together to maintain scarf energy in the freezing snow level creates a powerful emotional resonance of reliance and intimacy without words [cite: 9, 34].
*   **Loss and Rebirth:** When the scarf is torn by enemies or fades in the cold, the player feels a tangible loss of agency. The visual shrinking of the scarf correlates with mechanical disempowerment, making the final sequence (where the scarf becomes infinitely long and the player ascends) a moment of euphoric release [cite: 7, 35].

## 6. Affordance Theory and Visual Signifiers

The concept of **affordance**, originating with James J. Gibson and adapted to design by Don Norman, is critical in game art. An affordance is a property of an object that indicates how it can be used [cite: 36, 37]. In games, visual metaphors must function as "perceived affordances."

### 6.1 Visual Cues and Interaction
Game art uses a specific visual vocabulary to signal affordances:
*   **The "Yellow Paint" Convention:** Many modern games (e.g., *Resident Evil 4 Remake*, *God of War*) use bright yellow paint to mark climbable ledges or breakable boxes. This is a "signifier" that highlights the affordance. While effective for usability, it is often criticized for breaking immersion (ludonarrative dissonance) because it is a non-diegetic element plastered over a realistic environment [cite: 21].
*   **Lighting as Guidance:** In *Journey* and *Inside*, lighting is used more subtly. A spotlight or a glowing object draws the eye, acting as an indexical sign pointing toward the path forward [cite: 35]. In *Bloodborne*, the lanterns are visual beacons of safety in a dark world. Their blue/purple hue contrasts with the dark, muddy tones of the environment, signaling a "safe haven" and a connection to the Hunter's Dream [cite: 38, 39].
*   **Color-Coded Affordances:** In *Mirror's Edge*, objects that can be used for parkour (ramps, pipes, doors) turn bright red as the player approaches (Runner Vision). This visual metaphor represents the protagonist's "flow state" and focus. The color red shifts from a symbol of danger (in other games) to a symbol of opportunity and speed [cite: 21].

### 6.2 False Affordances and Subversion
Sometimes, game art intentionally subverts affordances to create emotional effects.
*   **Invisible Walls:** A path that looks open but is blocked by an invisible collider is a "false affordance." This breaks the semiotic contract and causes frustration.
*   **Subversive Horror:** In *P.T.* or *Layers of Fear*, the architecture changes behind the player’s back. A door that was there a moment ago disappears. This manipulation of spatial affordances creates disorientation and fear, using the player's reliance on object permanence against them [cite: 40].

## 7. Ludonarrative Dissonance and Visual Harmony

When the visual metaphor system contradicts the game mechanics, it results in **ludonarrative dissonance** [cite: 41, 42]. This term, coined by Clint Hocking, describes the conflict between the narrative (told through cutscenes/visuals) and the ludic (told through gameplay).

### 7.1 Visual Dissonance
*   **The "Cutscene Power" Problem:** In *Xenosaga* or *Batman: Arkham Knight*, characters may display god-like powers in cinematic visuals (flying, destroying armies) but are restricted to limited, vulnerable movements during gameplay. This visual mismatch weakens the player's belief in the avatar's power [cite: 43, 44].
*   **The "Looting Hero":** In *Uncharted*, the protagonist is visually presented as a charming, everyday explorer (Icon), but the gameplay requires him to kill hundreds of enemies (Index of violence). The visual presentation of the character does not match the indexical reality of the body count, creating a dissonance that some players find jarring [cite: 41, 43].

### 7.2 Achieving Harmony (Ludonarrative Resonance)
Games like *Shadow of the Colossus* and *Journey* achieve resonance because their visual metaphors align with mechanics.
*   **Shadow of the Colossus:** The colossi are majestic, sad creatures. As the player kills them, the visual feedback is not triumphant; the music is somber, and the protagonist's appearance physically degrades (skin becomes pale, veins darken). This visual corruption serves as an index of the moral cost of the player's actions, creating harmony between the act of killing and the narrative of tragedy [cite: 45].
*   **Inside:** The boy is small, faceless, and fragile (Icon). The deaths are brutal and visceral (Index). The mechanics involve hiding and running. The visual vulnerability perfectly matches the mechanical disempowerment, creating a coherent semiotic system of fear and oppression [cite: 46].

## 8. Conclusion

Visual metaphor systems in game art are not merely decorative; they are the foundational semiotic infrastructure upon which player experience is built. By utilizing Peircean signs—transforming icons into avatars, indexes into feedback systems, and symbols into narrative lore—game artists construct "storyworlds" that players inhabit and decode.

The emotional resonance of a game depends on the integrity of these semiotic frameworks. When the visual metaphor (e.g., the "rot" in *Hellblade*) aligns with the procedural rhetoric (the fear of permadeath), the player enters a state of deep immersion where the boundary between the digital signifier and the emotional signified dissolves. Conversely, when visual cues promise affordances that mechanics deny, or when the aesthetic tone clashes with ludic actions, the semiotic loop is broken.

Ultimately, the most powerful game art operates as a dynamic language. It does not just show the player a world; it teaches the player how to feel about that world through the grammar of color, light, architecture, and form. From the shifting watercolors of *Gris* to the brutalist concrete of *Control*, these visual systems prove that in the medium of video games, seeing is not just believing—seeing is playing, and playing is feeling.
