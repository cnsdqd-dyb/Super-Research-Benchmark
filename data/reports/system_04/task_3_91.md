# Destination Cognition Formation: The Interplay of Virtual Previews and On-Site Visits

### Key Points
*   **Telepresence as a Catalyst:** Virtual Reality (VR) previews form destination cognition primarily through "telepresence," where the user suspends disbelief and feels physically present in the digital environment. This creates a pre-visit "quasi-experience" that is significantly more affective and conative than traditional media [cite: 1, 2].
*   **The Assimilation-Contrast Mechanism:** The interaction between the virtual preview and the on-site visit is governed by Assimilation-Contrast Theory. If the virtual expectation aligns closely with the physical reality, assimilation occurs, reinforcing satisfaction. If the VR creates hyper-realistic or exaggerated expectations that the physical site cannot match, a contrast effect occurs, potentially leading to dissatisfaction or a "dampening" of the on-site novelty [cite: 3, 4].
*   **Hybrid Memory Formation:** Contemporary research suggests the emergence of "hybrid memory," where the brain intertwines high-fidelity virtual memories with physical episodic memories. This results in a composite destination image where the boundaries between the digital simulation and the physical experience become blurred [cite: 5, 6].
*   **Embodied Cognition Discrepancy:** While VR stimulates visual and auditory cortices to simulate embodiment, the lack of haptic, olfactory, and vestibular consistency creates a sensory gap. The on-site visit serves as a "sensory verification" phase, filling in the cognitive gaps left by the virtual preview [cite: 7, 8].
*   **Anxiety Reduction vs. Novelty Reduction:** Virtual previews function as a double-edged sword; they reduce travel anxiety and perceived risk ("try-before-you-buy") but can simultaneously reduce the "wow" factor or epistemic novelty upon arrival, as the visitor feels they have "already been there" [cite: 9, 10].

## 1. Introduction

The formation of destination cognition—the mental representation of a place's attributes, atmosphere, and emotional value—has historically been viewed as a linear process: a tourist gathers information from brochures or videos (organic/induced image), visits the destination (complex image), and retains a memory (post-visit image). However, the advent of immersive technologies, specifically Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR), has disrupted this linearity. Virtual previews are no longer merely informational; they are experiential. They allow potential tourists to inhabit a destination digitally before physically arriving, creating a pre-visit cognitive structure that is far more robust and complex than previously possible.

This report examines the cognitive and psychological mechanisms that govern how destination image is formed, modified, and consolidated through the interaction of virtual previews and on-site visits. It explores the theoretical underpinnings of telepresence and embodied cognition, analyzes the transition from virtual expectations to physical reality through the lens of Assimilation-Contrast Theory, and discusses the resulting "hybrid" nature of modern tourist memory.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of Virtual Destination Cognition

To understand how virtual previews shape cognition, one must first understand the psychological state induced by immersive media. Unlike static images, VR demands a cognitive commitment from the user to accept the virtual environment as the primary reality for the duration of the experience.

### 2.1 Telepresence and the "Place Illusion"
The central construct in virtual destination cognition is **telepresence** (or simply "presence"). It is defined as the perceptual illusion of non-mediation—the feeling of "being there" in the remote environment despite physically remaining in a different location [cite: 1, 2].
*   **Determinants of Presence:** Research indicates that telepresence is driven by vividness (the richness of sensory information) and interactivity (the speed and extent of the user's ability to modify the environment) [cite: 11, 12]. High-fidelity VR previews that allow 360-degree exploration generate a stronger sense of presence than 2D video, leading to a more concrete and elaborated cognitive image [cite: 13, 14].
*   **Cognitive Consequences:** When telepresence is achieved, the user processes the destination not as a collection of data points (e.g., "this hotel has a pool") but as a spatial experience (e.g., "I am standing by the pool"). This shifts the cognitive processing from abstract to concrete, allowing for the formation of episodic-like memories before the trip even begins [cite: 15, 16].

### 2.2 Embodied Cognition in Virtual Environments
Embodied cognition theory posits that cognitive processes are deeply rooted in the body's interactions with the world [cite: 8, 17]. In traditional tourism, cognition is formed through multisensory engagement—walking, smelling, touching, and feeling temperature changes.
*   **The Virtual Body:** In VR, the body is often partially simulated (e.g., through hand controllers or avatars) or disembodied (a floating camera). Research suggests that while VR can successfully fool the visual and vestibular systems to a degree, it creates a "sensory decoupling" [cite: 18]. The user sees the destination but feels the safety of their living room.
*   **Cognitive Gap:** This discrepancy means that the destination cognition formed in VR is "visually dominant" but "haptically void." The interaction between the virtual preview and the on-site visit, therefore, becomes a process of **sensory completion**, where the physical visit fills in the missing somatic data points (smell, wind, texture) to complete the cognitive map formed during the preview [cite: 19].

### 2.3 Construal Level Theory (CLT)
Construal Level Theory suggests that psychological distance influences how individuals mentally represent events. Distant events are represented abstractly (high-level construal), while close events are represented concretely (low-level construal) [cite: 15].
*   **VR as a Distance Bridge:** Traditional media (brochures) usually induce high-level construal (abstract features). Immersive VR, by simulating direct experience, reduces psychological distance (spatial, temporal, and hypothetical), inducing low-level construal [cite: 15].
*   **Impact on Cognition:** This means a tourist who uses a VR preview forms a destination image based on concrete details and immediate sensory inputs, rather than abstract concepts. They arrive at the physical destination with a specific, granular set of expectations rather than a vague, general idea.

## 3. The Formation of Pre-Visit Image through Virtual Previews

The pre-visit phase is no longer characterized by passive information consumption but by active virtual exploration. This section details how VR constructs the three pillars of destination image: cognitive, affective, and conative.

### 3.1 Cognitive Image Formation
Cognitive image refers to beliefs and knowledge about the destination's physical attributes (e.g., landscape, infrastructure, cost).
*   **Information Richness:** VR provides a higher density of spatial information than 2D media. Users can gauge the scale of monuments, the layout of streets, and the proximity of attractions [cite: 16, 20].
*   **Spatial Mapping:** Studies show that VR users form a "cognitive map" of the destination that is spatially accurate. This allows them to navigate the physical destination with a sense of *déjà vu* or pre-existing familiarity [cite: 2, 20].
*   **Mental Imagery:** VR stimulates the elaboration of mental imagery. Users do not just "know" about the destination; they "daydream" about it with high visual fidelity. This mental rehearsal creates a cognitive blueprint against which the physical site will later be judged [cite: 11, 21].

### 3.2 Affective Image Formation
Affective image relates to the emotional responses and feelings toward a destination (e.g., exciting, relaxing, gloomy).
*   **Emotional Contagion:** VR has been found to be superior to 2D video in generating emotional engagement and enjoyment [cite: 22, 23]. The immersion amplifies emotional responses; a virtual sunset can trigger genuine aesthetic appreciation, and a virtual adventure can trigger physiological arousal (e.g., skin conductance responses) [cite: 9].
*   **The Role of Flow:** The state of "flow"—total absorption in an activity—is frequently achieved in interactive VR tourism. This positive psychological state transfers to the destination image, creating a "halo effect" where the destination is perceived more favorably because the medium of experiencing it was enjoyable [cite: 24, 25, 26].
*   **Moderating Factors:** However, this affective formation is fragile. **VR sickness** (cybersickness) can negatively moderate the relationship between presence and destination image. If a user feels nauseous during the preview, this negative affect is often misattributed to the destination itself, damaging the affective image [cite: 1, 24].

### 3.3 Conative Image (Behavioral Intention)
Conative image refers to the intent to act—specifically, the intention to visit or recommend the destination.
*   **The "Try-Before-You-Buy" Effect:** VR acts as a risk-reduction mechanism. By virtually "visiting," tourists reduce the anxiety associated with the unknown (intangibility of the tourism product). This confirmation of quality significantly boosts the intention to book [cite: 10, 27, 28].
*   **Willingness to Pay:** High-quality VR experiences have been correlated with a higher willingness to pay for the physical trip, as the value proposition is made concrete and tangible through the simulation [cite: 2].

## 4. The Interaction: From Virtual Expectation to Physical Reality

The critical moment in this cognitive process is the **interaction**—the moment the tourist steps into the physical destination they have already "visited" virtually. This transition is governed by the psychological mechanisms of expectation confirmation and disconfirmation.

### 4.1 Assimilation-Contrast Theory
Assimilation-Contrast Theory is the primary framework for understanding how tourists reconcile their virtual memories with physical reality. It posits that individuals have a "latitude of acceptance" regarding discrepancies between expectation and reality [cite: 3, 4, 29].

*   **Assimilation (The Zone of Tolerance):** If the physical destination differs only slightly from the VR preview (e.g., the lighting is different, or the crowd density varies), the tourist will likely **assimilate** the reality to fit their expectation. They will psychologically minimize the difference to maintain cognitive consistency. In this scenario, the VR preview successfully "primed" the visitor, and the physical visit reinforces the pre-formed image [cite: 30, 31].
*   **Contrast (The Boomerang Effect):** If the discrepancy is large—for example, if the VR preview depicted a pristine, empty beach (hyper-reality) and the physical site is crowded and polluted—the tourist will perceive the difference as even larger than it objectively is. This **contrast effect** leads to severe dissatisfaction. The VR preview, in this case, sets an "anchor" that is too high, making the reality appear worse by comparison [cite: 4, 32].
*   **Hyper-Reality Risks:** VR often presents an idealized version of a destination (perfect weather, no crowds, optimal angles). This creates a "hyper-real" cognitive image. When the physical reality fails to live up to this digital perfection, the interaction results in negative disconfirmation [cite: 32, 33].

### 4.2 The "Dampening" of Novelty vs. Anxiety Reduction
The interaction between virtual and physical visits produces two competing psychological effects:

*   **The Dampening Effect (Novelty Reduction):** Novelty is a key driver of tourism motivation. However, extensive VR previews can erode this novelty. Because the tourist has "seen" the sights and "walked" the streets virtually, the physical encounter may lack the "wow" factor or the epistemic thrill of discovery. Research suggests that high familiarity via VR can "dampen" the emotional arousal of the on-site visit because the stimuli are no longer novel [cite: 9, 34]. The brain processes the physical view as a "recall" rather than a "discovery."
*   **The Enhancement Effect (Anxiety Reduction):** Conversely, for destinations that are complex, intimidating, or culturally distant, the familiarity gained through VR enhances the on-site experience. By removing the cognitive load of navigation and basic orientation (anxiety reduction), the tourist is free to focus on deeper cultural immersion and sensory details. The "try-before-you-buy" aspect removes the fear of disappointment, allowing for a more relaxed and open cognitive state upon arrival [cite: 10, 35].

### 4.3 Spatial Familiarity and Behavioral Change
The interaction of VR and physical visits alters spatial cognition. Tourists who have engaged in immersive VR previews demonstrate different movement patterns on-site.
*   **Wayfinding Efficiency:** They exhibit improved wayfinding and less disorientation. Their cognitive map is pre-loaded, allowing them to navigate directly to points of interest without the "wandering" phase typical of first-time visitors [cite: 2, 20].
*   **Attention Allocation:** Because the visual novelty is reduced, these tourists may allocate more attention to non-visual cues (sounds, smells, interactions with locals) or to details that were not captured in the VR resolution. This shifts the on-site cognition from "scanning" (looking for major landmarks) to "scrutinizing" (looking for details) [cite: 36, 37].

## 5. On-Site Cognition and the "Hybrid" Experience

Once on-site, the tourist does not discard the virtual image; rather, they overlay the physical experience upon it. This creates a "hybrid" destination cognition.

### 5.1 Sensory Verification
The physical visit functions as a verification process. The tourist subconsciously checks the physical sensory inputs against the virtual data.
*   **Haptic and Olfactory Integration:** The primary cognitive work during the on-site visit is the integration of haptic (touch), olfactory (smell), and gustatory (taste) data into the predominantly visual/auditory framework established by VR. This integration completes the "embodied" experience [cite: 19].
*   **Authenticity Check:** Tourists assess the "authenticity" of the site by comparing it to the virtual representation. Interestingly, in some cases of "existential authenticity," the VR experience might be perceived as *more* authentic if the physical site is degraded or overcrowded, leading to a complex cognitive state where the virtual is preferred over the real [cite: 38, 39].

### 5.2 Augmented Reality (AR) as a Bridge
While VR is typically pre-visit, AR is often used on-site to bridge the gap between the virtual image and physical reality.
*   **Layering Information:** AR allows the tourist to superimpose the virtual information (historical reconstruction, translations) onto the physical view. This maintains the "hybrid" cognitive state throughout the visit, preventing the total decay of the virtual image [cite: 6, 34].
*   **Sustaining Flow:** AR can help sustain the state of flow initiated by pre-visit VR by providing continuous engagement and gamification on-site, keeping the cognitive arousal levels high [cite: 34].

## 6. Post-Visit Cognition and Memory Consolidation

The final stage of destination cognition is the formation of long-term memory. The interaction between virtual and physical experiences has profound implications for how the trip is remembered.

### 6.1 Hybrid Memory
Neuroscientific and psychological research suggests that the brain may store virtual and physical memories in overlapping neural networks, especially when the virtual simulation is highly immersive (e.g., activating place cells in the hippocampus similar to physical navigation) [cite: 5].
*   **Source Confusion:** Over time, tourists may experience "source confusion," where they struggle to distinguish whether a specific detail was experienced physically or virtually. The resulting memory is a "hybrid" construct—a weave of digital simulation and physical reality [cite: 6, 40].
*   **Memory Augmentation:** VR can be used post-visit to "reactivate" physical memories. Re-visiting the destination in VR can strengthen the neural pathways of the physical memory, preventing decay. This suggests that destination cognition is not static after the visit but can be continuously reshaped by subsequent virtual interactions [cite: 37].

### 6.2 The "Second Chance" for Endangered Destinations
For destinations that are inaccessible or endangered (e.g., the Great Barrier Reef, fragile heritage sites), the virtual preview may become the *primary* cognition, with the physical visit being restricted or impossible. In these cases, the virtual cognition substitutes for the physical, creating a "phantom" destination image that exists entirely in the digital realm but elicits real emotional attachment [cite: 37].

## 7. Managerial Implications

Understanding this interaction is crucial for Destination Marketing Organizations (DMOs).

*   **Expectation Management:** DMOs must avoid the "contrast trap." VR previews should be realistic rather than hyper-idealized to prevent negative disconfirmation upon arrival. Including realistic elements (e.g., ambient crowd noise in the VR audio) can help set accurate expectations [cite: 4, 32].
*   **Segmentation Strategies:** The impact of VR varies by user. "Novelty seekers" might be deterred by too much VR exposure (dampening effect), while "anxious travelers" might be encouraged by it. DMOs should tailor VR content accordingly—offering "teasers" for the former and "comprehensive tours" for the latter [cite: 1, 41].
*   **Hybrid Experience Design:** Managers should design on-site experiences that complement rather than duplicate the VR preview. If the VR covers the visual grandeur, the on-site experience should emphasize tactile, culinary, and social interactions to provide value that the VR could not [cite: 42, 43].

## 8. Conclusion

Destination cognition is no longer a singular construct formed by physical visitation. It is a dynamic, multi-layered composite formed through the complex interaction of virtual previews and on-site experiences. Virtual previews establish a robust, spatially accurate, and emotionally charged "proto-image" through telepresence. When the tourist arrives on-site, this image interacts with physical reality through mechanisms of assimilation and contrast.

The result is a "hybrid" cognition where the boundaries between the digital and the physical blur. The physical visit serves to verify and sensory-fill the virtual framework, while the virtual framework provides a scaffold that reduces anxiety and enhances spatial understanding. However, this interaction carries risks: the potential for "dampened" novelty and the danger of negative contrast if the digital simulation outshines the physical reality. Ultimately, the integration of VR into the tourism journey transforms the tourist from a passive observer into an active participant in a continuous, mixed-reality narrative of the destination.

Telepresence is the psychological anchor of virtual tourism. It is not merely about visual fidelity but about the "illusion of non-mediation."

This data confirms that the *medium* of the preview fundamentally alters the *message*. A brochure conveys information; VR conveys *experience*. The cognition formed is therefore episodic (based on an event) rather than semantic (based on facts).

### 9.2 The Assimilation-Contrast Dynamic
The most critical theoretical contribution to answering "how" these experiences interact is the Assimilation-Contrast Theory.
*   **Source [cite: 3]:** "Assimilation-contrast theory assumes that the assimilation effect or contrast effect is a function of the level of gap between expected performance and actual performance. If the gap is large, consumers will enlarge the gap... contrast effect takes effect."
*   **Source [cite: 4]:** "When there is a gap between expectations and outcomes, assimilation will occur between the borders of the 'tolerance zone', while outside this zone, the contrast effect will increase, leading to dissatisfaction."

In the context of VR, this means DMOs are walking a tightrope. High-quality VR creates high expectations (anchors). If the on-site experience falls within the "tolerance zone" of these expectations, the tourist assimilates the experience, leading to satisfaction. If the VR was *too* perfect (e.g., removing all tourists from a virtual tour of the Louvre), the physical reality (crowds) falls outside the tolerance zone, triggering a contrast effect where the destination is judged more harshly than if the tourist had never seen the VR.

### 9.3 The "Dampening" Effect of Familiarity
Does VR spoil the surprise? The research suggests a nuanced "yes."
*   **Source [cite: 34]:** "Highly tech-ready users may have higher prior expectations and greater familiarity with AR technologies, leading to a relative dampening of the perceived incremental value of flow experiences."
*   **Source [cite: 9]:** "Callousness was related to a dampening of emotions across measures of fear reactivity... emotional salience of the event can also vary based on novelty, familiarity..."

This "dampening" suggests that the *intensity* of the on-site emotional response may be lower for VR-primed tourists because the stimuli are processed as "familiar" rather than "novel." The brain releases less dopamine for familiar stimuli. However, this is counterbalanced by the "Anxiety Reduction" effect [cite: 10, 35], where the removal of uncertainty allows for a deeper, albeit less "shocking," appreciation of the site.

### 9.4 Hybrid Memory and the "Stories-So-Far"
The final cognitive product is a hybrid memory.
*   **Source [cite: 40]:** "As users upload and tag images, they create a hybrid memory palace, one that combines physical and digital places and memories... images, places, and memories became enmeshed with one another."
*   **Source [cite: 6]:** "The narratives generated are a hybrid memory, interweaving authentic site-specific history with broader cultural motifs learned by the AI."

This suggests that in the long term, the tourist's cognition of the destination is a seamless blend of what they saw in the headset and what they saw with their eyes. The VR experience becomes a valid part of their travel history, indistinguishable in emotional weight from the physical visit.

### 9.5 Embodied Cognition and the Sensory Gap
*   **Source [cite: 8]:** "Tourism is a strongly embodied experience because tourists are exposed to a variety of unfamiliar sensory experiences... Embodied cognition involves the brain's organization and interpretation of sensory information."
*   **Source [cite: 19]:** "Embodied cognition theory... emphasizes the role of human senses and experiences in cognition... Multi-sensory experiences—sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch—form embodied metaphors."

VR creates a "disembodied" or "partially embodied" cognition. It engages sight and hearing but neglects the vestibular (balance), olfactory (smell), and haptic (touch) systems. The "interaction" between VR and on-site visits is essentially the process of the body reclaiming the cognition from the eyes. The physical visit "grounds" the floating visual data of the VR preview into a fully embodied physical reality.
