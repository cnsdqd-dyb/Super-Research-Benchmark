*   **Biometric Externalization:** Artists like Rafael Lozano-Hemmer employ biometric data (heartbeats, fingerprints) to create "externalized interiority," where the most intimate physiological functions of the participant become public architectural elements, creating a "memento mori" that is both individual and collective.

## 1. Introduction: The Sensory Turn in Digital Art

The integration of sensory interaction into digital installations has precipitated a profound ontological shift in the relationship between the artwork and the audience. Historically, the Western canon of art appreciation has been dominated by **oculocentrism**—the privileging of vision as the primary, if not exclusive, mode of aesthetic engagement [cite: 1]. In this traditional paradigm, the ontology of the artwork is that of a discrete, static object, and the ontology of the audience is that of a detached, observing subject. However, the advent of interactive digital media, particularly those employing haptics, proprioception, and biometrics, has collapsed this distance.

Contemporary digital installations do not merely show images to an audience; they read the audience. Through sensors, motion tracking, and biofeedback loops, the spectator’s physical presence—their heartbeat, breath, movement, and touch—becomes the raw material of the artwork itself [cite: 2, 3]. This report investigates how these sensory interactions construct a new ontology of audience participation. It argues that participation in this context is no longer a matter of social engagement or interpretation, but a fundamental structural necessity where the viewer and the work co-constitute one another in a "mixed reality" of flesh and code [cite: 1, 4].

We will examine the theoretical underpinnings of this shift through the lens of phenomenology and media theory, specifically the work of Mark Hansen and the concept of "bodies-in-code." We will analyze specific case studies, including the biometric architectures of Rafael Lozano-Hemmer and the immersive virtual environments of Char Davies, to illustrate how different sensory modalities (biometric vs. proprioceptive) construct different modes of being. Finally, we will critique the limits of this participation through the concepts of "strange ontologies" and the "illusion of agency," questioning whether these systems liberate the spectator or merely incorporate them into a logic of algorithmic control.

## 2. Theoretical Frameworks: Phenomenology and the Body-in-Code

To understand the ontology of audience participation in digital art, one must first establish how the digital mediates the physical body. The theoretical literature suggests that digital art has moved beyond the "disembodiment" often associated with early cyberspace theories (which viewed the virtual as a space of pure mind) toward a radical re-embodiment.

### 2.1 Mark Hansen and the "Body-in-Code"
The philosopher Mark B.N. Hansen provides a critical framework for analyzing sensory interaction. Hansen argues against the notion that virtual reality (VR) and digital media leave the body behind. Instead, he proposes the concept of **"bodies-in-code,"** arguing that cyberspace is anchored in the biological body [cite: 5, 6]. For Hansen, the body is the "active framer" of the image; in a digital regime where information is technically inaccessible to human senses (existing as binary code), the body’s sensory-motor response is what actualizes the data into a perceivable reality [cite: 7].

Hansen posits that all reality in the digital age is "mixed reality." This ontology assumes that the body is the primary access point not only to the physical world but also to the virtual. The "body-in-code" refers to a hybrid dimension of presence where the boundaries between the spectator’s physical space and the installation’s digital space dissolve [cite: 1]. In this framework, interactivity is not just a feature; it is a "constitutive ontological feature." The artwork loses its status as a fixed object and becomes a "media environment"—a zone of reconfiguration where the artwork and the recipient come to a mutual "con-fusion" [cite: 1].

### 2.2 Phenomenology of Perception
This approach draws heavily on the phenomenology of Maurice Merleau-Ponty, who emphasized that we experience the world *through* our bodies. In the context of installation art, phenomenology challenges the subject-object distinction. It posits that the viewer does not merely perceive the work but is "interconnected" with it [cite: 8].

In digital installations, this phenomenological experience is intensified. The technology often bypasses the interpretive mind and addresses the body directly through **affectivity** and **proprioception** (the sense of self-movement and body position) [cite: 7]. As noted in the research, the "oculocentric nature of technical-artistic devices is overshadowed in favour of a mainly embodied, situated and inactive knowledge resulting from the sensory feedback loop" [cite: 1]. This shift defines the ontology of participation: the participant exists in the work not as a "viewer" (a visual category) but as an "embodier" (a haptic/proprioceptive category).

### 2.3 Strange Ontologies
While Hansen focuses on the body's role in framing the digital, Mitchell Whitelaw and colleagues introduce the concept of **"strange ontologies"** to describe the structures of the digital systems themselves. Every simulated world requires an ontology (a definition of its existence as data) and a subject (the user). "Strange ontologies" arise when the logic of the simulation clashes with or subverts the lived experience of the user, or when "glitches" reveal the underlying constructed nature of the digital world [cite: 9, 10].

This concept is crucial for understanding participation because it highlights that the "world" the audience participates in is an artificial construct with its own rules of being. The interaction is a negotiation between the "formal ontology" of the code (the rules set by the artist/programmer) and the "phenomenological ontology" of the participant [cite: 10].

## 3. Biometric Ontologies: The Externalization of Interiority

One of the most potent forms of sensory interaction in digital art is the use of biometrics—technologies that measure and analyze biological data. In these works, the ontology of participation is defined by the **externalization of interiority**. The most intimate physiological processes (heart rate, breath, body heat) are captured and projected into the public sphere, transforming the participant’s body into the architecture of the installation.

### 3.1 Rafael Lozano-Hemmer and "Biometric Theatre"
The Mexican-Canadian artist Rafael Lozano-Hemmer is a seminal figure in this domain. His concept of **"Biometric Theatre"** describes installations where biological signatures are made tangible and embodied [cite: 11, 12]. Lozano-Hemmer’s work approaches the human body not as a receiver of stimuli, but as an integral part of the artwork itself [cite: 11].

#### Case Study: *Pulse Room* (2006)
*Pulse Room* is an interactive installation featuring hundreds of incandescent light bulbs suspended from the ceiling. An interface detects the heart rate of a participant. When a visitor holds the sensor, the closest bulb flashes at the exact rhythm of their heart. When they release it, that pattern is transferred to the grid, pushing previous recordings one step further down the line [cite: 3, 13].

*   **Ontological Implications:**
    *   **The Collective Body:** The installation creates a "connective topography" of pulses. It visualizes the individual not as a solitary entity but as part of a flowing, collective current. The ontology of the participant is transient; their "heartbeat" (their sign of life) remains in the room after they leave, but is eventually overwritten by new participants. This creates a digital *memento mori* [cite: 14, 15].
    *   **Involuntary Participation:** Unlike a joystick or a button, the heartbeat is largely involuntary. The participant "performs" simply by being alive. This shifts the nature of agency from "conscious choice" to "biological presence." Lozano-Hemmer notes that the fact that these beats are involuntary is "humbling," reminding us that what keeps us alive is self-regulated and beyond our direct control [cite: 16].
    *   **Architecture of Intimacy:** The work transforms the "stench of other humans" and the intimacy of the body into a "luminous and arrhythmic symphony" [cite: 13, 17]. The private interior becomes public architecture.

#### Case Study: *Pulse Topology* (2021) and *Pulse Index*
In *Pulse Topology*, 3,000 lightbulbs create a landscape of mountains and valleys that react to touchless biometric technology [cite: 12]. *Pulse Index* records fingerprints and heart rates, displaying them on a massive grid. As new people enter, old fingerprints recede. This creates a "communal archive" that blends anonymity with community [cite: 18]. The ontology here is one of **accumulation** and **displacement**—the participant is a data point in a massive, living archive.

### 3.2 The "Externalized" Subject
In these biometric works, the subject is "deterritorialized." The boundaries of the skin are crossed, and the "self" is expanded into the three-dimensional space of the gallery [cite: 19]. The participant’s biological data becomes a "malleable material" that interacts with the surroundings. This challenges the Western conception of the self as a contained, solid individual. Instead, the participant is "liquified" or "evaporated" into the system [cite: 17].

This aligns with the "post-human" perspective where the human is distributed into sensory interfaces. The participant is not observing the art; the art is *metabolizing* the participant.

## 4. Proprioceptive Ontologies: From "Doing" to "Being"

While biometric art often focuses on involuntary signals, **proprioceptive art** engages the voluntary, felt sense of the body’s position and movement in space. This form of interaction constructs an ontology of **immersion** and **embodiment**, often aiming to counter the disembodied nature of conventional digital interfaces.

### 4.1 Char Davies and *Osmose* (1995)
Char Davies’ seminal VR work *Osmose* is the primary case study for this ontological mode. Unlike commercial VR, which typically relies on hand-held controllers (emphasizing instrumentality and "doing"), *Osmose* uses a vest that tracks **breath** and **balance** [cite: 20, 21, 22]. Breathing in lifts the immersant up; breathing out lets them fall. Leaning controls direction.

*   **Ontological Shift: Being vs. Doing:**
    *   Davies explicitly designed the interface to shift the user from a mode of "doing" (control, manipulation, shooting, clicking) to a mode of "being" [cite: 20, 23]. The breath interface grounds the experience in the "most essential living act" [cite: 22].
    *   This constructs an ontology of **vulnerability** and **connection**. The user cannot "dominate" the virtual space; they must breathe with it. This challenges the Cartesian dualism that views the mind as separate from the body and the world as an object to be mastered [cite: 20, 21].
    *   **Dissolution of Boundaries:** The visual aesthetic of *Osmose*—semi-transparent, flowing particles—reinforces this. It avoids "hard-edged mimetic realism" (which reinforces subject/object separation) in favor of ambiguity. This facilitates a "psychological state of floating," where the boundaries between "inner and outer, self and world" dissolve [cite: 21, 24, 25].

### 4.2 Proprioception as "Cognitive Dance"
Beyond VR, the concept of proprioception is applied to how users navigate algorithmic systems. "Cognitive proprioception" refers to the user's ability to sense their own agency and the effects of their actions within a digital model (e.g., an AI recommendation system) [cite: 26]. Just as physical proprioception allows us to know where our limbs are without looking, digital proprioception allows us to "feel" our position within a data landscape.

However, in many digital environments, this sense is dulled. The "frictionless" design of many interfaces removes the feedback necessary for true proprioception, leading to a sense of helplessness or lack of control [cite: 26]. Proprioceptive art attempts to restore this "friction" and awareness, re-grounding the digital experience in the felt reality of the body.

### 4.3 The "Proprioceptive Turn" in Art
The rise of proprioceptive art can be seen as a direct response to the "disembodied online existence" of the 21st century [cite: 27, 28]. By foregrounding the bodily senses (balance, tension, movement), these works offer an "antidote" to the purely audiovisual diet of screens. They construct an ontology where the participant is defined by their **physical weight and volume** in space, rather than their visual consumption of content.

## 5. Haptic Ontologies: Touching the Virtual

Haptics—the technology of touch—adds another layer to the ontology of participation. Haptic interfaces allow users to "feel" digital objects, bridging the gap between the immateriality of code and the materiality of the body.

### 5.1 The Haptic Feedback Loop
In haptic installations, the ontology is defined by the **feedback loop**. The user acts (touches), and the system reacts (vibrates, resists, changes texture). This creates a "hybrid dimension of presence" [cite: 1].
*   **Haptic Voices:** An installation like *Haptic Voices* uses a vibrotactile wall to translate sound into vibration. This allows participants (including the Deaf and hard of hearing) to "feel" voices. The ontology here is **trans-sensory** or **synesthetic**—sound becomes touch, and the body becomes a resonator [cite: 29].
*   **Bodies-in-Code and Tactility:** Hansen argues that touch synthesizes all other senses. In digital environments, touch (or the simulation of it) is what confers "reality" onto the virtual. It generates a "felt correlate" of perception [cite: 30].

### 5.2 Haptics vs. Oculocentrism
Haptic art challenges the "distance" inherent in vision. Vision allows for detachment; touch requires contact. By prioritizing touch, digital installations force an ontology of **implication**. The participant cannot remain a voyeur; they must be physically involved. This aligns with the "sensory turn" in museum studies, where the goal is to create "immersive and fusional contact" with the interface [cite: 1, 31].

## 6. The Ontology of Agency: Participation or Illusion?

A critical component of the ontology of audience participation is the nature of **agency**. If the artwork is a "system," what is the status of the participant within that system? Are they a co-creator, or merely a variable in an algorithm?

### 6.1 The Illusion of Agency
Research suggests that digital devices and installations often create an **"illusion of agency"** [cite: 32, 33]. Users feel they are in control, but they are often merely responding to stimuli in predictable ways, or selecting from a pre-determined set of options (a "decision tree" rather than an open field).
*   **The "Cut-Scene" Effect:** In many interactive works, the user triggers an event, but the event itself is a pre-rendered sequence. The user has the agency of a "switch," not a "creator."
*   **Commands to Command:** The artist Giuseppe Torre critiques this in his work *Commands to Command*, arguing that in digital interactions, we "obey even in the very act of commanding." The interface structures our possibilities so rigidly that our "participation" is actually a form of submission to the code [cite: 33].

### 6.2 Distributed Authorship and Co-Creation
Despite the limits of agency, interactive art does shift the ontology of authorship. The work is no longer the sole product of the artist. It is a **"collaborative performance"** [cite: 34].
*   **Visitor-Made-Interactor:** The audience member becomes a "visitor-made-interactor" [cite: 35, 36]. Without their input, the work does not fully exist (e.g., *Pulse Room* is dark without a heartbeat).
*   **Open-Ended Systems:** Works that use "open-ended" algorithms (like artificial life or complex generative systems) allow for outcomes that the artist did not predict. Here, the agency of the audience is more robust, as they are influencing a dynamic system rather than triggering a script [cite: 2].

### 6.3 Object-Oriented Ontology (OOO) and the "Withdrawn" Object
Applying **Object-Oriented Ontology (OOO)** to interactive art complicates the picture. OOO (championed by Graham Harman) argues that objects exist independently of human perception [cite: 37, 38].
*   **The Paradox:** Interactive art seems to contradict OOO because it *requires* human participation to function. However, OOO theorists might argue that the "code" or the "system" has its own internal reality ("private life") that is never fully accessible to the user [cite: 39].
*   **The "Alien" Agency:** When users interact with complex machine learning algorithms, they often encounter an "alien agency"—the system behaves in ways that feel autonomous. The ontology of participation here is a negotiation with a non-human intelligence [cite: 40, 41]. The artwork is not a passive tool but an active agent (an "actant" in Latour's terms).

## 7. Conclusion: The Hybrid Ontology

Sensory interaction in digital installations constructs an ontology of audience participation that is **hybrid, relational, and fluid**.

1.  **Hybridity:** The participant is no longer just a biological entity; they are a "body-in-code," extended into the digital realm through sensors and feedback loops.
2.  **Relationality:** The artwork is not a static object; it is a "situation" or "event" that emerges from the relationship between the code and the participant’s body.
3.  **Fluidity:** The roles of "artist," "artwork," and "audience" are destabilized. The audience becomes the content (biometrics), the interface (proprioception), and the co-author (interaction).

This ontology moves art away from representation (showing the world) toward **simulation** and **enactment** (being in the world). Whether through the "humbling" surrender of a heartbeat in *Pulse Room* or the meditative breath of *Osmose*, the audience is invited to inhabit a reality where their physical existence is the fundamental condition of the artwork’s being. However, this participation is always tensioned against the "strange ontology" of the digital system itself—a system that grants agency only within the strict confines of its code.

## 8. Detailed Analysis of Key Case Studies

### 8.1 Rafael Lozano-Hemmer: The Biometric Archive
Lozano-Hemmer’s work is paramount because it scales individual biological data into architectural and social phenomena.
*   **Technique:** He uses photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors (often found in fitness trackers) to detect heart rates [cite: 14].
*   **Ontological Impact:** The "self" is visualized as a pattern of light. In *Pulse Room*, the grid of 300 bulbs represents a "community of hearts." It is a visualization of **collective vitality** but also of **mortality** (as the lights eventually fade or are pushed out). The participation is a "memento mori"—a reminder of death in the midst of a technological spectacle [cite: 14].
*   **Social Dimension:** The installation creates a "fortified social space" where strangers are connected by the rhythm of their bodies. It breaks the social isolation often associated with technology [cite: 16, 34].

### 8.2 Char Davies: The Immersive Self
Davies’ work stands in contrast to the "disembodied" rhetoric of 1990s cyberpunk.
*   **Technique:** Breath and balance sensors. The "vest" interface [cite: 22].
*   **Ontological Impact:** *Osmose* creates a "trance-like" state. Users report feelings of "floating" and "dissolution." This is a **phenomenological reduction**—stripping away the social and cultural layers of the self to reveal the "embodied consciousness" [cite: 21, 42].
*   **Critique of Vision:** By using semi-transparent graphics, Davies forces the user to rely on "felt" navigation rather than "seen" navigation. This subverts the "gaze" of traditional art [cite: 20].

### 8.3 TeamLab: The Borderless World
The Japanese collective TeamLab creates "borderless" worlds where digital projections react to touch and movement.
*   **Technique:** Projection mapping, motion sensors.
*   **Ontological Impact:** The artwork is "continuous." It flows from room to room and reacts to the visitor. If a visitor stands still, flowers might bloom around them; if they move, the flowers scatter. This constructs an ontology of **continuity** between the human and the environment (even if that environment is artificial) [cite: 2, 43].
*   **Agency:** While visually stunning, TeamLab’s work is often critiqued for offering "illusory agency"—the interactions are beautiful but fundamentally limited to "reaction" rather than deep "creation" [cite: 43].

## 9. Challenges and Critiques

### 9.1 The Ethics of Biometrics
The use of biometric data in art raises ethical questions. When an artist "harvests" heartbeats or fingerprints, they are engaging in a form of **data surveillance**. While Lozano-Hemmer describes his work as "consensual crowdsourcing" [cite: 11], it mirrors the biometric tracking used by states and corporations. The ontology of participation here borders on the ontology of the **subject-under-surveillance**.

### 9.2 The Limits of "Interactivity"
Critics like Slavoj Žižek (referenced in discussions of "interpassivity") and others argue that interactivity can be a false freedom. If the software pre-determines every possible outcome, the user is merely "playing" the system, not changing it. This "interactivity" can mask the underlying passivity of the consumer [cite: 33, 44].

### 9.3 Accessibility
Sensory installations often assume a "normative" body. Works that require standing, seeing, or specific motor controls can exclude those with disabilities. However, works like *Haptic Voices* (vibrotactile) explicitly try to broaden the ontology of participation to include non-visual and non-auditory experiences [cite: 29].

## 10. Future Directions: AI and Neuro-Arts

The future of this ontology lies in **neuro-arts** and **AI**.
*   **Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI):** Artists are beginning to use EEG data (brainwaves) as input. This would push the "externalization of interiority" to its ultimate limit—displaying thought itself [cite: 23].
*   **AI Co-Creation:** As AI becomes more responsive, the "feedback loop" will become a "dialogue." The AI will not just react; it will anticipate and improvise. This will create a **"strange ontology"** of partnership with a non-human intelligence [cite: 41].

In conclusion, sensory interaction in digital installations does not merely add a "feature" to art; it fundamentally reconstructs the ontology of the participant. It transforms the viewer from a detached observer into an embodied, data-generating, and co-constituting force within a hybrid reality. This shift redefines art as a living system, dependent on the "flesh" of the audience to sustain its digital "pulse."
