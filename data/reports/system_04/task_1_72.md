**Key Points**
*   **Exploratory vs. Deterministic Nature:** Currently, most regulatory bodies utilize climate stress testing primarily as a learning exercise to identify data gaps and vulnerabilities rather than a direct mechanism for setting Pillar 1 capital requirements. However, results are increasingly informing Pillar 2 supervisory assessments.
*   **Scenario Design as the Primary Variable:** The calibration of capital adequacy is heavily dependent on scenario variables, specifically the choice between static and dynamic balance sheet assumptions. Static assumptions generally yield higher hypothetical capital depletion by preventing banks from divesting high-risk assets, whereas dynamic assumptions allow for transition strategies but introduce modeling uncertainty.
*   **Pillar 2 is the Current Mechanism:** There is a global regulatory consensus, led by the Basel Committee and the ECB, that Pillar 2 (Supervisory Review) is currently the most appropriate mechanism for addressing climate risks. This allows for qualitative overlays and institution-specific capital add-ons (P2R) where quantitative modeling remains immature.
*   **Resistance to Pillar 1 Modifications:** Regulators, particularly the EBA, have largely rejected "Green Supporting Factors" or "Brown Penalizing Factors" within Pillar 1 (minimum capital requirements) due to a lack of evidence that environmental risk correlates perfectly with financial risk in the short term, and fears of distorting risk-based prudential frameworks.
*   **Jurisdictional Divergence:** A significant split exists between the European approach (ECB/EBA), which actively integrates climate risk into the SREP and capital add-ons, and the U.S. approach (Federal Reserve), which views climate scenarios as distinct from the capital-setting stress testing regime.

## 1. Introduction: The Intersection of Climate Scenarios and Prudential Capital

The integration of environmental risk into the prudential framework represents a paradigm shift in banking regulation. Historically, capital adequacy calibration—the process of determining the minimum amount of capital a bank must hold to withstand losses—relied on historical loss data (backward-looking). Climate risk, however, is characterized by radical uncertainty, non-linearity, and a lack of historical precedence, necessitating a forward-looking approach via scenario design [cite: 1, 2].

The central question facing regulators and financial institutions is how the parameters of these forward-looking scenarios—ranging from time horizons to balance sheet dynamics—translate into quantitative impacts on capital ratios. While the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) has clarified that climate risks can be captured within the existing Basel Framework [cite: 1, 3], the mechanism for doing so remains contested. The calibration of capital adequacy is not merely a mathematical output of stress tests; it is a function of scenario severity, the assumed transmission channels (credit, market, and operational risk), and the regulatory philosophy regarding whether capital should be used to buffer against risk or incentivize the economic transition [cite: 4, 5].

This report examines the mechanics of scenario design, the transmission of environmental risks into financial loss metrics (PD/LGD), and the resulting implications for capital adequacy under Pillar 1 and Pillar 2 of the Basel Framework.

## 2. Theoretical Framework of Climate Scenario Design

The calibration of capital adequacy in the context of climate risk is fundamentally determined by the architecture of the scenarios used. Unlike traditional macroeconomic stress tests (e.g., CCAR in the US or EBA stress tests in the EU), which typically span 3–5 years, climate scenarios must bridge the "tragedy of the horizon," extending decades into the future while attempting to inform current capital decisions.

### 2.1 The NGFS Reference Scenarios
The Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) has established the global standard for climate scenarios, which serve as the foundational input for most regulatory stress tests [cite: 6, 7]. The impact on capital adequacy varies drastically depending on which of the three primary NGFS scenario families is applied:

1.  **Orderly Transition:** Assumes early, ambitious policy action to reach net-zero CO2 emissions. Transition risks are moderate, and physical risks are minimized. In this scenario, capital depletion is generally manageable as asset values adjust gradually [cite: 6].
2.  **Disorderly Transition:** Assumes action is late, disruptive, and sudden. This scenario is critical for stress testing capital adequacy because it introduces sharp "Minsky moments" or asset price shocks. A sudden introduction of high carbon prices can lead to rapid repricing of "brown" assets, causing immediate spikes in Probability of Default (PD) and Loss Given Default (LGD) for corporate exposures [cite: 2, 6].
3.  **Hot House World:** Assumes limited policy action, leading to significant global warming. Here, transition risk is low, but physical risk (acute and chronic) becomes the dominant driver of capital erosion. This scenario tests the resilience of mortgage portfolios and physical collateral rather than corporate transition risk [cite: 6, 8].

### 2.2 The Time Horizon Paradox
A critical friction in calibrating capital adequacy is the mismatch between the duration of capital instruments (typically 1 year for regulatory capital calculations) and the horizon of climate risks (10–30 years).
*   **Short-term Scenarios (3–5 years):** Regulators like the ECB and NGFS have developed short-term "disorderly" scenarios to force climate risks to materialize within the standard capital planning horizon [cite: 9, 10]. These scenarios artificially bring forward transition shocks to test immediate solvency, making them the most relevant for current capital calibration.
*   **Long-term Scenarios (30+ years):** These are used for strategic analysis rather than capital setting. The uncertainty expands significantly over time, making the results less legally defensible as a basis for binding capital surcharges [cite: 7, 11].

### 2.3 Granularity and Sectoral Differentiation
Scenario design affects capital calibration through granularity. Traditional stress tests often rely on broad macroeconomic indicators (GDP, unemployment). Climate scenarios, however, require high granularity to be effective, as risks are concentrated in specific sectors (e.g., fossil fuels, energy-intensive manufacturing) and geographies [cite: 7, 12].
*   **Sectoral Shocks:** Scenarios that apply uniform shocks across sectors fail to capture the "risk differential" necessary for accurate capital calibration. Advanced scenario designs differentiate shocks at the NACE code level or even counterparty level, leading to more precise (and often higher) Risk-Weighted Asset (RWA) calculations for exposed banks [cite: 12].

## 3. Balance Sheet Assumptions: Static vs. Dynamic

Perhaps the single most consequential variable in scenario design affecting capital adequacy results is the assumption regarding the bank's balance sheet behavior during the stress horizon.

### 3.1 Static Balance Sheet Assumption
A static balance sheet assumption posits that the size, composition, and risk profile of a bank's portfolio remain invariant throughout the stress testing horizon [cite: 13, 14].
*   **Impact on Capital:** This approach typically results in the highest calculated capital depletion. It assumes banks cannot divest from high-carbon assets or reallocate capital to green technologies in response to policy shocks.
*   **Regulatory Usage:** The Bank of England (BoE), Bank of Canada (BoC), and Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) have largely utilized static assumptions to identify "pure" vulnerability and prevent banks from "optimizing away" the risk in their models [cite: 11, 15].
*   **Implication:** Under a static assumption, the capital adequacy calibration reflects the *current* risk profile projected into a stressed future, serving as a snapshot of solvency risk if no management action is taken.

### 3.2 Dynamic Balance Sheet Assumption
A dynamic balance sheet assumption allows banks to adjust their portfolios, pricing strategies, and funding mixes in response to the unfolding scenario [cite: 13, 16].
*   **Impact on Capital:** This generally results in lower capital depletion, as banks model their exit from "brown" sectors. However, it introduces significant model risk and behavioral uncertainty. If all banks assume they can divest simultaneously, it creates a "fire sale" risk that dynamic models often fail to capture endogenously [cite: 17].
*   **Regulatory Usage:** The ECB and ACPR (France) have experimented with dynamic assumptions for long-term horizons (e.g., 2025–2050), acknowledging that assuming a static portfolio over 30 years is unrealistic [cite: 17, 18].
*   **Implication:** Dynamic scenarios test the viability of a bank's *strategy* rather than just its current solvency. For capital calibration, this complicates the process, as regulators must validate the plausibility of the management actions (e.g., "Can you actually sell these coal assets in a disorderly transition?") [cite: 19].

## 4. Transmission Mechanisms: Translating Scenarios to Capital Ratios

To affect capital adequacy, climate scenarios must be translated into the traditional language of banking risk: Probability of Default (PD), Loss Given Default (LGD), and Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA).

### 4.1 Credit Risk Transmission
Credit risk is the primary channel through which climate scenarios impact capital ratios [cite: 20, 21].
*   **PD Modeling:** Scenarios utilize carbon price pathways and GDP shocks to impair corporate borrower profitability. For example, a high carbon price in a "Disorderly" scenario reduces the free cash flow of a high-emission borrower, increasing their PD. Banks are increasingly incorporating these climate drivers into Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) models, though expert judgment remains dominant due to data limitations [cite: 21].
*   **LGD and Collateral:** Physical risk scenarios (e.g., floods, wildfires) directly impact LGD by reducing the value of collateral (real estate). In the ECB's stress tests, physical risks were shown to increase expected real estate losses, directly impacting capital buffers required for mortgage portfolios [cite: 22].
*   **RWA Inflation:** As PDs and LGDs rise under stress scenarios, the Risk-Weighted Assets (the denominator of the capital ratio) increase. If capital (the numerator) remains constant or falls due to losses, the capital adequacy ratio deteriorates [cite: 23].

### 4.2 Market Risk Transmission
Integrating climate scenarios into the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) and market risk capital is challenging due to the mismatch in horizons. Market risk models (Value-at-Risk) typically look at 10-day liquidity horizons, whereas climate shocks play out over years [cite: 24, 25].
*   **Scenario Analysis in Trading Books:** ISDA and other bodies are developing frameworks to shock market risk factors (equity prices, credit spreads) based on short-term climate scenarios. However, current capital requirements for market risk largely rely on historical volatility, which does not yet capture future climate "green swans" [cite: 24, 26].

### 4.3 Operational Risk
Physical climate events (e.g., hurricanes disrupting data centers or branches) are categorized as operational risk. While some studies suggest these losses are currently immaterial to systemic capital adequacy (e.g., estimated at $22 million on average for specific events), regulators are increasingly asking firms to model these impacts under "severe but plausible" physical risk scenarios [cite: 27, 28].

## 5. Regulatory Approaches to Capital Calibration

The debate on how to use scenario outputs to calibrate capital requirements centers on the three pillars of the Basel Framework.

### 5.1 Pillar 1: Minimum Capital Requirements
Pillar 1 refers to the mandatory minimum capital banks must hold. There has been intense debate regarding the introduction of "Green Supporting Factors" (GSF) or "Brown Penalizing Factors" (BPF) to adjust Pillar 1 requirements based on climate characteristics.
*   **EBA Position:** The European Banking Authority (EBA) has explicitly recommended *against* introducing GSF or BPF at this stage. Their analysis concludes that a clear risk differential between green and brown assets has not yet been empirically proven to the extent required for Pillar 1. They argue that deviating from a risk-based framework for policy objectives could undermine financial stability [cite: 29, 30, 31].
*   **Risk-Based Approach:** Instead of political adjustment factors, regulators prefer that climate risks be captured through the standard risk weights (e.g., if a brown asset is riskier, its PD should naturally be higher, leading to higher capital requirements without a special "factor") [cite: 30, 31].

### 5.2 Pillar 2: Supervisory Review Process (SREP)
Pillar 2 is currently the primary mechanism for integrating climate scenario results into capital adequacy.
*   **ECB Methodology:** The European Central Bank uses the Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP) to assess how well banks manage climate risks. While the 2022 climate stress test was a "learning exercise" with no direct capital impact, the ECB has begun using the *qualitative* findings (e.g., governance failures, lack of data) to adjust Pillar 2 Requirements (P2R) [cite: 12, 22, 32].
*   **Capital Add-ons:** The ECB has explicitly stated that P2R add-ons will be used for banks that fail to address climate risk deficiencies. In the 2023 SREP cycle, a greater number of banks saw P2R impacts due to climate and environmental risks [cite: 33].
*   **Qualitative vs. Quantitative:** Currently, the link is largely qualitative (penalizing poor risk management). However, the transition to quantitative capital add-ons based on stress test depletion is the stated long-term goal as data quality improves [cite: 34].

### 5.3 Pillar 3: Market Discipline
While not a direct capital requirement, Pillar 3 disclosures (e.g., ESG risks, financed emissions) are being enforced to allow market participants to price climate risks. This indirect pressure affects a bank's cost of capital and market valuation, acting as a market-based calibration mechanism [cite: 23, 35].

## 6. Jurisdictional Divergence in Calibration

The application of scenario design to capital calibration varies significantly across major jurisdictions.

### 6.1 European Union (ECB/EBA)
The EU is the most aggressive jurisdiction in linking climate scenarios to capital.
*   **Approach:** The ECB views climate risk as a material financial risk that must be reflected in capital. They utilize the SREP to impose bank-specific capital add-ons (Pillar 2) based on climate risk management scores [cite: 33, 34].
*   **Stress Testing:** The ECB's 2022 Climate Risk Stress Test used NGFS scenarios and dynamic balance sheet assumptions for long-term views. While it did not directly reset Pillar 1 capital, the results fed directly into supervisory dialogues that determine Pillar 2 guidance [cite: 12, 22].

### 6.2 United Kingdom (BoE/PRA)
The UK has been a pioneer in scenario analysis (CBES) but remains cautious about immediate capital calibration.
*   **Approach:** The Bank of England's Climate Biennial Exploratory Scenario (CBES) used static balance sheets to isolate risks. The BoE has stated that these exercises are not yet used to set capital requirements directly, focusing instead on capability building [cite: 15, 36].
*   **Capital Regime:** The PRA expects firms to hold capital for material climate risks but acknowledges that standard Pillar 1 models may not yet capture them. They emphasize the use of the PRA Buffer (Pillar 2B) for risks not captured in Pillar 1 [cite: 37, 38].

### 6.3 United States (Federal Reserve)
The U.S. adopts a distinctively different stance, viewing climate scenarios as exploratory tools separate from the binding capital regime.
*   **Approach:** The Federal Reserve's pilot climate scenario analysis (CSA) explicitly stated there would be "no capital consequences." The Fed views its mandate as safety and soundness but has faced political and industry pushback against using climate scenarios to set capital requirements [cite: 39, 40, 41].
*   **Philosophy:** The Fed emphasizes that climate risks should be managed through existing risk management frameworks rather than through novel capital surcharges or Pillar 1 adjustments [cite: 39, 40].

## 7. Challenges and Limitations

The calibration of capital adequacy via climate scenarios is fraught with methodological hurdles that limit its current precision.

### 7.1 Data Gaps and Proxies
A significant portion of stress testing relies on proxies (e.g., sector averages) rather than actual counterparty emissions data. The ECB found that banks often use proxies for Scope 3 emissions, leading to potential underestimation of risks. This lack of reliable data makes regulators hesitant to impose binding quantitative capital requirements [cite: 10, 12].

### 7.2 Model Uncertainty and Non-Linearity
Climate risks are non-linear and subject to "tipping points" (e.g., ecosystem collapse, sudden policy shifts). Traditional credit risk models (PD/LGD) assume linear relationships based on historical correlations. Scenario designs that rely on these linear models likely underestimate the "tail risk" of extreme climate events, suggesting that current capital buffers might be insufficient [cite: 2, 9].

### 7.3 The "Green Bubble" Risk
Paradoxically, scenario designs that heavily penalize brown assets could incentivize a rush into green assets, creating a "green bubble." Regulators like the ECB have warned that exposures to green industries also carry credit risk (e.g., technology obsolescence, over-funding), which must be captured in capital calibration [cite: 19].

## 8. Conclusion

Scenario design is the fulcrum upon which the integration of environmental risk into capital adequacy turns. The choice of **static versus dynamic balance sheets**, the **granularity of sectoral shocks**, and the **time horizon** of the stress test fundamentally alter the perceived capital depletion of financial institutions.

Currently, the global regulatory consensus favors a **Pillar 2 approach**, where scenario analysis informs supervisory judgment and qualitative capital add-ons, rather than a mechanical adjustment of Pillar 1 risk weights. This reflects a recognition of the data limitations and model uncertainties inherent in climate risk. While the EU is moving toward explicit capital charges via the SREP, the U.S. remains focused on risk management capabilities without direct capital linkage.

As data quality improves and scenario methodologies mature, it is expected that the link between scenario outcomes and quantitative capital requirements will tighten, transitioning from "learning exercises" to binding constraints on bank capital planning.
