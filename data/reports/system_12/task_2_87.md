**Yes, state transition matrices (often embedded within state-and-transition models, or STMs) of successional trajectories do constrain the effectiveness of ecological restoration.**[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf)

These matrices, derived from Markov chain models or similar frameworks, quantify the probabilities of shifts between discrete ecosystem states (e.g., grassland → shrubland) driven by succession, disturbances, or management. They reveal structural limitations like low transition probabilities, hysteresis (where return paths differ from forward ones), and alternative stable states stabilized by feedbacks (e.g., soil erosion or plant-soil interactions).[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf)

### Key Constraints:
- **Thresholds and Irreversibility**: Crossing ecological thresholds (e.g., heavy grazing reducing grass cover below a viability level) makes reversal improbable without intensive intervention, long recovery times, or extreme events. In the Chihuahuan Desert, grasslands transition to mesquite-dominated shrublands via erosion; recovery is "dependent on unacceptably long recovery times, active restoration, extreme events, or a reversal of climatic change."[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf) Similar dynamics occur in Patagonian rangelands, where degradation creates "irreversible or difficult to reverse" states.[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf)
  
- **Alternative Stable States**: Matrices show ecosystems can lock into undesired states due to self-reinforcing feedbacks. For instance, woody encroachment in fire-suppressed grasslands prevents natural reversion because fuel loads are too low for restorative fires.[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf) Fragmented vegetation in arid systems leads to "persistent low-productivity/high bare ground" states.[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf)

- **Slow or Low-Probability Successional Rates**: Natural succession matrices often predict gradual changes over decades, constraining restoration timelines. Transient fluctuations (e.g., weather-driven shifts) must be distinguished from true transitions, but misidentification can lead to ineffective efforts.[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf)

### Informing Restoration Despite Constraints
While constraining, these models guide effective strategies by identifying feasible interventions (e.g., targeted grazing reductions or seeding to boost low-probability transitions) and testable hypotheses for adaptive management. They emphasize context-dependence (soils, climate) and the need for monitoring to update probabilities.[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf) For example, in Mongolian rangelands, remnant grasses signal recoverable states within "a few years to several decades" via management.[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf)

In summary, the matrices highlight when restoration is feasible via natural or assisted succession versus cases requiring overcoming "traps" like thresholds—directly informing realistic expectations and avoiding futile efforts.[[1]](https://jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/17-010.pdf)
