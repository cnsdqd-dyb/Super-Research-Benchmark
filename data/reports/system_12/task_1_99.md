**The Universal Declaration on the Human Genome and Human Rights (1997), adopted by UNESCO, is the primary international framework establishing normative constraints on CRISPR germline interventions with respect to human dignity.[[1]](https://www.unesco.org/en/ethics-science-technology/human-genome-and-human-rights)[[2]](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/POST-PN-0611/POST-PN-0611.pdf)**

This non-binding yet influential declaration treats the human genome as humanity's common heritage, emphasizing its role in the "fundamental unity of all members of the human family, as well as the recognition of their inherent dignity and diversity."[[1]](https://www.unesco.org/en/ethics-science-technology/human-genome-and-human-rights) Key provisions include:

- **Article on interventions**: "Interventions on the human genome should be admitted only for preventive, diagnostic or therapeutic reasons and without enacting modifications for descendants."[[1]](https://www.unesco.org/en/ethics-science-technology/human-genome-and-human-rights) This directly constrains heritable (germline) editing, such as with CRISPR-Cas9, which can alter eggs, sperm, or embryos and pass changes to future generations.[[1]](https://www.unesco.org/en/ethics-science-technology/human-genome-and-human-rights)
- **Link to dignity**: Research on the human genome must not override "respect for the human rights, freedoms and dignity of individuals or groups" (Article 10).[[2]](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/POST-PN-0611/POST-PN-0611.pdf) Practices contrary to human dignity, like those risking heritable changes, are prohibited.[[1]](https://www.unesco.org/en/ethics-science-technology/human-genome-and-human-rights)

UNESCO's International Bioethics Committee (IBC) reinforced this in 2015 with a moratorium call on human germline genome editing, explicitly citing CRISPR-Cas9 concerns about heritable modifications.[[1]](https://www.unesco.org/en/ethics-science-technology/human-genome-and-human-rights)

**Closely related is the Council of Europe’s Oviedo Convention on Human Rights and Biomedicine (1997)**, legally binding for signatories. Article 1 protects "the dignity and identity of all human beings," while Article 13 bans germline modifications: "An intervention seeking to modify the human genome may only be undertaken for preventive, diagnostic or therapeutic purposes and only if it is not intended to introduce any modification in the genome of any descendants."[[2]](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/POST-PN-0611/POST-PN-0611.pdf)[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-022-01147-y)

These frameworks predate CRISPR (developed ~2012) but directly apply to it, framing germline editing as a dignity violation if heritable, due to risks to equality, identity, future generations, and the genome's symbolic status.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-022-01147-y) Ethical debates continue—some argue unmodified genomes aren't essential to dignity—but these set the core global norms.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-022-01147-y) Other documents, like UNESCO’s 2005 Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights, add future-generation protections.[[2]](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/POST-PN-0611/POST-PN-0611.pdf)
