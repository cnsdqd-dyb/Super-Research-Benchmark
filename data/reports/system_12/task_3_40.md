Commuting networks—often modeled as graphs with nodes as locations (e.g., municipalities or neighborhoods) and edges as commuter flows—have been analyzed using complex network theory. Studies apply metrics like degree distribution, clustering, centrality, and entropy to capture properties such as small-world behavior or hierarchical structure.[[3]](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/0-387-71134-1_8)

Percolation analysis, a key method for detecting critical transitions (e.g., emergence of giant connected components or hierarchical scales), is commonly used on these networks. For instance:
- Percolation identifies relational scales and nested urban clusters by progressively merging communities based on flow connectivity, revealing phase-like transitions in network structure.[[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00213)[[4]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223619851_Does_Commuting_Distance_Matter_Commuting_Tolerance_and_Residential_Change)
- This helps delineate functional urban areas but is applied to fixed empirical networks (e.g., Chile or Germany), not varied by parameters like separation.[[5]](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.00213)

Job-housing separation (or imbalance) is widely studied in relation to commuting patterns, often linked to longer distances, spatial mismatch, and excess commuting.[[6]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1361920915301644)[[7]](https://www.mdpi.com/2412-3811/10/7/164) It influences network topology indirectly (e.g., longer-range links), potentially lowering percolation thresholds in spatial networks, but no studies simulate or empirically test critical transitions by systematically increasing separation.[[2]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363638186_Community-Detection-Based_Spatial_Range_Identification_for_Assessing_Bilateral_Jobs-Housing_Balance_The_Case_of_Beijing)[[8]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366110965_Exploring_the_topological_characteristics_of_urban_trip_networks_based_on_taxi_trajectory_data)

The term "commuting chains" appears rarely in network contexts—sometimes denoting multi-leg trips or sequences—but not tied to complex network analysis or critical phenomena here.[[9]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1361920925001348) Related work on trip chains or flows uses clustering or entropy but lacks explicit critical transitions.[[2]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363638186_Community-Detection-Based_Spatial_Range_Identification_for_Assessing_Bilateral_Jobs-Housing_Balance_The_Case_of_Beijing)

**In summary**, while commuting networks display critical behaviors via percolation (e.g., cluster merging), no research directly links these to varying job-housing separation, and "commuting chains" are not a focus in such analyses. Theoretical spatial network models suggest longer links (from greater separation) could trigger transitions toward higher connectivity, but this remains untested empirically for commuting.[[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00213)
