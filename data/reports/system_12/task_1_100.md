**Decision priorities in autonomous weapon systems (AWS), also known as lethal autonomous weapon systems (LAWS), are not governed by a single universal standard but are primarily established through human-led processes during system design, development, and deployment to align with international humanitarian law (IHL) principles like distinction (differentiating combatants from civilians), proportionality (balancing military gain against civilian harm), and precautions in attack.**[[1]](https://www.icrc.org/sites/default/files/document/file_list/autonomous_weapon_systems_under_international_humanitarian_law.pdf)[[2]](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/2023-03/ihl_and_aws.pdf)

### Key Mechanisms for Establishing Priorities
1. **Design and Programming Phase**:
   - Humans (engineers, programmers) embed decision logic via software algorithms, sensors (e.g., cameras, radar), and machine learning models trained on datasets to recognize targets based on proxies like shape, heat signatures, movement, or uniforms.[[3]](https://www.icrc.org/en/download/file/1707/4221-002-autonomous-weapons-systems-full-report.pdf)[[4]](https://www.hrw.org/report/2025/04/28/a-hazard-to-human-rights/autonomous-weapons-systems-and-digital-decision-making)
   - Priorities are set through **operational parameters**, such as confidence thresholds (e.g., 90% certainty for target classification), target categories (prioritizing materiel like vehicles over personnel), geographic/time limits, and exclusion zones to minimize civilian exposure.[[1]](https://www.icrc.org/sites/default/files/document/file_list/autonomous_weapon_systems_under_international_humanitarian_law.pdf)[[3]](https://www.icrc.org/en/download/file/1707/4221-002-autonomous-weapons-systems-full-report.pdf)
   - Legal reviews (e.g., under Article 36 of Additional Protocol I) and testing in realistic environments ensure predictability, reliability, and IHL compliance, with safeguards like fail-safes to abort engagements if risks to non-targets (civilians) exceed thresholds.[[5]](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)[[2]](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/2023-03/ihl_and_aws.pdf)

2. **Activation and Deployment Phase**:
   - Commanders/operators set mission-specific constraints (e.g., rules of engagement, environment assessments) before activation, evaluating civilian presence and system performance.[[1]](https://www.icrc.org/sites/default/files/document/file_list/autonomous_weapon_systems_under_international_humanitarian_law.pdf)
   - U.S. DoD Directive 3000.09 (2023) mandates "appropriate levels of human judgment" over force use, requiring transparent interfaces for monitoring, intervention, and deactivation. Systems must terminate if they deviate from intent or enter prohibited areas.[[5]](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)

3. **Operational Phase**:
   - Systems process real-time data for target selection/engagement, prioritizing based on pre-programmed rules (e.g., imminent threats first). Human "on-the-loop" supervision allows overrides in semi-autonomous modes.[[3]](https://www.icrc.org/en/download/file/1707/4221-002-autonomous-weapons-systems-full-report.pdf)
   - Defensive examples like Israel's Iron Dome or U.S. Phalanx CIWS prioritize incoming missiles over personnel, with human verification options.[[4]](https://www.hrw.org/report/2025/04/28/a-hazard-to-human-rights/autonomous-weapons-systems-and-digital-decision-making)

### Policies and Frameworks
- **U.S. Policy**: DoD emphasizes minimizing unintended engagements via robust testing, bias mitigation, cybersecurity, and ethical AI principles (e.g., traceability, governability). Fully autonomous lethal targeting of humans is not pursued; focus is on materiel defense.[[5]](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)
- **ICRC and International Views**: Stress "meaningful human control" to retain judgment for nuanced IHL assessments. No AWS can fully replicate human empathy or context evaluation; calls for prohibitions on unpredictable systems or those targeting people directly.[[1]](https://www.icrc.org/sites/default/files/document/file_list/autonomous_weapon_systems_under_international_humanitarian_law.pdf)[[6]](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/what-you-need-know-about-autonomous-weapons)

### Challenges and Limitations
- Algorithms struggle with dynamic environments (e.g., urban areas with civilians mimicking combatants), biases in training data, and qualitative proportionality (e.g., weighing abstract military advantage).[[4]](https://www.hrw.org/report/2025/04/28/a-hazard-to-human-rights/autonomous-weapons-systems-and-digital-decision-making)[[3]](https://www.icrc.org/en/download/file/1707/4221-002-autonomous-weapons-systems-full-report.pdf)
- No deployed AWS fully autonomously targets humans; current systems are semi-autonomous. Critics (e.g., HRW, ICRC) argue full autonomy risks dehumanization and IHL violations, advocating bans.[[4]](https://www.hrw.org/report/2025/04/28/a-hazard-to-human-rights/autonomous-weapons-systems-and-digital-decision-making)

In summary, civilian protection relies on **human foresight in programming safeguards and oversight**, not inherent machine ethics, with ongoing UN discussions (e.g., CCW) seeking binding rules.[[1]](https://www.icrc.org/sites/default/files/document/file_list/autonomous_weapon_systems_under_international_humanitarian_law.pdf)
