**Genome editing technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9, have sparked profound ethical debates that extend beyond human applications to non-human animals and ecosystems, challenging anthropocentric frameworks and laying groundwork for cross-species (or interspecies) justice.**[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271)

### Challenging Anthropocentrism in Ethical Discourse
Traditional bioethics often prioritizes human health, efficiency, and benefits (e.g., xenotransplantation or disease models), reflecting anthropocentrism where non-human interests are instrumentalized.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271) Genome editing disrupts this by highlighting underrepresented animal perspectives: editing alters animals' moral status, dignity, and species-specific capacities, forcing ethicists to question human hubris in "playing God" or breaching natural boundaries.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271) For instance, creating chimeric organisms or gene drives (e.g., to eradicate disease-carrying insects) raises concerns about ecological imbalances and the intrinsic value of non-human life, shifting focus from human utility to multispecies flourishing.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271)

### Rebuilding Foundations for Cross-Species Justice
Genome editing ethics rebuilds justice foundations by addressing **power asymmetries and historical wrongs** between species:
- **Moral Status and Dignity**: Editing non-human primates or livestock (e.g., hornless cattle for welfare) prompts debates on whether altered animals retain "dignity" or telos (natural purpose). Kantian arguments claim animals lack self-determination, justifying edits; critics counter that instrumentalization violates intrinsic worth, advocating protections akin to rights.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271) This elevates sentience-based moral status across species.
- **Reparations and Equity**: Humans have historically exploited animals (e.g., in agriculture); editing could "reparate" via welfare enhancements but risks perpetuating imbalance. Calls emerge for intergenerational and interspecies equity, weighing risks (e.g., unintended ecosystem harms) against benefits like reviving extinct species caused by human actions.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271)
- **Beyond Consequentialism**: Pure human-benefit calculus is critiqued; virtue ethics asks "what kind of humans do we become?" through animal alterations. Multidisciplinary approaches (including animal welfare science) promote proactive governance, subsidiarity (least-harm alternatives), and public inclusion for non-anthropocentric norms.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271)

Related works amplify this: UNESCO reports urge broadening ethics to "other life forms," while Nuffield Council analyses on farmed animals stress welfare justice.[[2]](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000378723) Interspecies justice theories (e.g., agency and assent) intersect with editing in research ethics, viewing edits as potential steps toward equitable relations, like cultured meat transitions.[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11475729)[[4]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10806-025-09965-1)

### Key Implications and Pathways Forward
- **Positive Rebuilding**: Enables conservation (e.g., gene drives against invasives) or welfare (pain-free animals), fostering symbiotic human-nonhuman relations.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271)
- **Risks**: Unpredictable edits could exacerbate speciesism or environmental injustice, demanding global epistemic justice (e.g., diverse voices on anthropocentrism).[[5]](https://euroseeds.eu/app/uploads/2021/03/ege_ethics_of_genome_editing-opinion_publication.pdf)

Ultimately, these ethics compel a post-anthropocentric paradigm: justice as relational, recognizing non-humans' agency, welfare, and ecosystem roles, with genome editing as a catalyst for normative evolution.[[1]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271)
