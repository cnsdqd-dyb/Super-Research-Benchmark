Sensory interaction in digital installations—encompassing visual, auditory, haptic, proprioceptive, and even olfactory inputs—fundamentally reshapes the ontology of audience participation, shifting it from passive spectatorship to an embodied, performative co-creation of the artwork itself.[[1]](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715)[[2]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23808985.2024.2382752)

### From Static Object to Performative Process
Traditional art ontology posits the artwork as a fixed object for contemplation, with audiences as external observers. In digital installations, sensory interaction inverts this: the work becomes a dynamic *media environment* or *process*, constitutively dependent on audience actions for its realization. As Andrea Giomi argues, interactivity introduces a "performative ontology" where creation and reception fuse through real-time feedback loops. Sensory inputs (e.g., motion sensors detecting gestures, breath-activated interfaces) generate perceptible outputs (light, sound, projections) that vary with the interactor's intensity and duration, co-determining the artwork's form. This constructs participation as *enactive interdependence*: the audience's body is no longer distant but integral to the work's becoming.[[1]](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715)

### Embodied Presence and Deterritorialization
Sensory engagement fosters a deterritorialized *presence*, extending human anatomy via technology (technogenesis). Installations like Char Davies' *Osmose* (1995) use breath and balance sensors to immerse users in virtual forests, dilating corporeality through proprioception and psycho-acoustic feedback—presence emerges as "internal somatic awareness," reconfiguring the "here and now."[[1]](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715) Similarly, Mark Hansen's concept of "bodies-in-code" describes hybrid entities where sensory data (e.g., motion capture in Merce Cunningham's *Biped*) simulates kinaesthetic traces, blending physical and pixelated bodies. This ontology defines participation as *embodied simulation*: users inhabit affordances that match sensory predictions, minimizing perceptual dissonance and amplifying agency (e.g., interactivity as "the extent to which sensory information matches users’ predictions of action outcomes").[[2]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23808985.2024.2382752)

### Relational Agency and Shared Becoming
Participation gains a relational ontology, akin to Gareth White's "shared agency" as the "proper ontology of audience participation as artwork," but amplified multisensorially in digital contexts.[[3]](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/64138/1/51Astrid%20Breel%20PhD%20thesis.pdf) Audience actions—gestures altering projections, voices modulating soundscapes—create emergent narratives, dissolving creator-spectator binaries. In *Fractal Flesh* (1995–96), audience signals remotely control a performer's split-body projection, enacting "long-distance contact" and hybrid agency.[[1]](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715) This fosters immersion as a loop of sensory vividness, boundary alteration (e.g., virtual body ownership), and motivation, where users prioritize the mediated world, embodying roles that influence outcomes.[[2]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23808985.2024.2382752)

Ultimately, this ontology posits audience participation as the *ontological condition* of digital installations: without sensory interaction, the work lacks instantiation. It privileges processual, hybrid being over static essence, inviting ethical reflections on agency, embodiment, and the posthuman spectator.[[1]](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715)
