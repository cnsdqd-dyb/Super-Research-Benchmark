### Key Concepts and Context
- **Clustering coefficient**: This measures local cohesion (e.g., triangles among a node's neighbors), often indicating small-world properties or hierarchical structure in networks like mutualistic (e.g., plant-pollinator) or molecular ecological networks. Higher clustering can enhance robustness to perturbations by promoting local redundancy.
- **Network modularity**: Quantifies division into tightly connected modules with sparse inter-module links. In trophic networks (e.g., food webs), high modularity boosts stability against extinctions; in mutualistic networks, nestedness is more critical than modularity for robustness.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1188321)[[4]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20705861)
- **Biodiversity loss thresholds**: These refer to tipping points (e.g., extinction cascades or percolation thresholds) where sequential species loss causes network collapse. Predictions often use simulations of random/targeted extinctions, with structure (e.g., modularity, nestedness) forecasting the critical loss fraction (~10-40% in mutualistic networks).[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1188321)

### Evidence from Literature
- Studies like Thébault & Fontaine (2010) show modularity predicts higher robustness in trophic networks but less so in mutualistic ones (where nestedness dominates); no role for clustering coefficient.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1188321)
- Clustering and modularity correlate (e.g., high clustering often signals modular hierarchies), both aiding general stability/robustness, but are analyzed separately. No papers indicate clustering modifies modularity-based threshold forecasts.
- Related work (e.g., habitat networks) finds optimizing clustering raises robustness to habitat loss (a biodiversity driver), but not via modularity or species extinction thresholds.[[5]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0240940)
- In molecular ecological networks, shifts in clustering/modularity link to environmental stress (e.g., CO₂), implying stability benefits, but without threshold predictions tied to biodiversity loss.

Indirect links exist (e.g., both properties enhance small-world resilience), but predictions rely on modularity/nestedness independently.[[6]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41109-021-00426-y) Future models might integrate them for refined forecasts.
