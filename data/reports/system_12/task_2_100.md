**No specific "autonomy framework" exclusively dedicated to brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and phrased exactly as such appears in current literature or policy discussions.**[[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.09653)[[2]](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106952)[[3]](https://iapp.org/news/a/navigating-the-legal-and-ethical-landscape-of-brain-computer-interfaces-insights-from-colorado-and-minnesota)

However, several related **privacy-preserving frameworks, ethical guidelines, and emerging laws** emphasize user **autonomy** (e.g., self-determination, cognitive liberty, and control over neural data) while applying principles like **data minimization**, **explicit consent**, and **least intrusive practices** to limit privacy intrusions. These effectively ensure "minimum intervention" by collecting/processing only essential data, anonymizing it, and empowering users. Here's how key examples achieve this:

### 1. **Privacy-by-Design Frameworks for BCIs** (e.g., Systems-Engineering Approach)
   - A proposed **generic framework** for BCI privacy and cybersecurity integrates **privacy threat modeling, risk assessment, and engineering** from the outset.[[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.09653)
   - **Minimum intervention mechanisms**:
     - **Transparency and self-determination**: Treats BCI transparency as essential for user **informational autonomy**, allowing users to control data flows and understand intrusions.
     - **User-centric design**: Builds in features for **user control**, such as on/off switches and granular permissions, minimizing unnecessary neural data access.
     - **Extensibility**: Applies to diverse BCIs (e.g., medical vs. consumer), focusing on **privacy-by-design** to avoid over-collection.[[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.09653)

### 2. **Ethical and Policy Recommendations** (e.g., GAO, FPF)
   - Reports highlight the lack of a unified BCI privacy framework but recommend **consumer data controls** to enhance autonomy and limit interventions.[[2]](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106952)[[4]](https://fpf.org/blog/brain-computer-interfaces-privacy-and-ethical-considerations-for-the-connected-mind)
   - **Key protections**:
     - **Granular user controls**: Hard on/off toggles, selective data sharing, and **privacy-enhancing technologies** (e.g., encryption, local processing) to process minimal data.[[4]](https://fpf.org/blog/brain-computer-interfaces-privacy-and-ethical-considerations-for-the-connected-mind)
     - **Data minimization**: Collect only necessary brain signals; anonymize to prevent re-identification.
     - **Consent and oversight**: Rethink notices/terms for BCI literacy; use ethical review boards to ensure interventions are proportional.

### 3. **State Laws Protecting Cognitive Liberty** (e.g., Colorado, Minnesota)
   - **Colorado Privacy Act (CPA)**: Classifies neural data as sensitive, requiring **explicit opt-in consent** before collection and **anonymization** (data untraceable without extra effort).[[3]](https://iapp.org/news/a/navigating-the-legal-and-ethical-landscape-of-brain-computer-interfaces-insights-from-colorado-and-minnesota)
     - Minimizes intervention via **strict limits on use/sharing**, encryption, audits, and penalties (fines, lawsuits).
   - **Minnesota's neural rights bill**: Grants **mental privacy** and **cognitive liberty** rights, banning unauthorized collection or decision-making interference.[[3]](https://iapp.org/news/a/navigating-the-legal-and-ethical-landscape-of-brain-computer-interfaces-insights-from-colorado-and-minnesota)
     - Ensures minimum intrusion by mandating **informed consent** and prohibiting "consciousness bypass."

### Shared Context Across Approaches
- **Autonomy as core**: Users retain **decision rights** over their neural data (e.g., thoughts, intent), preventing "brainjacking" or surveillance.
- **Proportionality**: Like GDPR's data minimization, BCIs limit to "necessary" signals, using edge computing to avoid cloud uploads.[[4]](https://fpf.org/blog/brain-computer-interfaces-privacy-and-ethical-considerations-for-the-connected-mind)
- **Challenges**: No federal U.S. standard; risks from unclear ownership persist, but user-empowering tools (e.g., revocable consents) bridge gaps.[[2]](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106952)

These elements collectively prioritize **user sovereignty** while curbing privacy risks, evolving as BCI tech (e.g., Neuralink) advances.[[2]](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106952)
