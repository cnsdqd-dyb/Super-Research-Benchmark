**Yes, the lagged response of soil-vegetation feedbacks—often manifested as "memory effects" from soil moisture anomalies influencing vegetation growth and carbon fluxes—does amplify prediction uncertainty in global carbon budget models.**[[1]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192323003817)[[2]](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/)

These feedbacks involve soil conditions (e.g., moisture deficits or nutrient legacies) persisting over time, delaying vegetation responses like photosynthesis (gross primary production, GPP) and net primary production (NPP), which in turn affect net ecosystem exchange (NEE) and the broader terrestrial carbon sink.[[1]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192323003817)[[2]](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/)[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03325-5)

### Key Evidence from Research:
- **Role in Interannual Variability (IAV) and Model Uncertainty**: Many terrestrial carbon models struggle with IAV in carbon fluxes because they overlook these lagged or "memory" effects—the delayed impacts of past climate on current ecosystem states. For instance, neglecting memory leads to substantial errors in global NEE simulations, with R² improvements of 0.37–0.55 and RMSE reductions of 39–52% when included in deep learning models. This memory buffers climate effects longer for precipitation (soil-related) than temperature, varying by ecosystem and season (longer in non-growth periods).[[1]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192323003817)
- **Tropical Dominance**: In the tropics, lagged effects explain ~64% of NBE IAV (2001–2015), mainly via lagged NPP from foliar carbon and plant-available soil water states. These dampen concurrent climate impacts but are often underrepresented in process-based models, biasing sink projections.[[2]](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/)
- **Soil Moisture as a Key Mediator**: Soil moisture stress is a major driver of carbon cycle uncertainty across models, with legacy effects (lags) exacerbating discrepancies in GPP and land carbon uptake variability. Vegetation responds to soil changes with delays, as seen in grasslands and permafrost regions, where soil carbon lags behind vegetation.[[4]](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1539745)[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03325-5)[[5]](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/pubman/item/item_18428_1/component/file_18429/5820oa.pdf)

### Implications for Global Carbon Budget Models:
Earth system models (e.g., in CMIP or Global Carbon Budget assessments) often rely on concurrent climate drivers, underestimating these feedbacks. This amplifies uncertainty in predicting carbon sinks/sources, especially under climate change, where intensified droughts or warming could propagate via soil-vegetation legacies.[[6]](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/clim/27/9/jcli-d-13-00177.1.xml)[[1]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192323003817) Incorporating explicit memory (e.g., via data assimilation or advanced phenology schemes) reduces errors but remains challenging globally.[[1]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192323003817)

In summary, these lagged feedbacks heighten model spread and bias in carbon budget forecasts, underscoring the need for better representation of soil-vegetation dynamics.[[1]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192323003817)
