**No, traditional mutual aid networks generally do not sustain robust social support in communities with high floating populations.**[[1]](https://discovery.researcher.life/article/how-urban-communities-affect-the-social-integration-of-floating-population-evidence-from-guangdong-china/a6f355b068e93a8c83c3669dee6c3819)[[2]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11892263)

Traditional mutual aid—rooted in stable, long-term relationships like kinship, neighboring, and reciprocal exchanges in rural or settled communities—relies on enduring trust, shared history, and low anonymity. High floating (or transient/migrant) populations, such as China's urban hukou-less migrants (over 240 million as of recent reports), disrupt this by introducing frequent turnover, diverse origins, and temporary housing like dormitories or rentals.[[3]](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pubfiles/2268_1607_Liu_WP13ZL1.pdf)[[4]](https://d-nb.info/1205656626/34)

**Key challenges include:**
- **Weak ties and limited reciprocity**: Migrants form informal networks based on hometown ties, coworkers, or co-ethnics (e.g., "Zhejiang Village" enclaves), but these are shallower and less reliable than rural ones. They provide some aid (e.g., job leads, small loans) but falter for ongoing needs like healthcare or elderly care due to mobility.[[5]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/grow.70077)[[6]](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/9118782/file/9118784.pdf)
- **Social isolation and exclusion**: Studies in Guangdong and elsewhere show floating populations experience lower neighborhood social capital, reduced subjective well-being, and barriers to integration. Urban anonymity exacerbates this, with migrants less embedded in host communities.[[1]](https://discovery.researcher.life/article/how-urban-communities-affect-the-social-integration-of-floating-population-evidence-from-guangdong-china/a6f355b068e93a8c83c3669dee6c3819)[[7]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0147176725001075)
- **Context-specific evidence from China**: Research highlights how privatized urban housing erodes danwei-style (work-unit) mutual aid, leaving floating groups reliant on fragile personal networks amid hukou restrictions. Pandemic or crisis responses sometimes revive ad-hoc mutual aid, but it's not sustained long-term.[[8]](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/415e5ba7-78da-474b-b635-9fb72cd936f9/download)

**Adaptations and partial sustainability**:
- Migrants recreate scaled-down mutual aid via hometown associations (tongxianghui) or boss-led groups, aiding economic survival but not full social welfare.[[9]](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/2bf00582-0896-4791-bf71-16fd2b978907/Yuhan_Lan_Tesi_Finale.pdf)
- In some enclaves or crises (e.g., Madrid's migrant areas), grassroots networks emerge, blending traditional elements with modern logistics.[[10]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/02637758241242219)
- Formal interventions (e.g., community centers like "Xiao He's Homes") often supplement gaps.[[11]](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=147730)

Overall, while informal networks offer *some* support, they are insufficient and unstable in high-mobility settings, prompting calls for policy enhancements like inclusive urban services.[[12]](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-5309/15/7/1060)
