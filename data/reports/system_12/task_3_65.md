**Yes, geochemical anomaly identification does constrain the economics of urban mineral development, particularly when accounting for grade gradients.**

Geochemical anomalies—elevations in element concentrations signaling potential mineralization—are fundamental in early-stage mineral exploration.[[1]](https://pubs.usgs.gov/bul/1000f/report.pdf) They delineate targets for follow-up drilling, but their interpretation directly influences resource estimation, including grade-tonnage models that assess economic viability.[[2]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346818776_Grade-and-Tonnage_Models)

**Role of grade gradients:** These describe spatial variations in ore grade, often decreasing outward from a high-grade core (e.g., sharp drop-offs in deposits like gold or porphyry systems).[[3]](https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/segweb/economicgeology/article/96/2/367/22065/Application-of-Mass-Balance-Modeling-of-Sources)[[4]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1713443/000106299317003541/exhibit99-49.htm)[[5]](https://geoscience.nt.gov.au/gemis/ntgsjspui/bitstream/1/92041/2/ML30260_2016_A_Prelim_Econ_Assess_of_Maud_Creek.pdf) Steep gradients limit the economic pit shell or underground extent, as lower-grade margins may fall below cut-off grades after costs. Anomaly delineation methods (e.g., fractal, machine learning) refine these boundaries, constraining modeled tonnage and NPV.[[6]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169136818305237)[[7]](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-163X/14/5/500)

**Urban context:** "Urban mineral development" often refers to extracting resources beneath/near cities or "urban mining" (secondary recovery from waste), both facing amplified constraints like high land costs, regulations, and contamination risks.[[8]](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/4907/1/UrbanGeochemMappingFINALversion.pdf)[[9]](https://www.geochemicalperspectives.org/wp-content/uploads/v6n1.pdf) Urban geochemical mapping detects anomalies from legacy mining/pollution, but for development, imprecise identification risks overestimating viable grades, inflating capex/opex (e.g., remediation, relocation). Grade gradients exacerbate this, as urban heterogeneity (e.g., infrastructure) demands high-resolution data to avoid uneconomic low-grade halos.[[10]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264532935_Urban_Geochemical_Mapping_A_Review_of_Case_Studies_in_this_Volume)

In summary, poor anomaly definition leads to flawed grade models, potentially deeming projects uneconomic or causing failures; precise ID (via stats/ML) tightens constraints but improves viability assessments.[[9]](https://www.geochemicalperspectives.org/wp-content/uploads/v6n1.pdf) In urban settings, this is critical due to elevated risks/costs.
