**In ice flow models, the stress-strain relationship—primarily governed by Glen's flow law (strain rate ε̇ ∝ τ³, where τ is deviatoric stress and n ≈ 3)—describes the nonlinear viscous internal deformation of ice, while basal sliding adds a separate velocity component at the bed (often parameterized as v_b ∝ τ_b^m, with m typically 1–3 or higher for Coulomb-like friction).[[1]](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022JF006914)[[2]](https://www.antarcticglaciers.org/glacier-processes/glacier-flow-2/glacier-flow-ii-stress-and-strain)**

**This rheology is well-constrained by decades of laboratory experiments, confirming n ≈ 2.5–3.5 across typical stresses and temperatures in glaciers, though deviations occur at very low stresses (e.g., ice shelves) or in temperate ice (potentially more linear, n ≈ 1).[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11981940)[[4]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2309788121)**

**Changes in basal sliding (e.g., speedup from subglacial meltwater lubrication or terminus retreat) reduce basal shear stress τ_b, accelerate flow, and thin ice, which lowers the driving stress τ_d = ρ g H sinα (H: thickness, α: surface slope). The key constraint arises from Glen's nonlinearity (n > 1): as τ decreases, effective viscosity η ∝ τ^{1-n} sharply increases, strongly suppressing internal deformation rates and providing a stabilizing negative feedback.[[1]](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022JF006914)[[5]](https://essopenarchive.org/doi/pdf/10.22541/essoar.175222461.14851755/v1)**

**This limits runaway acceleration: for instance, in Greenland models, terminus perturbations cause initial sliding-driven speedup at outlets, but inland propagation of thinning ("diffusive" via longitudinal stress coupling and deformation) is modulated by rheology, yielding committed dynamic sea level rise (SLR) of ~6–33 mm by 2100, with primary uncertainty from sliding parameters (friction coefficient) rather than rheology alone.[[1]](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022JF006914)**

**In Antarctica, flow law uncertainties (e.g., ±20% in rate factor A or n variations) can rival or exceed sliding uncertainties for flow-driven loss, amplifying SLR projections by factors of 2–5 under ocean forcing, as softer rheology (higher A, lower n) enhances drawdown from sliding changes.[[6]](https://www.pik-potsdam.de/~anders/publications/zeitz_winkelmann21.pdf)[[5]](https://essopenarchive.org/doi/pdf/10.22541/essoar.175222461.14851755/v1)**

**Overall, the cubic stress-strain relation bounds SLR sensitivity to sliding perturbations by damping upstream deformation responses, but ongoing debates on fabric, anisotropy, and temperate ice rheology introduce ~10–50% uncertainties in centennial projections (e.g., ISMIP6 ensembles), emphasizing needs for better lab-to-field scaling.[[7]](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021RG000754)[[8]](https://tc.copernicus.org/articles/20/495/2026)[[9]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373915349_Uncertainties_in_forecast_surface_mass_balance_outweigh_uncertainties_in_basal_sliding_descriptions_for_21st_Century_mass_loss_from_three_major_Greenland_outlet_glaciers)**
