**Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) cause gastrointestinal (GI) toxicity primarily by inhibiting cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), the constitutive isoform responsible for synthesizing protective prostaglandins (PGs) such as PGE2 and PGI2 in the gastric mucosa.**[[1]](https://tmedweb.tulane.edu/pharmwiki/doku.php/nsaid_side_effects)[[2]](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547742)

These PGs maintain mucosal integrity by stimulating mucus and bicarbonate secretion, enhancing epithelial blood flow, inhibiting acid secretion, and promoting ulcer repair.[[1]](https://tmedweb.tulane.edu/pharmwiki/doku.php/nsaid_side_effects)[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884640) Inhibition disrupts this balance, leading to erosions, ulcers, and bleeding, with risks amplified by factors like age >65, prior ulcers, or high NSAID doses.[[4]](https://www.medsafe.govt.nz/profs/PUArticles/ReducingGIReactionRiskwith%20NSAIDsAndCox2.htm)

**Management centers on balancing COX selectivity—favoring COX-2 inhibition for anti-inflammatory efficacy while minimizing COX-1 suppression to preserve PG-mediated repair—with adjunctive strategies to support mucosal defense.**[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884640)[[5]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4346004)

- **COX-2 selective NSAIDs (coxibs, e.g., celecoxib) or relatively selective agents (e.g., diclofenac, meloxicam):** These preferentially target inducible COX-2 at inflammation sites, achieving ~80% COX-2 inhibition for analgesia/antipyresis while sparing COX-1-derived PGs.[[1]](https://tmedweb.tulane.edu/pharmwiki/doku.php/nsaid_side_effects)[[6]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9626023)[[5]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4346004) Clinical trials (e.g., CLASS, VIGOR) show 50-70% reductions in endoscopic ulcers and complications vs. non-selective NSAIDs like ibuprofen or naproxen, as PG synthesis in mucosa remains largely intact.[[7]](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2004.02198.x)[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884640) Dosing is optimized (e.g., short half-life drugs at lower frequencies) to limit peak COX-1 inhibition.[[5]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4346004)

- **Prostaglandin replacement for mucosal repair:** Misoprostol, a PGE1 analog, directly restores PG effects—boosting mucus/bicarbonate, blood flow, and epithelial proliferation—reducing ulcers by ~40-80% in high-risk patients, complementing selectivity by bypassing COX inhibition.[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884640)

This balance is not absolute: even coxibs exhibit dose-dependent COX-1 effects, and concomitant aspirin negates GI benefits by further blocking COX-1.[[1]](https://tmedweb.tulane.edu/pharmwiki/doku.php/nsaid_side_effects)[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884640) **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs, e.g., omeprazole) are often co-prescribed as first-line gastroprotection, suppressing acid to aid repair indirectly (superior to H2-blockers or misoprostol alone in high-risk cases), allowing selective NSAIDs' PG preservation to suffice.**[[8]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19192936)

**Overall hierarchy:** Avoid NSAIDs if possible (use acetaminophen); otherwise, prioritize coxibs + PPI in high-risk patients; eradicate H. pylori if present; monitor with endoscopy if needed. Emerging options like NO-NSAIDs enhance repair via nitric oxide without fully relying on COX selectivity.[[3]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884640) Note coxibs' cardiovascular risks (e.g., thrombosis from unopposed thromboxane), favoring non-selective NSAIDs + PPI in some cardiac patients.[[1]](https://tmedweb.tulane.edu/pharmwiki/doku.php/nsaid_side_effects)
