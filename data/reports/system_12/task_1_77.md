**Yes, Gibbs free energy minimization fundamentally constrains the scalable efficiency of circular economies through its intimate link to entropy production and the irreversibility of material mixing.**[[1]](http://web.mit.edu/ebm/www/Publications/RECYCLE%20IEEE%202008.pdf)[[2]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0921800998000986)

Gibbs free energy (G = H - TS) is minimized at equilibrium under constant temperature and pressure, driving systems toward states of maximum entropy (disorder) for given constraints.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_free_energy) In circular economies—which seek to close material loops via reuse and recycling—this equilibrium tendency manifests as a barrier: spontaneous mixing of materials (e.g., alloys, composites, or dispersed wastes) yields a negative Gibbs free energy of mixing (ΔG_mix < 0), primarily due to the entropy of mixing (ΔS_mix > 0), as ΔH_mix ≈ 0 for ideal solutions.[[1]](http://web.mit.edu/ebm/www/Publications/RECYCLE%20IEEE%202008.pdf)

To recycle, processes must reverse this by separating components, requiring a minimum work input equal to w_min = -ΔG_mix ≈ RT_0 ΔS_mix, where ΔS_mix = -R Σ x_i ln x_i (x_i = mole fractions).[[1]](http://web.mit.edu/ebm/www/Publications/RECYCLE%20IEEE%202008.pdf) For dilute streams (low x), this approximates RT_0 ln(1/x), which diverges as x → 0—making recovery from highly dispersed materials (common at scale) thermodynamically prohibitive without massive exergy inputs.[[1]](http://web.mit.edu/ebm/www/Publications/RECYCLE%20IEEE%202008.pdf)

**Scalability exacerbates this constraint:** As circular economies expand, materials cycle more times, disperse further (e.g., via wear, corrosion, or consumer use), and accumulate mixing entropy. Real separations achieve only 0.1–3.6% thermodynamic efficiency, with costs scaling logarithmically with 1/x or mixing entropy H, forming an "apparent recycling boundary" beyond which most materials become uneconomic to recover.[[1]](http://web.mit.edu/ebm/www/Publications/RECYCLE%20IEEE%202008.pdf) Perfect closure is impossible per the second law, necessitating entropy exports (e.g., to "wastebaskets" like landfills or oceans) and continuous exergy influx (e.g., solar), but even then, losses accumulate, limiting indefinite scaling without proportional energy escalation.[[2]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0921800998000986)

**Mitigations exist but are bounded:** Approaches like "circular thermodynamics" minimize internal entropy production via nested cycles and export minimal entropy, aligning ΔG → 0 through entropy-enthalpy compensation.[[4]](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-8954/1/3/30) Exergy analyses reinforce this, prioritizing high-quality energy to combat dissipation, yet thermodynamic myths of 100% circularity persist—real systems degrade toward equilibrium, capping efficiency (e.g., metals save 6–39% energy via recycling but incur irrecoverable losses).[[1]](http://web.mit.edu/ebm/www/Publications/RECYCLE%20IEEE%202008.pdf)[[5]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0921344925001971)

In essence, while feasible at small scales for concentrated/high-value streams, Gibbs-driven entropy barriers render fully scalable, high-efficiency circular economies thermodynamically constrained, demanding hybrid strategies with virgin inputs and waste management.[[6]](https://businesspf.hs-pforzheim.de/fileadmin/user_upload/uploads_redakteur/Forschung/INEC/Dokumente/Publikationen/The_actual_goals_and_limits_of_circular_economy.pdf)[[7]](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-thermodynamic-myth-paul-martin-9nfic)
