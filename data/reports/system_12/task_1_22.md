**Behavioral game theory (BGT) models in investment decision-making aim to balance rational equilibrium concepts with bias correction.[[1]](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2005-11626-003)[[2]](https://retos.ups.edu.ec/index.php/retos/article/view/6353/6023)**

BGT, pioneered by researchers like Colin Camerer, starts with standard game-theoretic frameworks—such as Nash equilibrium, where players are fully rational—and refines them using experimental data to account for cognitive biases, bounded rationality, and learning.[[1]](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2005-11626-003)[[3]](https://scispace.com/papers/progress-in-behavioral-game-theory-x3xm6cyu0v) Models like quantal response equilibrium (QRE) or cognitive hierarchy introduce "noise" or limited rationality levels, creating new predictive equilibria that correct classical rationality assumptions while preserving strategic interaction.[[4]](https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/gess/computational-social-science-dam/documents/internal/hnax/habil.pdf)

In investments, BGT applies to scenarios like herding, market crashes, or portfolio choices under strategic uncertainty (e.g., how one trader's actions affect others), blending rational predictions with empirical adjustments for biases like overconfidence or fairness concerns.[[4]](https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/gess/computational-social-science-dam/documents/internal/hnax/habil.pdf)

**In contrast, prospect theory (PT) parameterization explicitly amplifies loss aversion effects.**[[5]](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/prospecttheory.asp)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_theory)

PT, by Kahneman and Tversky, uses a value function with a loss aversion parameter λ (typically 2–2.5), making losses psychologically weigh about twice as much as equivalent gains relative to a reference point.[[7]](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf)[[8]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167268119301209) Parameter estimation from data "amplifies" this asymmetry to explain behaviors like the disposition effect (holding losers, selling winners) or equity premium puzzle in investments—it's not a balance toward rationality but an intentional deviation to fit observed risk-seeking in losses and aversion in gains.[[9]](https://nicholasbarberis.github.io/rt33c.pdf)

While PT can integrate into game theory (e.g., prospect-theoretic Nash bargaining),[[10]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268120304108) its core parameterization heightens loss aversion rather than equilibrating it with rational corrections. BGT better matches the "balance" description, especially in interactive investment contexts.[[11]](https://hammer.purdue.edu/ndownloader/files/36416058)
