**National Quantum Governance Frameworks with embedded accountability mechanisms.**

Quantum computing introduces a fundamental asymmetry: the urgent security needs driven by threats like Shor's algorithm, which can break widely used asymmetric cryptography (e.g., RSA, ECC) underpinning digital trust (such as PKI, digital signatures, and secure communications), contrasted with the current low trust in emerging post-quantum solutions due to their novelty, performance overheads, and incomplete migration.[[1]](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11095v1)[[2]](https://thequantuminsider.com/2025/05/14/quantum-computings-asymmetric-risk-means-one-break-is-all-it-takes)

This gap risks "harvest now, decrypt later" attacks, where encrypted data is stored today for future quantum decryption, eroding digital trust.[[2]](https://thequantuminsider.com/2025/05/14/quantum-computings-asymmetric-risk-means-one-break-is-all-it-takes)

The key responsibility mechanism addressing this is **national quantum technology governance frameworks**, often termed "responsible quantum innovation" or "quantum responsibility" approaches. These frameworks, analyzed in works like "A Quantum of Responsibility?" (comparing strategies from the US, EU, China, etc.), integrate accountability through ethical guidelines, risk assessments, standardization mandates (e.g., NIST PQC), migration timelines, and liability provisions for security failures.[[3]](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/91229818-1c58-49c1-bb77-0e274e0edc12/content)[[4]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44206-025-00205-7)

They work by:
- **Assigning clear roles**: Governments, industry, and researchers share responsibility (e.g., via RACI matrices in quantum-safe architectures).[[5]](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17034v1)
- **Enforcing standards and audits**: Bodies like NIST and CNSA 2.0 require post-quantum firmware signing and crypto-agility, with reporting for breaches to build verifiable trust.[[1]](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11095v1)
- **Legal and ethical oversight**: Emerging liability regimes hold vendors accountable for quantum-vulnerable products, incentivizing PQC adoption and fostering trust via "reasonable foresight."[[6]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-risk-regulation/article/quantum-computing-and-the-law-navigating-the-legal-implications-of-a-quantum-leap/3D6C2D3B2B425BB3B2FEE63BF42EB295)[[7]](https://iclg.com/news/23203-the-quantum-reckoning-law-s-next-frontier)

Complementary tools include Trusted Computing Bases (TCB) with post-quantum Roots of Trust (RoT) for hardware-enforced integrity, and sociotechnical governance for the QSC transition.[[1]](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11095v1)[[8]](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16974v1)

These mechanisms bridge the asymmetry by aligning incentives, ensuring proactive security, and rebuilding digital trust through transparency and enforceability.[[9]](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID4393248_code3428588.pdf?abstractid=4393248&mirid=1)
