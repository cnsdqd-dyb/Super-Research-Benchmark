# Phenotypic Plasticity in Invasive Species  
Phenotypic plasticity is the ability of an organism to change its traits or behavior in response to environmental conditions. Many invasive species exhibit high phenotypic plasticity, which can enhance their invasion success ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1629039/#:~:text=Clearly%20if%20phenotypic%20plasticity%20leads,19)). By adjusting to **harsh or novel conditions**, a plastic invader maintains or even improves its fitness where less-flexible species would fail ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1629039/#:~:text=Clearly%20if%20phenotypic%20plasticity%20leads,19)). Studies have found that invasive plants often show greater plasticity in fitness-related traits than non-invasive species ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1629039/#:~:text=to%20exclude%20other%20invading%20species,19)). In essence, this flexibility allows invaders to tolerate a broader range of environments, aiding their establishment and spread in new areas.  

# Incorporating Plasticity in Ecological Risk Models  
Ecological models used for invasion risk assessment – such as species distribution models (SDMs) – historically assume a fixed niche or trait set for a species. **Phenotypic plasticity parameters** aim to capture an invader’s adaptive range (e.g. wider climatic tolerances or trait adjustment capacity) in these models. Including such parameters means the model does *not* treat the species’ environmental responses as static. Research shows that standard SDMs, which usually ignore large-scale plastic responses, may misrepresent how an invader spreads ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3288080/#:~:text=distributions%20rely%20largely%20on%20species,of%20plasticity%20in%20explaining%20the)). When plasticity is explicitly modeled, the predicted invasion dynamics can change significantly ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3288080/#:~:text=plastic%20strategy%20,range%20dynamics%20may%20emerge%20by)). For example, a model that allows organisms to **adapt their physiology or behavior** can predict faster range expansion and greater resilience to environmental variability than a model that only uses fixed genetic traits ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3288080/#:~:text=plastic%20strategy%20,range%20dynamics%20may%20emerge%20by)). In short, adding phenotypic plasticity into the model’s parameters makes the simulation more *realistic* by accounting for the invader’s capacity to adjust to new settings.  

# Impact on Risk Prediction Accuracy  
Including phenotypic plasticity parameters generally **improves the accuracy** of invasion risk predictions. By accounting for an invader’s broader environmental tolerances, models can better identify areas at risk that a static-niche model might overlook. For instance, invasive species often expand into climates outside their native-range envelope. An SDM calibrated only on the native range (ignoring plastic niche shifts) may underestimate the regions the species can invade ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423003992#:~:text=The%20findings%20indicated%20the%20niche,a%20single%20model%20in%20a)). In one study, models that didn’t account for niche expansion **under-predicted** the suitable range of an aquatic invader, missing parts of the Southern Hemisphere that the species later occupied ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423003992#:~:text=The%20findings%20indicated%20the%20niche,a%20single%20model%20in%20a)). Incorporating the species’ full tolerance range or adaptive capacity would capture those at-risk areas, thereby reducing false negatives in prediction. Indeed, using all available information on an invader’s climatic tolerance is considered crucial for reliable risk mapping ([www.perspectecolconserv.com](https://www.perspectecolconserv.com/en-modeling-invasive-species-risk-from-articulo-S2530064420300250#:~:text=growth%20rates%29,the%20slider%20turtle%20Trachemys%20scripta)). By reflecting the invader’s jack-of-all-trades ability to thrive in varied conditions, plasticity-aware models tend to flag a more correct and complete set of vulnerable habitats.  

However, proper calibration of phenotypic plasticity parameters is important for **balanced accuracy**. If a model overestimates plasticity or uses occurrence data from non-established populations, it might predict invasions in many areas where the species never actually sustains (false positives) ([www.perspectecolconserv.com](https://www.perspectecolconserv.com/en-modeling-invasive-species-risk-from-articulo-S2530064420300250#:~:text=growth%20rates%29,we%20analyze%20how%20lack%20of)). On the other hand, underestimating plasticity can give a false sense of security by missing potential invasion sites. In practice, models that include realistic plasticity traits have delivered more robust forecasts of invasive spread under current and changing conditions ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3288080/#:~:text=plastic%20strategy%20,range%20dynamics%20may%20emerge%20by)). They remain closer to observed invasion patterns, as plastic invaders often persist through environmental fluctuations that would halt a non-plastic species. In summary, **phenotypic plasticity parameters enhance risk prediction accuracy** by making ecological models better reflect an invasive species’ adaptive potential – ensuring we neither underrate nor overrate the threat, but predict it with greater confidence.
