## Understanding Green Wave Phase Offset and Random Arrivals

**Green wave phase offset** refers to the timing coordination between consecutive traffic signals – essentially shifting the start of green at one intersection relative to another to allow a platoon of vehicles to travel through multiple signals without stopping. This coordination is very effective when vehicles arrive in platoons at predictable times. **Intersection capacity distribution** in this context means how the ability to serve vehicles (throughput per cycle or per hour) is utilized across an intersection or a series of intersections. The question asks: *What effect does adjusting that phase offset have on capacity distribution when vehicle arrivals are random (uncoordinated)*?

## Effect of Phase Offset Under Random Arrivals

When **vehicle arrivals are random**, there is no consistent platoon or timing pattern for the signals to “catch.” In such cases, changing the green wave phase offset has **minimal effect on capacity utilization** or throughput distribution at the intersection:

- **Random arrivals lack a platoon to align with:** If vehicles arrive uniformly at random times (as in a Poisson process), shifting the signal timing doesn’t create a green wave benefit. No matter how you offset the signals, vehicles will still arrive evenly throughout the cycle, rather than in a synchronized bunch. As a result, **the fraction of vehicles that arrive during the green phase remains roughly proportional to the green time split** (g/C) regardless of the offset ([www.civil.iitb.ac.in](https://www.civil.iitb.ac.in/~vmtom/nptel/574_SignalLos/web/web.html#:~:text=Good%20signal%20progression%20will%20result,may%20be%20determined%20using%20Equation)) ([www.civil.iitb.ac.in](https://www.civil.iitb.ac.in/~vmtom/nptel/574_SignalLos/web/web.html#:~:text=AT%20%20,Highly%20favorable)). For example, if the signal has a 50% green split, about 50% of randomly arriving vehicles will find a green light on average – whether the green starts earlier or later makes no statistical difference over time.

- **No significant change in average capacity or delay:** With random arrivals, a “perfect” offset cannot increase the intersection’s capacity beyond its normal level, because there’s no well-timed platoon to exploit. The **average number of vehicles that can pass through per cycle (effective capacity)** stays the same for any offset. In traffic engineering terms, the progression factor for delay/capacity is ~1.0 under random arrivals, indicating no improvement from coordination ([www.civil.iitb.ac.in](https://www.civil.iitb.ac.in/~vmtom/nptel/574_SignalLos/web/web.html#:~:text=AT%20%20,Highly%20favorable)). This means **average delays and queue lengths remain essentially unchanged by re-timing offsets** when arrivals are random.

- **Distribution of throughput remains statistically similar:** The **cycle-by-cycle variation** in vehicles served (or capacity used) is governed by random fluctuations in arrival numbers, not by signal timing alignment. One offset might shift *when* during the cycle vehicles get served (e.g. at the beginning vs. throughout), but it doesn’t change *how many* arrive in total over a long period. In other words, the probability distribution of vehicles served per cycle (or green phase) stays the same under different offsets if arrivals are truly random. You might still see some cycles where the green is not fully utilized (few arrivals) and others where a queue forms, but this randomness occurs regardless of offset choice.

## Summary 

**In summary, green-wave offsets yield little to no capacity benefit under random arrival patterns.** Unlike coordinated (platooned) flow – where a well-chosen offset can significantly increase the proportion of vehicles that arrive on green and thus improve capacity utilization – random arrivals mean each offset scenario is basically just a time-shifted version of the same random process. The intersection’s capacity usage will remain dictated by the green time ratio and stochastic arrival variation, not by the offset. Essentially, **with random (unsynchronized) traffic, adjusting phase offsets doesn’t meaningfully change the distribution of throughput or delays** – the green wave advantage disappears when there’s no wave to catch.

**References:** Good signal coordination increases the share of vehicles arriving on green lights, but under random arrivals this share stays roughly equal to the green time fraction ([www.civil.iitb.ac.in](https://www.civil.iitb.ac.in/~vmtom/nptel/574_SignalLos/web/web.html#:~:text=Good%20signal%20progression%20will%20result,may%20be%20determined%20using%20Equation)). In fact, traffic models classify purely random arrivals as yielding no progression benefit (platoon ratio ≈ 1.0, i.e. progression factor = 1) ([www.civil.iitb.ac.in](https://www.civil.iitb.ac.in/~vmtom/nptel/574_SignalLos/web/web.html#:~:text=AT%20%20,Highly%20favorable)), reinforcing that phase offsets have negligible effect on capacity in such conditions.
