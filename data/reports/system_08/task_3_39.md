# Impact of OD Distribution on Capacity Allocation

**Origin-Destination (OD) matrices** capture how many people or goods travel from each origin to each destination in a network. The **spatiotemporal distribution** of an OD matrix refers to how these travel demands vary across different locations *and* over time (e.g. which routes are busy and at what hours). This distribution directly influences how we allocate **capacity** in the network – for example, how many vehicles, transit frequency, or road lanes are provided on each corridor. In essence, where and when people travel (the OD pattern) determines where and when infrastructure or service capacity is needed.

## Spatial Distribution and Density Gradients

Spatial **density gradients** describe how demand or population density is distributed across an area – for instance, a city with a **steep density gradient** has a densely populated core that quickly drops off toward the periphery (monocentric city), whereas a **shallow gradient** indicates a more uniform or polycentric distribution of population and jobs. These differences lead to very different OD patterns and thus affect capacity allocation:

- **High Density Gradient (Monocentric)**: In cities with a strongly centralized pattern (e.g. a dominant downtown), a large share of trips originate in outlying areas and **converge to the city center** (and back) during peak times. This means OD flows are heavily concentrated along radial corridors leading to the core. Planners must allocate **high capacity** on those key corridors – for example, wider highways or high-frequency transit lines into downtown – to handle the concentrated demand. Paris is a classic example of a *“strong center”* city where population density (and travel demand) peaks in the core ([transportgeography.org](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter8/transportation-urban-form/distance-density-urban/#:~:text=a%20steep%20gradient%20related%20to,In%20several%20cases%2C%20population)). Such cities often invest in major trunk infrastructure (e.g. commuter rail, metro lines, multi-lane arterials) focused on the center. The downside is that this infrastructure may be underutilized outside peak periods or outside the main flow direction, since demand is so core-focused. Capacity allocation in this scenario requires balancing **intense peak loads** in one area against quieter conditions elsewhere.

- **Low Density Gradient (Polycentric or Uniform)**: In regions with a flatter density gradient or multiple centers, trips are more **dispersed across the network** rather than all funneling into one CBD. Los Angeles, for example, has relatively uniform population density without a single dominant core ([transportgeography.org](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter8/transportation-urban-form/distance-density-urban/#:~:text=a%20steep%20gradient%20related%20to,In%20several%20cases%2C%20population)). Here, OD matrices show many smaller flows criss-crossing between various subcenters or neighborhoods. Capacity allocation must be **spread out** to cover a wider area – a larger number of moderate-capacity roads or transit routes rather than a few massive corridors. In other words, a *dispersed demand pattern* requires a more extensive network to serve the same population ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-445X/13/1/77#:~:text=Whether%20denser%20land%20use%20can,of%20providing%20proximate%20public%20transport)). Planners might need ring roads, feeder buses, or multiple business districts served by separate lines. Each link or route carries less extreme volume, but there are more of them to cover all origin-destination pairs. The challenge in this scenario is providing sufficient coverage and connectivity; a lower-density city often requires **greater total network length or fleet size** to connect dispersed trips ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-445X/13/1/77#:~:text=Whether%20denser%20land%20use%20can,of%20providing%20proximate%20public%20transport)). On the positive side, no single corridor is overwhelmed to the same degree as in a monocentric city, so capacity can be right-sized more evenly across the network.

**Network Design Implications:** Under a steep density gradient, capacity allocation tends to be **radial and peaked**, focusing resources on main routes into the core. Under a shallow gradient, allocation is more **grid-like and distributed**, ensuring broader coverage. For example, a high-density core might warrant a subway line carrying huge volumes, whereas a suburban-dispersed area might rely on many bus routes or highways with moderate traffic. In practice, many cities combine elements of both – a strong downtown plus some secondary centers – so planners allocate a hierarchy of capacities (e.g. high-capacity trunk lines to downtown *and* orbital routes between subcenters). The key is that the spatial OD distribution drives *where* capacity is added: concentrated demand = capacity concentrated on few links, dispersed demand = capacity spread over many links.

## Temporal Variations and Peak Demand

OD matrices also vary by time of day and day of week. **Temporal distribution** of trips (e.g. rush-hour vs midday) critically affects *when* capacity is needed:

- **Peak Hours**: Work and school schedules typically produce **sharp peaks** in travel demand during morning and evening rush hours ([www.systra.com](https://www.systra.com/en/expert_insights/tarification-3-how-to-manage-peaks-and-falls%E2%80%AFin-transport-demand/#:~:text=Dealing%20with%20variations%20in%20demand,the%20transport%20systems%20and%20infrastructure)). If the OD matrix has strong temporal concentration (many trips all happening in a short window), transit vehicles and roads must handle a high volume in that period. Capacity allocation must therefore be planned for the **peak load** – e.g. scheduling extra buses/trains, adding temporary lanes, or simply building infrastructure wide enough for the rush. This ensures that during the busiest hour, everyone can be carried. However, the result is that outside the peak the same capacity may sit **underutilized**. For instance, a commuter rail line might be full at 8 AM but half-empty at noon. This mismatch poses a cost efficiency challenge: as experts note, providing enough service at peak times without having the system idle or extremely costly off-peak is a key balance to strike ([www.systra.com](https://www.systra.com/en/expert_insights/tarification-3-how-to-manage-peaks-and-falls%E2%80%AFin-transport-demand/#:~:text=Moreover%2C%20although%20there%20is%20a,providing%20travel%20possibilities%20to%20all)). High peak-to-offpeak ratios force operators to maintain large fleets and infrastructures for a few hours’ use per day.

- **Off-Peak and Dynamic Allocation**: In cases where the OD demand is more **evenly spread over time** (for example, in cities with staggered work hours or high all-day activity), capacity can be utilized more continuously. This means fewer vehicles sitting idle and potentially smaller design capacity, since the “design hour” demand is closer to the average. Transportation planners often use measures like a “peak hour factor” to account for how concentrated demand is. A lower concentration (flatter demand curve) lets us allocate less total capacity while still meeting demand, because that demand is time-distributed. In contrast, highly peaked demand requires **extra capacity reserved** for those peak periods. Strategies like congestion pricing, staggered shifts, or off-peak fare discounts are sometimes employed to **spread out peak demand** so that capacity can be used more evenly over time ([www.systra.com](https://www.systra.com/en/expert_insights/tarification-3-how-to-manage-peaks-and-falls%E2%80%AFin-transport-demand/#:~:text=Dealing%20with%20variations%20in%20demand,the%20transport%20systems%20and%20infrastructure)), reducing the need for overbuilding.

**Combining Space and Time:** The **worst-case scenario** for capacity planners is a strongly monocentric city with very peaky demand – this means huge flows all going to one place at the same time (think of a big downtown at 9 AM), which necessitates massive capacity that lies largely idle the rest of the day. In this scenario, you might see extreme crowding at peak (if capacity isn’t enough) and then underused trains or roads off-peak. Conversely, a polycentric city with diversified trip patterns and staggered demand can use its capacity more efficiently – different parts of the network are busy at different times, smoothing the load. For example, one suburban center might peak slightly later than another, or home-bound trips are less synchronized, allowing vehicles to be redeployed. 

## Key Takeaways for Capacity Allocation

- **Match Capacity to OD Patterns:** Ultimately, the spatiotemporal OD distribution is the basis for capacity planning. Transportation engineers use OD matrices to identify which routes need how much capacity. Heavily traveled origin-destination pairs get priority in capacity allocation (larger roads, dedicated transit lines, higher service frequency), whereas lightly traveled pairs might share capacity or get smaller-scale solutions. This ensures resources go where demand is.

- **High vs Low Density Context:** Different **density gradients demand different strategies**. In a high-density, monocentric context, expect to allocate big capacity on central corridors (and possibly plan for directional flows, like inbound vs outbound traffic management). In a low-density or polycentric context, plan for a **more extensive network** – more routes or distributed infrastructure – to reach many origins and destinations ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-445X/13/1/77#:~:text=Whether%20denser%20land%20use%20can,of%20providing%20proximate%20public%20transport)). The former optimizes for volume on select links, and the latter optimizes for coverage and accessibility.

- **Handling Temporal Surges:** Plan for peak demand but look for ways to **improve utilization**. This can include dynamic capacity (e.g. running more buses only at rush hour, reversible traffic lanes for the dominant flow direction) and demand management (incentives to travel at off-peak times) ([www.systra.com](https://www.systra.com/en/expert_insights/tarification-3-how-to-manage-peaks-and-falls%E2%80%AFin-transport-demand/#:~:text=Moreover%2C%20although%20there%20is%20a,providing%20travel%20possibilities%20to%20all)). The goal is to avoid wasteful over-capacity during lull periods while still meeting the peak needs.

- **Examples:** Many Asian and European cities with compact forms (steep density gradients) have invested in **high-capacity transit and roads into city centers**, because that’s where and when demand spikes ([transportgeography.org](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter8/transportation-urban-form/distance-density-urban/#:~:text=Notable%20differences%20in%20population%20density,higher%20densities%20at%20peripheral%20locations)). On the other hand, sprawling U.S. cities (shallow gradient) often require **multiple highway corridors and decentralized transit routes** to connect various sub-areas – the capacity is spread out rather than all in one place. In practice, Los Angeles’ relatively uniform density means no single transit line can serve all trips, so the city developed an extensive freeway grid and is expanding a web of rail lines. Paris, with a sharp center, funnels enormous ridership into a few core metro/RER lines. Each city’s capacity allocation reflects its OD distribution pattern.

In summary, the spatiotemporal distribution of OD matrices is the blueprint for capacity allocation: it tells us **where, when, and how much** travel demand must be served. **Different density gradients shape those blueprints** – a concentrated gradient focuses capacity into key arteries at certain times, while a flatter gradient spreads capacity over a wider area and potentially a wider timeframe. Effective planning requires aligning infrastructure and service provision with these patterns, ensuring that high-demand routes and times get sufficient capacity while avoiding over-provision elsewhere. By doing so, a transport network can operate efficiently, handling peak loads **without** excessive idle capacity in the off-hours, and serving the travel needs of both dense cores and spread-out communities appropriately.
