# Eigenvalue Decomposition and Asymptotic Population Dynamics

**Age-Structured Transition Matrices and Eigenvalues:** In age-structured demographic models (e.g. Leslie matrices), each age-specific transition matrix $A$ encodes survival and fecundity rates for each age class. By performing an **eigenvalue decomposition** of $A$, we obtain eigenvalues ($\lambda_i$) and eigenvectors ($\mathbf{v}_i$) that fundamentally describe how the population changes over time. In particular, the **dominant eigenvalue** – the largest eigenvalue in magnitude – governs the long-run or **asymptotic behavior** of the population’s growth and reproduction ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=%23%23%20%5B3%2C%5D%200.2596561%20,8302696)). The **Perron–Frobenius theorem** guarantees that for a primitive (irreducible, non-negative) matrix like a Leslie matrix, there is a unique real dominant eigenvalue $\lambda > 0$ with a corresponding positive eigenvector. This dominant eigen-pair determines the eventual growth rate and stable structure of the population.

## Dominant Eigenvalue – Long-Term Growth Rate 🔑

The **dominant eigenvalue** $\lambda_{1}$ represents the population’s **intrinsic growth factor per time step** (or per generation). As time progresses, the population’s total size $N(t)$ will asymptotically grow or decline **geometrically** at rate $\lambda_{1}$. For example, if $\lambda_{1}=1.10$, the population eventually grows about 10% each time step; if $\lambda_{1}=0.90$, it declines ~10% each step ([www.zoology.ubc.ca](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~bio301/Bio301/Lectures/Lecture18/OverheadsV0.html#:~:text=population%20will%20be%20multiplied%20by,the%20leading%20eigenvalue%20every%20census)). In fact, after transient fluctuations die out, the finite-rate of increase $\lambda_{1}$ is the **ultimate geometric growth rate of the population** ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=stable%20age%20distribution,an%20eigenvalue%3F%E2%80%99%20An%20eigenvalue%20is)) ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=%23%23%20%5B3%2C%5D%200.2596561%20,8302696)). This means that the ratio $N(t+1)/N(t)$ approaches $\lambda_{1}$ as $t$ becomes large. In continuous-time models, the analogous dominant eigenvalue gives $r = \ln(\lambda_{1})$, the **Malthusian growth rate**. Thus, the eigen-decomposition reveals whether the population will **grow without bound** ($\lambda_{1}>1$), **decline to extinction** ($\lambda_{1}<1$), or **approach a steady size** ($\lambda_{1}=1$) in the long run.

- *Example:* For a Leslie matrix with dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_{1}=0.9064$, the population’s size will asymptotically shrink by 9.36% each census interval ([www.zoology.ubc.ca](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~bio301/Bio301/Lectures/Lecture18/OverheadsV0.html#:~:text=population%20will%20be%20multiplied%20by,the%20leading%20eigenvalue%20every%20census)). Conversely, a dominant eigenvalue of $\lambda_{1}=1.096$ indicates a ~9.6% increase per interval, as found in one 3×3 age matrix where $\lambda_{1}=1.0962$ was the largest eigenvalue ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=%23%23%20%5B3%2C%5D%200.2596561%20,8302696)). No matter the initial population size, eventually the growth multiplier per time step approaches $\lambda_{1}$.

## Stable Age Distribution – Dominant Eigenvector 📊

Along with $\lambda_{1}$, the **right eigenvector** associated with this dominant eigenvalue (often denoted $\mathbf{v}_{1}$) gives the **stable age distribution (SAD)**. This eigenvector’s components are proportional to the long-run equilibrium proportions of the population in each age class ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=%23%23%20%5B3%2C%5D%200.2596561%20,8302696)). In other words, **asymptotically the population’s age structure converges to $\mathbf{v}_{1}$**, normalized to sum to 1. No matter how the population is initially distributed across ages, repeated multiplication by the transition matrix $A^t \mathbf{n}(0)$ will “wash out” transient differences. Eventually, the fraction of individuals in age class $i$ approaches $v_{1,i}$ (the $i$th component of the dominant eigenvector). This is the **stable age distribution**, reached when the population grows (or declines) in a self-consistent way each time step ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=This%20suggests%20that%20when%20the,Let%E2%80%99s%20explore%20this%20through%20simulation)) ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=%60stageDynamics2%20%3C,1%3A3%5D%2C%20c%28%27Young%27%2C%20%27Middle%27%2C%20%27Old%27%29%2C%20bty%3D%27n)). 

- *Interpretation:* The stable age distribution means each age class’s relative abundance becomes constant in the long run. For example, an eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_{1}=(0.61,\,0.24,\,0.11,\,0.03)$ (after normalization) indicates that eventually ~61% of individuals will be in age class 1, 24% in age 2, and so on ([www.uwyo.edu](https://www.uwyo.edu/dbmcd/popecol/feblects/lect14.html#:~:text=,0.03)). Once this stable structure is reached, **each age class grows at the same rate $\lambda_{1}$**, maintaining those fixed proportions over time. Any initial age imbalances will gradually disappear as the population’s age composition “settles” into the eigenvector pattern.

## Asymptotic Exponential Growth and Reproduction Function 📈

**Putting it together:** Eigenvalue decomposition allows us to express the population at time $t$ as a combination of eigenvector “modes.” For large $t$, **the mode associated with $\lambda_{1}$ dominates** all others. If we write an initial population vector as $\mathbf{n}(0) = c_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1} + c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2} + \cdots$ (where $\lambda_{1} > |\lambda_{2}| \ge |\lambda_{3}| \ge \dots$), then after $t$ transitions: 

\[ \mathbf{n}(t) \;=\; A^t\,\mathbf{n}(0) \;=\; c_{1}\lambda_{1}^t\mathbf{v}_{1} + c_{2}\lambda_{2}^t\mathbf{v}_{2} + \cdots. \]

As $t \to \infty$, the terms with $\lambda_{2}, \lambda_{3}, \dots$ become negligible relative to the dominant term (since $|\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}|^t \to 0$ if $\lambda_{2}$ is smaller in magnitude). Thus, **asymptotically $\mathbf{n}(t) \approx c_{1}\,\lambda_{1}^t\,\mathbf{v}_{1}$**. This shows explicitly that the population’s growth trajectory approaches exponential change at rate $\lambda_{1}$, and $\mathbf{v}_{1}$ dictates the **reproductive output in each age class**. 

In effect, the **population reproduction function stabilizes to an exponential form**: total population $N(t)$ grows approximately as $N(0)\,\lambda_{1}^t$ for large $t$, and the distribution of births across age classes becomes constant each time step. For a *reproducing* population, this means the **age-specific birth rates and contributions become predictable in the long run**. Each age class $i$ will produce offspring in proportion to its stable share $v_{1,i}$ and all classes grow at the common factor $\lambda_{1}$. The long-term **per-generation net reproduction is determined by $\lambda_{1}$** as well.  (In fact, $\lambda_{1}$ is the eigen-root of the characteristic equation equivalent to Lotka’s equation for the net maternity function in continuous-age models.)

## Summary of Asymptotic Effects 🔍

- **Exponential Growth/Decay:** The dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_{1}$ sets the asymptotic growth factor per time interval. The entire population (and any reproductive output measure) eventually changes by a factor of $\lambda_{1}$ each step ([www.zoology.ubc.ca](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~bio301/Bio301/Lectures/Lecture18/OverheadsV0.html#:~:text=population%20will%20be%20multiplied%20by,the%20leading%20eigenvalue%20every%20census)). If $\lambda_{1}>1$, the population’s reproduction leads to unbounded exponential growth; if $\lambda_{1}<1$, the population steadily declines (each generation not fully replacing itself); if $\lambda_{1}=1$, the population asymptotically levels off (replacement rate exactly one).

- **Stable Age Structure:** The right eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_{1}$ gives the stable age distribution ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=%23%23%20%5B3%2C%5D%200.2596561%20,8302696)). After enough time, the proportion of individuals (and thus proportion of births) in each age class stabilizes to these eigenvector values, regardless of initial conditions. The **transient oscillations or “age waves”** caused by starting away from the stable distribution die out as less dominant eigen-modes fade ([www.zoology.ubc.ca](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~bio301/Bio301/Lectures/Lecture18/OverheadsV0.html#:~:text=match%20at%20L404%20eigenvector%20does,at%20a%20stable%20age%20distribution)). The population’s reproductive output in each age class becomes a fixed fraction of total reproduction in the asymptotic state.

- **Reproductive Value (Left Eigenvector):** (As an extra note) The **left eigenvector** associated with $\lambda_{1}$ yields the **reproductive value** of each age class ([www.uwyo.edu](https://www.uwyo.edu/dbmcd/popecol/feblects/lect14.html#:~:text=,move%20from%20a%20given%20node)). This is another “reproduction function” interpreting how much contribution an individual of a given age will make to future population growth. While the right eigenvector gives the stable distribution of ages, the left eigenvector weights ages by their future reproductive output. At equilibrium, younger ages often have higher reproductive value (more future offspring potential) ([www.uwyo.edu](https://www.uwyo.edu/dbmcd/popecol/feblects/lect14.html#:~:text=,reproductive%20value%20of%20zero)). The concept of reproductive value further highlights how the eigen-decomposition links age-specific reproduction to long-term growth: ages with higher left-eigenvector values are disproportionately important to maintaining the dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_{1}$.

In conclusion, **eigenvalue decomposition provides a powerful lens on population reproduction dynamics**. It tells us that no matter how complex the age-specific birth and death rates are, the long-term outcome simplifies: the population ultimately grows (or declines) *approximately as a single exponential function* determined by the dominant eigenvalue ([theoretical-ecology.github.io](https://theoretical-ecology.github.io/code/agestructure.html#:~:text=stable%20age%20distribution,an%20eigenvalue%3F%E2%80%99%20An%20eigenvalue%20is)). Concurrently, the *shape* of the population (the age distribution) converges to the profile given by the corresponding eigenvector. These results explain the asymptotic behavior of population reproduction: after transient fluctuations, an age-structured population reproduces in a steady, proportional way – **growing or shrinking each year by the same factor ($\lambda_{1}$), with a fixed age-wise contribution pattern**. This stable exponential regime is fully characterized by the eigenvalue and eigenvector from the age-specific transition matrix, linking matrix algebra to population biology in a very direct way.

Over time, the influence of other eigenvalues diminishes, so the population’s growth and age structure settle into this eigenmode ([www.zoology.ubc.ca](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~bio301/Bio301/Lectures/Lecture18/OverheadsV0.html#:~:text=match%20at%20L404%20eigenvector%20does,at%20a%20stable%20age%20distribution)).
