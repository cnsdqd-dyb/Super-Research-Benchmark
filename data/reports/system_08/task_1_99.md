## International Human Rights Framework and Human Dignity in Germline Editing  

The primary normative framework guiding CRISPR germline interventions is **international human rights law**, which places human dignity at its core. Several global and regional instruments establish constraints on altering the human germline to ensure respect for human dignity and prevent abuses:  

- **UNESCO’s Universal Declaration on the Human Genome and Human Rights (1997):** This non-binding declaration frames the human genome as “the heritage of humanity” and asserts that genome editing must respect *inherent human dignity*. Notably, Article 24 charges UNESCO’s International Bioethics Committee with identifying practices “contrary to human dignity, such as germ-line interventions” ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/universal-declaration-human-genome-and-human-rights?TSPD_101_R0=080713870fab2000afe5da330dd990247b07219963797c8a953eb1d01ac26493a4c80cec7bae52cd0861f3597f143000ef31df69e4156ade3fa325a3be4393ce06ed39b4834fa0db70f0009f4e50c462429cd3ad6ed11317637021eeb6a33ce4#:~:text=The%20International%20Bioethics%20Committee%20of,line%20interventions)). In a 2015 report, UNESCO’s bioethics panel went further, urging a ban on hereditary genome editing – warning that altering genes passed to descendants could *“jeopardize the inherent and therefore equal dignity of all human beings”*, essentially reviving eugenics under the guise of improvement ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248990/#:~:text=editing,72)). This reflects a global consensus that **germline edits for reproductive purposes conflict with human dignity**.  

- **Council of Europe’s Oviedo Convention (Convention on Human Rights and Biomedicine, 1997):** This is the first legally binding bioethics treaty, rooted in human rights and dignity. **Article 13** of the Oviedo Convention explicitly prohibits any intervention aimed at modifying the genome of descendants. It allows genetic alterations only for preventive, diagnostic, or therapeutic purposes *“and only if its aim is not to introduce any modification in the genome of any descendants.”* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248990/#:~:text=Oviedo%20Convention%20is%20the%20first,using%20HGGE%20for%20reproductive%20purposes)). The Convention’s preamble emphasizes *“the need to respect the human being both as an individual and as a member of the human species,”* underscoring that the ban on heritable genome editing is grounded in protecting **human dignity** ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248990/#:~:text=preamble%3A%20%E2%80%98convinced%20of%20the%20need,An%20answer%20is)). In essence, this treaty (and its explanatory report) reason that germline gene editing could endanger human dignity by opening the door to designer babies or eugenic practices ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248990/#:~:text=which%20protects%20the%20interests%20of,57)). European Union law aligns with this principle as well – for example, the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights explicitly forbids “eugenic practices” and EU regulations bar clinical trials that would modify a subject’s germline identity ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248990/#:~:text=Within%20the%20EU%20%E2%80%98Charter%20of,trial%20is%20indispensable%20for%20the)).  

**In summary,** the **human rights framework** – exemplified by UNESCO’s declaration and the Oviedo Convention – establishes the normative constraints on CRISPR germline editing in the name of human dignity. These instruments treat the integrity of the human genome and the equality of all humans as paramount, thereby **prohibiting heritable genome modifications** that could violate human dignity or lead to new forms of discrimination ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248990/#:~:text=editing,72)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8248990/#:~:text=in%20this%20field%2C%20it%20is,reduced%20to%20objects%20of%20design)). This framework reflects a widespread ethical stance that human germline interventions must be tightly controlled to uphold the dignity and rights of both individuals and humanity as a whole.
