# Two-Tier Influenza-Like Illness Reporting System

A well-balanced **influenza-like illness (ILI)** surveillance system uses a two-tier approach that combines broad clinical reporting with targeted laboratory testing ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6417674/#:~:text=The%20surveillance%20system%20was%20found,type)). This design maximizes **epidemiological timeliness** (through rapid symptom-based case detection) while ensuring **diagnostic accuracy** (through confirmatory lab tests):

- **Symptom-based sentinel reporting (timeliness):** Health care providers (often in a sentinel network) rapidly report cases meeting a standard ILI case definition (e.g. fever and cough/sore throat) as soon as patients present. This **syndromic surveillance** captures trends in influenza-like activity in near real-time, enabling early detection of outbreaks or seasonal increases ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6417674/#:~:text=The%20surveillance%20system%20was%20found,type)). By relying on clinical symptoms, data can be gathered **quickly** from many sites without waiting for lab results, providing timely epidemiological indicators.

- **Targeted laboratory confirmation (accuracy):** A subset of reported ILI cases have respiratory specimens (like nasal/throat swabs) sent to laboratories for testing (e.g. PCR) to **confirm influenza virus infection** and determine the strain or subtype ([www.ecdc.europa.eu](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/seasonal-influenza/surveillance-and-disease-data/facts-sentinel-surveillance#:~:text=Physicians%20in%20sentinel%20networks%20may,reports%20of%20ILI%20and%20ARI)). These lab results validate the clinical reports by distinguishing true influenza from other ILI causes ([www.ecdc.europa.eu](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/seasonal-influenza/surveillance-and-disease-data/facts-sentinel-surveillance#:~:text=Physicians%20in%20sentinel%20networks%20may,reports%20of%20ILI%20and%20ARI)). Although lab testing takes additional time, it **ensures diagnostic accuracy**, refining the surveillance data and guiding public health responses (such as confirming an influenza outbreak and informing vaccine strain matching).

This **integrated sentinel system** provides the best of both approaches: immediate, widespread reporting based on clinical presentation, coupled with confirmatory testing for specificity. In practice, many national influenza surveillance programs and the WHO’s Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System use this design – *rapid ILI case reporting by sentinel clinicians, backed by laboratory confirmation at reference labs* – to achieve a timely yet reliable picture of influenza activity ([www.ecdc.europa.eu](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/seasonal-influenza/surveillance-and-disease-data/facts-sentinel-surveillance#:~:text=Physicians%20in%20sentinel%20networks%20may,reports%20of%20ILI%20and%20ARI)). Such a system allows health authorities to respond quickly to rising ILI trends while trusting that the data reflect actual influenza infections, effectively balancing speed and accuracy in influenza surveillance.
