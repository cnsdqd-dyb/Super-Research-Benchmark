# Service Degradation Strategy during Peak Traffic

During peak load (high traffic demand), systems often **gracefully degrade** service to maintain stability and performance. The **traffic scheduling** mechanism follows predefined rules to decide which services to slow down, reduce in quality, or temporarily restrict. In essence, the degradation strategy is determined by a combination of **policy-driven priorities** and **real-time load conditions**. Key factors include:

- **Quality-of-Service (QoS) Priorities:** Many networks classify traffic into priority levels, ensuring critical services are favored during congestion. For example, real-time or mission-critical traffic (like voice calls or emergency data) is given the highest priority, while non-urgent traffic (like large file downloads or backups) is marked lower ([support.riverbed.com](https://support.riverbed.com/bin/support/static/gq5grb960m7ced4rcm3bmf2dgj/html/gdag6njekpgaeh45em0oqk3hh0/sh-ug-html/steelhead/sh-ug-html/qos_profiles.html#:~:text=RealTime%E2%80%94Specifies%20real,example%2C%20VoIP%20or%20video%20conferencing)). The scheduler will degrade lower-priority services first – by delaying packets, reducing bandwidth, or even dropping some requests – to keep high-priority services running smoothly. This tiered QoS policy directly shapes **what gets degraded first** when capacity is limited.

- **Service Level Agreements and Business Rules:** The strategy is also guided by business decisions and SLAs. Services or users with guaranteed performance contracts (e.g. premium subscribers or emergency services) must be preserved under load, so other activities may be curtailed. For instance, an e-commerce site might prioritize interactive user traffic over background tasks: **non-critical processes** (like search engine crawlers or bulk analytics jobs) can be slowed or shed during peak hours to ensure real users experience minimal delay ([aws.amazon.com](https://aws.amazon.com/it/builders-library/using-load-shedding-to-avoid-overload/#:~:text=In%20fact%2C%20we%20can%20use,And%20if%20a)). In short, management policies determine which functions are considered *sacrificial* so that core services remain reliable.

- **Dynamic Load Thresholds (Load Shedding):** Modern systems monitor their utilization (CPU, bandwidth, queue lengths, etc.) and have thresholds that trigger overload protection. Once the system nears its capacity, the scheduler invokes **load shedding** – it intentionally rejects or trims new requests beyond a limit ([aws.amazon.com](https://aws.amazon.com/it/builders-library/using-load-shedding-to-avoid-overload/#:~:text=On%20the%20surface%2C%20load%20shedding,that%20the%20service%20replies%20before)). By shedding excess load, the system can focus resources on the requests it **does accept**, keeping their latency low and preventing a total collapse. This decision is automatic based on real-time metrics: when thresholds are hit, the system follows the configured strategy to drop lower-value traffic and **preserve overall throughput**.

- **Graceful Degradation of Features:** In software and distributed systems, engineers design *graceful degradation* policies for peak load scenarios. This means turning off or scaling back optional features when the system is stressed. For example, a video service might reduce streaming quality or disable high-resolution streams first if bandwidth is constrained, ensuring at least a basic service continues for all users. Similarly, web applications might temporarily remove non-essential components (personalized recommendations, heavy graphics, etc.) while keeping core functionality active. This approach “dims or turns off” non-critical features while protecting the **core service flows** and meeting essential performance goals ([www.designgurus.io](https://www.designgurus.io/answers/detail/how-do-you-implement-graceful-degradation-and-load-shedding#:~:text=Graceful%20degradation%20keeps%20your%20product,Load)).

- **Scheduling Algorithm Behavior:** The underlying scheduling algorithm in routers, servers, or base stations enforces the above policies. Algorithms like priority queuing or weighted fair scheduling inherently decide how to allocate resources under contention. They use configured weights/queues to give more share to high-priority traffic. Thus, during peak load, the scheduler may simply serve high-priority queues first and let lower-priority queues build up or drop packets. In telecom networks, for example, **guaranteed-bit-rate services** preempt capacity from non-guaranteed ones. In computing, thread schedulers might let critical tasks run before background tasks. The **design of the scheduler** – and the parameters set by administrators – ultimately determine how service degradation unfolds (who gets throttled vs. who continues unaffected).

**In summary, the service degradation strategy at peak times is not ad-hoc** – it’s determined by predefined *priorities, SLAs, and load-management policies* baked into the traffic scheduling system. High-priority and essential services are safeguarded by pushing lower-priority or non-essential traffic to degrade first. By monitoring load and applying these rules, the system can gracefully maintain core service quality during peak demand, sacrificing less important activities to prevent widespread failures or unacceptable delays. This ensures that even under heavy traffic, critical services remain available at the expense of reduced performance in less critical ones.
