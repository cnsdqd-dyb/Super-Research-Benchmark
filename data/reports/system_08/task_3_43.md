# Network Flow Assignment Convergence and Elastic Demand

## Convergence in Network Flow Assignment 
Network flow assignment (often in the context of traffic or transportation models) refers to distributing flow (e.g. trips) across a network until an equilibrium is reached. **Convergence** means the assignment algorithm has iterated enough that the solution is near equilibrium – for example, no traveler can significantly reduce their travel time by changing routes (Wardrop’s principle). Convergence is typically measured by a **gap** (like relative gap or differences in link flows between iterations). A low gap indicates that the flow pattern’s travel times and costs are consistent (i.e. “converged”). Achieving a tighter convergence (smaller gap) usually requires more iterations or advanced algorithms, increasing computational effort ([ajhassoc.com](https://ajhassoc.com/index.php/2015/01/02/convergence-criteria-for-equilibrium-traffic-assignments/#:~:text=iterations,doubling%20the%20number%20of%20iterations)). For instance, roughly **doubling** the number of iterations might halve the remaining assignment error ([ajhassoc.com](https://ajhassoc.com/index.php/2015/01/02/convergence-criteria-for-equilibrium-traffic-assignments/#:~:text=iterations,doubling%20the%20number%20of%20iterations)), illustrating how demanding fine convergence can be on computation.

## Elastic Demand vs. Fixed Demand 
**Elastic demand** means the total amount of flow (or trips) between origins and destinations is not fixed; instead, it varies depending on travel cost or time. In an elastic demand model, if travel times/costs rise, some trips may be reduced (people defer or forego travel), and if times drop, more trips might be induced. This is in contrast to **fixed demand**, where the number of trips between each origin-destination pair is predetermined and does not change with congestion or travel time. 

With elastic demand, the assignment problem becomes **coupled** with demand calculation: travel times from the network influence how much demand is realized, and that demand in turn affects network congestion. The equilibrium condition must satisfy both route choice optimality *and* consistency between travel cost and the number of trips (often interpreted as a balance of supply and demand for travel). In practice, models achieve this through an iterative feedback loop: they assign traffic, compute travel times, adjust demand based on those times, and repeat until demand and travel times are in balance.

## Impact on Model Complexity and Stability 
When demand is elastic, the convergence of the network flow assignment has a **significant effect on the overall model complexity** and the stability/accuracy of results:

- **Need for Iterative Feedback:** Because demand responds to travel times, an *outer loop* of iteration is required (assign -> update demand -> reassign) to reach a consistent solution. If the inner assignment step is not well converged, it feeds back inexact travel times, which can mislead the demand adjustment. In other words, incomplete convergence in the assignment can **“prevent, limit, or delay convergence of the outer loop”** (the demand-supply equilibrium) ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1337938#:~:text=loops%20is%20sufficient%20to%20not,destination)). The model might oscillate or stabilize slowly if demand is reacting to noisy (inaccurate) travel cost estimates.

- **Accuracy vs. Computational Effort Trade-off:** Ensuring a high level of assignment convergence typically improves accuracy in an elastic demand model but at the cost of greater computational complexity. A tightly converged assignment provides reliable travel times for the demand model, which is crucial for correct trip estimates. If the assignment is fully converged to equilibrium, the model’s outcome (flows, travel times, trip volumes) will be a stable equilibrium solution. However, achieving this adds substantial computational burden – many more iteration steps or more complex algorithms are needed, extending run time ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1337938#:~:text=destination,based%20assignments.%20A%20method%20using)). Conversely, using a looser convergence criterion (fewer iterations) simplifies and speeds up the model run but can produce errors in flow and demand forecasts (since travel times are a bit off). Modelers must balance these factors, seeking a convergence level “sufficient to not prevent or delay convergence of the outer loops, while avoiding excessive run-time from unnecessarily fine convergence” ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1337938#:~:text=loops%20is%20sufficient%20to%20not,destination)).

- **Model Solution Methods:** There are two general approaches to handle elastic demand equilibrium, each with complexity implications:  
  - *Iterative Equilibration:* This is the standard feedback process described above. Its complexity grows with the number of outer-loop iterations and the stringency of inner-loop convergence. If the assignment is poorly converged each time, more outer-loop iterations may be needed, effectively increasing complexity. Modern practice often uses **adaptive convergence criteria** – coarse convergence in early iterations and finer convergence in later ones – to reduce total runtime while maintaining accuracy ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1337938#:~:text=higher%20error%20shows%20from%20origin%2C,convergence%20limits%20in%20less%20time)). This approach acknowledges that very fine convergence early on is wasted effort (because demand will change anyway), but very fine convergence at the end is needed for stability.  
  - *Combined (Integrated) Solution:* Alternatively, one can formulate a **combined equilibrium model** that solves route flows and demand simultaneously. For example, Sheffi’s method adds dummy nodes/links to convert a combined distribution-assignment problem into a single extended network assignment ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/11/7/2167#:~:text=flow%20and%20network%20flow,precision%20solution)). In principle, this yields a high-precision equilibrium solution without needing iterative feedback (the solution inherently meets both route and demand equilibrium conditions). The trade-off is that this unified model is **larger and more complex**. The converted network introduces many additional links and paths (representing the elasticity of demand), which can significantly increase computation. In fact, early implementations saw efficiency issues because the number of paths per OD pair exploded, slowing down algorithms ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/11/7/2167#:~:text=However%2C%20the%20original%20GP%20method,Our%20numerical)). Specialized algorithms (e.g. path-based or gradient-projection methods) have been developed to handle this, but they underline that incorporating elastic demand directly makes the problem more complex to solve.

- **Stability of Results:** Under elastic demand, small errors from assignment convergence can lead to disproportionate changes in demand. For example, if travel time on a route is underestimated due to assignment not fully converging, the model might over-predict trips on that route (since it appears cheaper/faster than it truly would be at equilibrium). This can cause the next iteration to swing the other way (overloading a route then correcting), leading to instability. Thus, a well-converged assignment is important to maintain **stable and realistic demand estimates**. Research has shown a relationship between assignment gap and errors in origin–destination travel times ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1337938#:~:text=loops%2C%20while%20avoiding%20excessive%20run,lenient%20in%20early%20feedback%20iterations)). A higher gap (low convergence) means larger errors in travel times, which can misinform the demand model. Therefore, to reliably capture the equilibrium under elastic demand, a tighter convergence (lower gap) is usually required compared to a fixed-demand assignment.

## Conclusion  
In summary, **network flow assignment convergence plays a critical role in model complexity when demand is elastic**. High convergence precision yields more accurate and consistent travel times for the demand model, ensuring that the supply–demand equilibrium is correctly represented. However, achieving this precision increases computational complexity (more iterations, longer run times, or more elaborate algorithms). Under elastic demand conditions, one must strike a balance: enough convergence to produce stable, correct results without unnecessarily burdening the model with excessive computation ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1337938#:~:text=loops%20is%20sufficient%20to%20not,destination)). The effect is essentially a trade-off – better convergence improves model fidelity but makes the model **more complex** (in terms of solution effort), whereas looser convergence simplifies computation but can compromise the equilibrium accuracy. 

Practical modeling guidelines often recommend fairly tight assignment convergence criteria for final equilibrium with elastic demand, or the use of adaptive methods to manage complexity ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1337938#:~:text=higher%20error%20shows%20from%20origin%2C,convergence%20limits%20in%20less%20time)). By doing so, the model can reach a trustworthy equilibrium between network flow and demand without excessive runtime, maintaining both **accuracy and efficiency** in the modeling process.
