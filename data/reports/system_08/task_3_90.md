## Expectations vs. Experience: The Disconfirmation Mechanism

Our satisfaction with an outcome is largely determined by how the **actual experience** compares to what we **expected** during planning. In psychology and consumer behavior, this is explained by the **expectancy-disconfirmation model**. We mentally compare the expected outcome to the real outcome and evaluate the “gap” between the two ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_satisfaction#:~:text=Work%20done%20by%20Parasuraman%2C%20Zeithaml,of%20performance%20according%20to%20expectation)). If the experience **meets or exceeds** our expectations (a *positive disconfirmation*), we feel satisfied or even delighted; if it **falls short** of what we expected (a *negative disconfirmation*), we feel dissatisfied ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_confirmation_theory#:~:text=evaluations%20or%20judgments%20are%20made,to%20increase%20dissatisfaction)). In short, satisfaction rises when reality is better than or equal to the plan, and it drops when reality is worse than anticipated ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_confirmation_theory#:~:text=evaluations%20or%20judgments%20are%20made,to%20increase%20dissatisfaction)).

This **gap-based evaluation** is a fundamental mechanism behind satisfaction. Researchers note that an individual’s expectations form a baseline for judgment – the outcome is **“good” or satisfying only in relation to what was expected** ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_confirmation_theory#:~:text=evaluations%20or%20judgments%20are%20made,to%20increase%20dissatisfaction)). For example, if you plan a vacation expecting mediocre weather but end up with sunny days (actual experience surpasses expectation), the positive surprise boosts your satisfaction. Conversely, if you expected great service at a restaurant but received poor treatment, the negative gap yields disappointment. In formal terms, **satisfaction is the result of comparing expected rewards with actual outcomes** ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_satisfaction#:~:text=,purchase%20and%20use%2C%20resulting%20from)). When a product, service, or event outperforms one’s original expectations, it creates positive disconfirmation and **increases satisfaction**; when it underperforms, negative disconfirmation **decreases satisfaction (leading to dissatisfaction)** ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_confirmation_theory#:~:text=evaluations%20or%20judgments%20are%20made,to%20increase%20dissatisfaction)).

Our brains even encode this mechanism at a neural level. Studies in neuroscience show that the brain generates a **“reward prediction error”** – essentially the difference between expected and actual reward. Dopamine neurons fire signals reflecting whether a result is better or worse than we anticipated ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dopamine#:~:text=learning%2C%20dopamine%20also%20functions%20as,response%20in%20certain%20dopaminergic%20cells)). **A larger-than-expected reward triggers positive error signals** (associated with feelings of satisfaction or pleasure), while a smaller-than-expected outcome produces negative error signals (experienced as disappointment). This neural feedback loop reinforces the same idea: we feel satisfied when reality beats our plan, and dissatisfied when it doesn’t. In summary, **satisfaction evaluations arise from this gap** between what we planned to happen and what actually happened, with our mind (and brain) using the difference to judge how pleased we should be with the outcome ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_confirmation_theory#:~:text=evaluations%20or%20judgments%20are%20made,to%20increase%20dissatisfaction)) ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_satisfaction#:~:text=Work%20done%20by%20Parasuraman%2C%20Zeithaml,of%20performance%20according%20to%20expectation)).
