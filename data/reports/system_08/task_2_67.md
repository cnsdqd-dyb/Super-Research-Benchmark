## Balancing Sensitivity and False Positives in High-Risk MRI Screening

**Alternating MRI and Mammography:** High-risk women (e.g. BRCA mutation carriers) often undergo intensive screening because MRI dramatically improves cancer detection. However, MRI’s high sensitivity comes with more false positives and potential overdiagnosis ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12634791/#:~:text=However%2C%20prior%20studies%20reveal%20that,51)). A common strategy to balance these is an **alternating screening schedule** – performing breast MRI and mammography in staggered 6-month intervals rather than together annually. This regimen maintains vigilant surveillance while avoiding back-to-back tests that can compound false alarms. Studies show that alternating MRI with mammography achieves similarly high cancer detection but with *fewer false-positive recalls* (notably reducing BI-RADS 3 “probably benign” findings) compared to doing both modalities at the same time ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4340856/#:~:text=137%20women%20had%20screening%20which,3%20assessments%20among%20stacked%20exams)). This means fewer unnecessary short-interval follow-ups and biopsies, easing patient anxiety and burden without sacrificing early detection.

**Risk-Tailored Frequency:** Screening plans are also tailored to individual risk to prevent overdiagnosis. For example, **younger high-risk women** (often with dense breasts) may start annual MRIs early – MRI catches aggressive, clinically significant tumors and is less likely than mammography to pick up indolent lesions (like low-grade DCIS) that might never progress ([www.elsevier.es](https://www.elsevier.es/en-revista-radiologia-english-edition--419-articulo-screening-in-patients-with-increased-S2173510720300422?newsletter=true#:~:text=been%20evaluated,e)). In contrast, as high-risk women get older or after many clear MRIs, clinicians might **dial back MRI frequency** (e.g. moving to biennial MRIs or continuing only mammograms) to avoid detecting very slow-growing lesions that would not threaten health ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12634791/#:~:text=overall%20BC%20incidence%20in%20the,refine%20risk%20stratification%20seems%20valuable)). In fact, evidence suggests maintaining *some* imaging (even at a reduced interval) beyond age 50–60 can catch meaningful cancers early while limiting cumulative false positives and overtreatment ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12634791/#:~:text=However%2C%20prior%20studies%20reveal%20that,Despite%20our)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12634791/#:~:text=overall%20BC%20incidence%20in%20the,refine%20risk%20stratification%20seems%20valuable)). This personalized, risk-adjusted approach – using MRI when and where it yields the most benefit, and spacing or pausing it when risks outweigh rewards – helps **maximize lifesaving detections while minimizing false positives and overdiagnosis** in high-risk breast cancer screening programs.
