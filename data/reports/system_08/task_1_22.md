# Behavioral Game Theory vs Prospect Theory in Investment Decisions

## Balancing Rational Equilibrium with Bias Corrections  
**Behavioral Game Theory (BGT)** models of investment decisions aim to integrate human biases into traditional game-theoretic frameworks. Rather than assuming perfectly rational investors, BGT includes psychological factors (e.g. social preferences, fairness, framing effects) that classical models ignore ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_game_theory#:~:text=Behavioral%20game%20theory%20seeks%20to,development%20of%20the%20last%20three)). In practice, this means the model “balances” rational equilibrium reasoning with adjustments for known biases. For example, standard game theory would predict purely self-interested or risk-neutral behavior, but real investors exhibit biases like **fairness concerns** or changing risk appetite after gains/losses. BGT accounts for such deviations – evidence shows people become risk-seeking after losses (a “break-even” impulse) or more daring with house money after wins, biases **not captured by traditional models** ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_game_theory#:~:text=behavior,make%20a%20series%20of%20decisions)). By correcting for these biases within an equilibrium framework, a behavioral game theory model yields predictions closer to observed behavior while still maintaining a logical consistency. In short, it tries to preserve the structure of rational strategy equilibrium **but with bias corrections** that reflect how investors actually behave.  

## Prospect Theory and Amplified Loss Aversion  
In contrast, **Prospect Theory** explicitly builds bias into the decision model – often **amplifying loss aversion** effects. Prospect theory posits that people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point and feel losses more acutely than equal-sized gains ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_theory#:~:text=Prospect%20theory%20stems%20from%20loss,any%20absolute%20measure%20of%20utility)). When an investment model is parameterized this way, the value function is steeper for losses than for gains, meaning a $100 loss “hurts” significantly more than a $100 gain pleases. In fact, empirically **losses tend to be weighted about twice as much as equivalent gains** in decision-making ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_aversion#:~:text=When%20defined%20in%20terms%20of,proposed%20by%20%208%20Amos)). This parameterization makes loss aversion a dominant force: investors become overly sensitive to downturns and potential losses. As a result, prospect theory–based models often predict behaviors like excessive caution or reluctance to realize losses – **losses loom larger than gains** by design. For instance, myopic loss aversion can lead investors to overreact to short-term stock price dips and adopt overly conservative strategies out of fear ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_theory#:~:text=When%20referring%20to%20investment%20decisions%2C,loss%20potentially%20influences%20the%20broker)). In summary, using prospect theory in an investment decision model firmly embeds and even heightens biases (especially loss aversion), whereas a behavioral game theory approach tries to moderate those biases within a rational equilibrium context.
