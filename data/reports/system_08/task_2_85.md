# Lagged Soil–Vegetation Feedback and Carbon Budget Uncertainty

**Yes.** A delayed (lagged) response in soil–vegetation feedback loops tends to **increase the uncertainty** in global carbon budget model predictions. In Earth system and carbon-cycle models, soil and vegetation processes interact over different timescales, and any time-lag in their feedback means the ecosystem’s carbon response isn’t instantaneous. This **time lag** makes it harder for models to predict how the land will absorb or release carbon, thereby **amplifying the uncertainty** in future carbon budget and climate projections ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/index.html#:~:text=component%20of%20the%20global%20atmospheric,principle%20to%20any%20land%20biosphere)) ([www.carbonbrief.org](https://www.carbonbrief.org/analysis-how-carbon-cycle-feedbacks-could-make-global-warming-worse/#:~:text=Yet%20climate%20change%20also%20depends,%E2%80%93%20than%20is%20commonly%20thought)). 

## Why Lagged Responses Increase Uncertainty

- **Delayed Ecosystem Effects:** Climate anomalies or CO₂ changes can have **linger­ing effects** on vegetation growth and soil carbon release. For example, an extreme drought may reduce plant growth not only in that year but also in subsequent years while ecosystems recover. Similarly, warming can trigger **gradual soil carbon loss** over decades (e.g. permafrost thaw or deep soil decomposition), continuing even after temperatures stabilize. These **lagged effects** mean current changes set in motion carbon fluxes that unfold later, making outcomes more **path-dependent** and harder to predict.

- **Model Divergence:** If models differ in how they represent or parameterize these lagged processes, their predictions can **diverge widely**. A study found that introducing more detailed soil and vegetation processes (e.g. layered soil carbon or microbial dynamics) – which inherently include slower, lagged responses – led to **much greater variability** in projected soil carbon change compared to a simple model ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04526-9#:~:text=different%20models%20to%20projections%20of,complexity%20and%20the%20need%20to)). The complex models spanned both **strong carbon losses or gains** under warming (positive or negative feedback), whereas a simpler model always showed carbon loss (positive feedback) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04526-9#:~:text=different%20models%20to%20projections%20of,increase%20confidence%20in%20soil%20C)). In other words, accounting for realistic lags and feedback complexity **widened the range** of possible outcomes, increasing uncertainty.

- **Incomplete Knowledge:** Our limited understanding of how concurrent vs. lagged effects share control over ecosystem carbon fluxes further **hampers accurate predictions** ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/index.html#:~:text=on%20inter,induced%20lagged%20changes%20to)). One analysis of tropical carbon balance noted that uncertainty about the **relative contributions of immediate vs. delayed responses** “stifles efforts” to understand ecosystem carbon sensitivity to climate ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/index.html#:~:text=on%20inter,induced%20lagged%20changes%20to)). This makes it challenging for global models to get the **right timing and magnitude** of land carbon changes. If a model assumes vegetation and soils respond quickly, it might underestimate ongoing emissions (or uptake) that occur with a delay – and vice versa. Such differences translate into **forecast uncertainty** for the global carbon budget.

## Impact on Global Carbon Budget Projections

Because land carbon uptake and release is a major factor in how much atmospheric CO₂ rises, **lagged feedbacks on land can amplify uncertainty in climate outcomes**. In fact, scientists identify uncertainties in carbon-cycle feedbacks (largely from land processes like vegetation growth and soil respiration) as **one of the dominant sources of spread** in climate projections ([www.carbonbrief.org](https://www.carbonbrief.org/analysis-how-carbon-cycle-feedbacks-could-make-global-warming-worse/#:~:text=These%20uncertainties%20are%20%E2%80%9Cone%20of,different%20model%20projections%2C%20according%20to)). When these feedback uncertainties are accounted for, the world **could warm much more – or a bit less – than commonly thought** under the same emissions scenario ([www.carbonbrief.org](https://www.carbonbrief.org/analysis-how-carbon-cycle-feedbacks-could-make-global-warming-worse/#:~:text=Yet%20climate%20change%20also%20depends,%E2%80%93%20than%20is%20commonly%20thought)). In practical terms, this means the allowable carbon budget for a given climate target becomes **harder to pin down**. Some models might project a strong lagged soil carbon release (shrinking the land sink and leaving more CO₂ in the air), while others might project sustained uptake by ecosystems – leading to different predictions of atmospheric CO₂ trajectory.

Researchers emphasize that we need to **explicitly include and constrain** these lagged soil–vegetation effects to improve model accuracy ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/index.html#:~:text=annual%20variations%20in%20foliar%20C,the%20terrestrial%20land%20C%20sink)). By doing so, models can better capture the true behavior of the land carbon sink as climate changes, rather than giving a false sense of certainty with an oversimplified immediate response. In summary, the **time-lagged feedback between soils, vegetation, and climate does increase prediction uncertainty** in global carbon budget models, since it adds complexity and memory to the system that models must get right to reliably forecast future CO₂ levels and climate outcomes ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/17/6393/2020/index.html#:~:text=on%20inter,induced%20lagged%20changes%20to)) ([www.carbonbrief.org](https://www.carbonbrief.org/analysis-how-carbon-cycle-feedbacks-could-make-global-warming-worse/#:~:text=Yet%20climate%20change%20also%20depends,%E2%80%93%20than%20is%20commonly%20thought)).
