# Attribution Weight in Purchase Conversion Models

## Understanding Attribution Weight in Conversion Models  
In marketing **attribution modeling**, the *attribution weight* refers to how much credit a particular marketing touchpoint or channel gets for a purchase conversion. Purchase conversion models aim to allocate credit for a sale across the various marketing interactions (ads, emails, searches, etc.) that preceded it. The goal is to determine **how each marketing effort contributed** to the conversion, and assign a weight (percentage of credit) accordingly. In practice, attribution weights are determined by the attribution model being used – either **rule-based models** with preset weighting rules or **data-driven models** that use statistical analysis to infer each channel’s contribution.

## Rule-Based Attribution Models (Heuristic Weights)  
Traditional attribution models use simple rules to assign weights to marketing touchpoints. These models don’t learn from data; instead, they apply a fixed scheme for credit distribution. Some common rule-based models include: 

- **Last-Click Attribution:** *100% of the credit* goes to the last marketing touchpoint before conversion. For example, if the customer’s final step was clicking a Google Ad, that ad gets all the credit.  
- **First-Click Attribution:** *100% of the credit* goes to the first touchpoint that started the customer journey (e.g. an initial Facebook ad click).  
- **Linear Attribution:** All touchpoints share credit equally. If a customer had 4 marketing interactions before buying, each gets 25% of the credit ([medium.com](https://medium.com/data-science/data-driven-marketing-attribution-1a28d2e613a0#:~:text=,to%20the%20touches%20in%20between)).  
- **Time-Decay Attribution:** Touchpoints closer in time to the conversion get more weight than earlier ones. Credit decays exponentially for older interactions, reflecting the assumption that recent touches influence the purchase decision more.  
- **Position-Based (U-Shaped) Attribution:** A large portion of credit is split between the first and last interactions (e.g. 40% each) and the remainder is divided among the middle touchpoints ([medium.com](https://medium.com/data-science/data-driven-marketing-attribution-1a28d2e613a0#:~:text=,to%20the%20touches%20in%20between)). This emphasizes the touch that kicked off the journey and the one that closed the sale.  

In these heuristic models, the attribution weight is basically *determined by the rule definition*. For instance, in a linear model we **decide upfront** that every channel gets equal weight, whereas in time-decay we **choose a decay rate** that dictates weights over time. The advantage of rule-based weighting is simplicity, but it may not reflect the true influence of each marketing effort because it ignores actual performance data.

## Data-Driven Attribution Models (Statistical/Algorithmic Weights)  
Modern conversion models determine attribution weights using data and algorithms rather than fixed rules. These **data-driven attribution** approaches evaluate how each marketing touchpoint actually impacted conversions by analyzing user journey data. In essence, the model will give more credit to channels or ads that **demonstrably increase the likelihood of conversion**, and less credit to those that don’t ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6394265#:~:text=kattint%C3%A1sokat%20%C3%A9s%20a%20vide%C3%B3kkal%20v%C3%A9gzett,tulajdon%C3%ADt%20ezeknek%20az%20%C3%A9rt%C3%A9kes%20interakci%C3%B3knak)). The weight of a marketing effect is inferred from patterns in the data, often using one of the following methods or frameworks:

- **Comparative Path Analysis:** Data-driven models (like Google Ads’ *data-driven attribution*) compare the journeys of users who converted with those who didn’t. By examining sequences of interactions, the model identifies which steps tend to drive conversions ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6394265#:~:text=kattint%C3%A1sokat%20%C3%A9s%20a%20vide%C3%B3kkal%20v%C3%A9gzett,tulajdon%C3%ADt%20ezeknek%20az%20%C3%A9rt%C3%A9kes%20interakci%C3%B3knak)). If including a certain ad or channel in the path correlates with a higher conversion rate, the model will assign that touchpoint a higher weight. Essentially, it uses your historical conversion data to calculate each interaction’s *actual contribution* to conversions ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6259715?hl=en-ZA&ref_topic=10556777#:~:text=Image%3A%20Data,driven%20attribution)) ([support.google.com](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6394265#:~:text=kattint%C3%A1sokat%20%C3%A9s%20a%20vide%C3%B3kkal%20v%C3%A9gzett,tulajdon%C3%ADt%20ezeknek%20az%20%C3%A9rt%C3%A9kes%20interakci%C3%B3knak)). Over time, the algorithm learns how much each marketing touch increases conversion probability and adjusts weights accordingly. This is often implemented with machine learning or regression models behind the scenes, and it yields a custom attribution model tailored to your data.

- **Markov Chain (Removal Effect) Attribution:** A Markov chain model treats the customer journey as a sequence of states (channels) leading to conversion or drop-off. The *attribution weight* is determined by simulating what happens if a given channel is removed from the path. Specifically, one calculates the **removal effect** of each marketing channel – the percentage of conversions that would be lost if that channel didn’t exist ([adequate.digital](https://adequate.digital/en/markov-chain-attribution-modeling-complete-guide/#:~:text=The%20removal%20effect%20represents%20the,touchpoint)). For example, if removing the “Email” touchpoint from all journeys causes a significant drop in overall conversion probability, then “Email” had a high contribution. The removal effect for each channel is normalized to sum to 100%, yielding each channel’s share of credit ([adequate.digital](https://adequate.digital/en/markov-chain-attribution-modeling-complete-guide/#:~:text=The%20removal%20effect%20represents%20the,touchpoint)). In short, the weight is based on how the absence of that channel would affect conversions. This Markov approach captures the incremental value of each touchpoint in the context of the entire journey.

- **Shapley Value (Game-Theoretic) Attribution:** This approach borrows from cooperative game theory to fairly distribute credit among all players (marketing channels) that “cooperate” to produce a conversion. Each channel’s attribution weight is its **average marginal contribution** to the conversion outcome, computed across all possible combinations of channels ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=order,difference%20of%20gains%20when%20a)). In practical terms, we evaluate how much each channel increases conversion when added to a given set of other channels. The Shapley value formula looks at every order in which a customer could encounter channels and averages the incremental benefit of each channel being part of the journey ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=order,difference%20of%20gains%20when%20a)) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/data-science/data-driven-marketing-attribution-1a28d2e613a0#:~:text=match%20at%20L148%20Now%20that,total%20payoff)). This yields a weight for each channel that reflects its true contribution in all scenarios. A channel that consistently adds a big lift to conversion when present will get a larger Shapley value. One advantage of Shapley value attribution is that it satisfies fairness properties (efficiency, symmetry, null player, etc.), ensuring the total credit is fully allocated and channels that add no value get zero credit ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=Shapley%20value%20has%20become%20popular,allocation%20which%20satisfies%20different%20axioms)) ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=match%20at%20L370%20These%20properties,define%20as%20a%20fair%20attribution)). In fact, Google Analytics’ **data-driven attribution** model historically used a Shapley value approach for multi-touch attribution ([medium.com](https://medium.com/data-science/data-driven-marketing-attribution-1a28d2e613a0#:~:text=match%20at%20L94%20,uses%20it%20for%20channel%20attribution)). By using Shapley values, marketers can **fairly divide credit for sales** between channels based on each channel’s individual contribution to the overall conversion payoff ([medium.com](https://medium.com/data-science/data-driven-marketing-attribution-1a28d2e613a0#:~:text=match%20at%20L85%20the%20Shapley,contribution%20to%20the%20total%20payoff)).

- **Machine Learning Models:** Some advanced attribution systems train machine learning models (like logistic regression, decision trees, or neural networks) to predict conversion probability from a user’s sequence of marketing touches. The feature importances or model coefficients can then be interpreted as attribution weights. For example, a logistic regression might assign a higher weight (coefficient) to “Paid Search ad click” than to “Display ad impression” if it finds that searches are more strongly associated with conversion. Similarly, tree-based models might compute SHAP values (a concept related to Shapley) for each feature to indicate how much that feature contributed to the predicted conversion for each user. These methods are essentially another way to derive data-driven weights, aligning with the idea that each marketing effect’s weight corresponds to its **incremental lift** in conversion likelihood.

## Key Considerations in Determining Weights  
No matter the method, **attribution weights are essentially determined by how much a marketing touch influences conversion outcomes in the model’s eyes.** In rule-based models, this is simplistic (based on assumption rather than data). In data-driven models, it’s grounded in analysis: either looking at **conversion rate deltas** with/without the channel, simulating journeys with channels added or removed, or distributing credit in a way that every channel’s marginal impact is recognized. All these approaches ensure that the **sum of weights across all marketing touches equals 100%** of the conversion credit, so the full value of each sale is accounted for by the various marketing contributions.

It’s important to note that data-driven attribution models rely on patterns in observational data (who saw what and who converted). They attempt to infer causality but are fundamentally **correlation-based** ([adequate.digital](https://adequate.digital/en/markov-chain-attribution-modeling-complete-guide/#:~:text=You%20should%20also%20remember%20that,as%C2%A0conversion%20lift%20methodology%C2%A0does)). If a certain channel often precedes conversion, the model may give it weight assuming it influenced the outcome – but this might misattribute credit if, for instance, some channels tend to appear at the end of a journey without truly swaying the decision. The **gold standard** for determining true causal attribution is controlled experiments (like A/B tests or holdout tests) ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=1.%20A%2FB)), where one can directly measure lift from a marketing intervention. However, experiments are not always feasible for every channel all the time. Hence, conversion models use the best available data-driven heuristics to estimate weights, and marketers should treat the results as informed estimates of contribution rather than absolute truth. 

In summary, **the attribution weight of marketing effects** in purchase conversion models is determined either by predefined rules (allocating credit in a fixed way) or by algorithmic analysis of consumer journey data (allocating credit proportional to each touchpoint’s observed impact on conversion). Advanced models like Markov chains and Shapley value attribution explicitly quantify each channel’s incremental contribution to the conversion, providing a more evidence-based distribution of credit than naive first- or last-touch rules. The end result is a set of weights that tell you, for a given conversion, how much each marketing activity contributed to making that sale happen. These weights guide marketers in understanding the **relative effectiveness** of different channels and in making budget allocation decisions for future campaigns.
