# Suppressing Motion Artifacts in Low-Power Wearable Monitors

Wearable vital-sign monitors face a key challenge: **motion artifacts** can corrupt signals like ECG or PPG (photoplethysmography), making it hard to extract heart rate, oxygen saturation, or other vitals. Simple filtering alone often fails because motion noise overlaps the physiological signal spectrum ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1468#:~:text=A%20motion%20artefact%20is%20a,The%20results%20of%20this%20study)). The goal is to remove or reduce these artifacts *without distorting the true vital signal*, all while operating on minimal power. Below we outline signal-processing algorithms that enable accurate continuous monitoring under these constraints.

## Traditional Filtering Techniques

Basic filtering is the first line of defense, though its effectiveness is limited by spectral overlap. Common approaches include: 

- **Band-Pass/Band-Stop Filters (FIR/IIR):** Designing digital filters to pass the frequency band of the vital sign (e.g. typical heart-rate frequencies) while attenuating known noise bands. For example, an ECG band-pass (≈0.5–40 Hz) can filter baseline drift and high-frequency noise. However, motion artifacts often fall into the same band as the signal, so fixed filters risk removing parts of the physiological waveform ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1468#:~:text=A%20motion%20artefact%20is%20a,The%20results%20of%20this%20study)). In worst cases, overly aggressive filtering can distort ECG or PPG features, potentially *“leading to misdiagnosis”* ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1468#:~:text=heart%20diseases%20are%20reflected%20in,a%20normal%20ECG%20and%20an)).

- **Moving Average or Median Filters:** These sliding-window filters smooth short-term fluctuations. A moving average can cancel high-frequency jitter, while a median filter is excellent for removing sharp spikes (e.g. momentary motion-induced outliers). They are simple and low-cost computationally. Yet, heavy motion may last longer than the window, and these filters might also blur genuine signal changes ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1468#:~:text=paper%2C%20the%20performance%20of%20the,the%20adaptive%20filter%20can%20effectively)).

- **Notch and Adaptive Line Filters:** If motion or interference has a dominant frequency (for instance, repetitive motion at a certain rate), a notch filter can target it. But motion artifacts are often irregular, so fixed notches aren’t a complete solution. In practice, band-pass and smoothing filters alone rarely suffice for strong movement; more adaptive methods are needed ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=Conventional%20spectrum%20filtering%20methods%20have,PPG%20signal%20and%20motion%20artifact)).

## Adaptive Filtering and Sensor Fusion

**Adaptive noise cancellation (ANC)** is a powerful technique for wearables. It uses an *additional reference signal* (or multiple references) correlated with motion to adaptively filter the measured vital signal:

- **Accelerometer-Based Adaptive Filters:** Most wearables include accelerometers that sense motion. The accelerometer outputs can serve as reference noise signals for an adaptive filter (often an LMS filter). The algorithm continuously adjusts filter weights to subtract motion-induced components from the biosignal ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=Figure%2010B%20%20shows%20an,right%29%20crosses)). This approach is effective because the accelerometer “captures various motion information” ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=Figure%2010B%20%20shows%20an,right%29%20crosses)) which, when filtered, leaves a cleaner heart or pulse signal. Importantly, LMS adaptive filtering is computationally light (O(N) per sample) – *“owing to its simple algorithm, the speed of the ANC algorithm is fast”* ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=an%20adaptive%20filter%20to%20input,right%29%20are%20spectrally)), making it feasible on low-power processors. One example is a **multiple-reference ANC**, which uses multi-axis accelerometer data (and even multiple PPG sensors) to create several noise references. Combining their outputs can yield a robust heart-rate estimate even during intense activity ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27893403/#:~:text=signals%20has%20significant%20commercial%20values,closest%20to%20the%20estimated%20HR)) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27893403/#:~:text=adaptive%20noise%20cancellation%20%28ANC%29%20technique,are%20employed%20to%20ensure%20accurate)). Researchers showed that using three-axis accelerometry plus an extra PPG channel difference as references improved wrist PPG heart-rate accuracy during exercise ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27893403/#:~:text=adaptive%20noise%20cancellation%20%28ANC%29%20technique,are%20employed%20to%20ensure%20accurate)).

- **Physiological Reference Signals:** Instead of an accelerometer, some designs use another physiological measurement that is sensitive to motion but not needed for primary monitoring. For instance, a wearable ECG may include an **impedance pneumography** channel (measuring chest motion from breathing). This can act as a reference for adaptive filtering of ECG, since body motion and respiration artifacts show up in the impedance signal. Using this as the reference input to an adaptive filter significantly reduced ECG motion noise ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1468#:~:text=demonstrate%20that%20the%20adaptive%20filter,artefact%20in%20the%20ECG%20signal)). The result is a cleaner ECG with minimal added computation, as the filter continuously “adjusts its impulse response to filter out the noise” ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/15/5493#:~:text=2.%20Methodology%20,in%20the%20input%20with%20little)).

- **Sensor Fusion & Differential Measurement:** Some algorithms combine multiple sensor feeds to cancel artifacts. For example, a dual-PPG setup with two light wavelengths or two different emitter intensities can be used – one measurement might be more sensitive to motion than the other. By calibrating the signals when the subject is still, then subtracting one from the other during motion, it’s possible to isolate motion artifact and correct it ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=For%20motion%20artifact%20reduction%20methods,of%20one%20channel%20is%20adjusted)) ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=until%20the%20difference%20of%20the,176%20Zhu%20et%20al)). Branche & Mendelson demonstrated that a brighter LED PPG channel had less motion interference, so using two intensities and subtracting could leave mostly noise which can be removed ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=regarded.%20A%20signal,Then%2C%20in%20the%20case%20of)). This approach exploits hardware (extra LEDs) to simplify the signal processing needed, thus can be power-efficient if the added sensor power is low.

- **Kalman Filtering (State Estimation):** Some systems model the vital sign (e.g. heart rate or inter-beat interval) as a state that evolves over time, and use a Kalman filter to track it. The Kalman filter prediction-update loop can “smooth” out sudden jumps caused by artifacts, effectively filtering motion noise in the measured rate. For instance, if a device computes heart rate every few seconds, it can treat true heart rate as slowly varying and discount large one-step changes unless corroborated. This method maintains continuous tracking by blending new data with prior estimates, and it’s computationally light (essentially a few matrix multiplications) – suitable for microcontrollers. It’s especially useful for *maintaining accurate continuous monitoring*, since it prevents momentary noise from producing wild false readings.

## Separation and Transform Techniques

More advanced algorithms leverage signal transformations or source-separation methods to tackle motion artifacts:

- **Independent Component Analysis (ICA):** When multiple channels of data are available, ICA can separate the composite signals into independent source signals (ideally isolating the true vital sign from motion noise). For example, with a multi-channel PPG (multiple wavelengths or sensor positions), one can apply ICA to tease apart underlying components: one component might represent pulsatile blood flow, another the motion artifact ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=One%20of%20the%20most%20representative,of%20the%20joint%20probability%20distribution)). Lee *et al.* developed a 12-channel multi-wavelength PPG system and applied ICA, achieving over 96% sensitivity in heart-rate detection even during fast walking and ~82% during running ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1493/htm#:~:text=in%20blood%20are%20not%20robust,PPV%2C%20and%20FDR%20were%2099)). This demonstrates that source-separation can greatly suppress motion artifact, though ICA is computationally heavier. To fit power budgets, implementations often use optimized algorithms or dimensionality reduction (e.g. truncated SVD as used by Lee *et al.*) to reduce math operations ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1493/htm#:~:text=in%20blood%20are%20not%20robust,PPV%2C%20and%20FDR%20were%2099)). Nonetheless, when feasible, ICA-based artifact removal has been *widely adopted* in research for its effectiveness ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=artifact%20removal%2C%20several%20compensation%20methods,both%20independent%20and%20dependent%20motion)).

- **Wavelet Denoising and EMD:** Time-frequency decomposition methods like **wavelet transforms** or **Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD)** can separate a signal into components at different frequency bands or intrinsic mode functions. Motion artifacts often manifest in specific scales or as irregular trends, which wavelet or EMD methods can identify and strip out while preserving the heart/respiratory signal. For instance, wavelet denoising can suppress baseline wander or abrupt motion spikes by zeroing out certain wavelet coefficients. These techniques showed moderate success in studies ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1468#:~:text=paper%2C%20the%20performance%20of%20the,the%20adaptive%20filter%20can%20effectively)), but they require more computation (performing transformations on sliding windows). With careful optimization (e.g. using integer wavelet transforms or limiting decomposition levels), they can be employed in wearables, but developers must balance their power cost versus the benefit in signal quality.

- **Time-Varying Spectral Filtering:** Instead of fixed filters, some algorithms track the signal in the frequency domain over time. One such technique, **SpaMA (Spectral Filtering for Motion Artifacts)**, computes the power spectrum of both the PPG and the accelerometer signal in each short time window ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4732043/#:~:text=we%20investigated%20a%20novel%20technique,12%20subjects%20while%20performing%20treadmill)). By comparing these spectra, it identifies which frequency peaks in the PPG are likely due to motion (if they also appear in the accelerometer spectrum) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4732043/#:~:text=PPG%20signals%20and%20HR%20based,12%20subjects%20while%20performing%20treadmill)). Those frequencies can then be filtered out or de-emphasized, reconstructing a cleaner waveform. Frequency-tracking methods often also impose continuity constraints – for example, assume heart rate doesn’t jump drastically between consecutive windows ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=addition%2C%20a%20heuristic%20method%20in,176%20Zhu%20et%20al)). The dominant frequency closest to the last known heart rate is chosen as the true heart rate each time, rejecting outlier peaks caused by motion. This process of *peak tracking* in spectra has been used to maintain accurate HR readings during exercise ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27893403/#:~:text=signal%20,are%20employed%20to%20ensure%20accurate)). Implementing spectral analysis on-device can drain power (due to FFT computations), but using short windows and efficient FFT algorithms (or even dedicated DSP hardware) can make it viable. Some wearables perform this heavy processing intermittently or send data to a phone for analysis, to conserve device battery.

## Machine Learning and Adaptive Algorithms

Emerging solutions apply machine learning to recognize and remove artifacts. These range from classical ML to deep learning:

- **Adaptive Learning Filters:** Simple learning-based approaches might adjust filter parameters based on motion intensity. For example, the system could detect high motion (via accelerometer) and temporarily widen a filter bandwidth or switch algorithms to one more robust (trading off some sensitivity for stability). These heuristic approaches adapt on the fly without an explicit trained model, and are straightforward logic with minimal power impact.

- **Artifact Detection and Classification:** One strategy is to classify segments of data as “clean” or “corrupted.” Algorithms extract features from the signal (e.g. PPG morphology, statistical measures) and feed them to a light-weight classifier (like a support vector machine or a shallow neural network) to decide if the current reading is trustworthy ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=The%20learning,estimated%20from%20ECG%20by%20the)). Detected artifact sections might be excluded or corrected. Front-end preprocessing (like bandpass filtering and baseline removal) is used to simplify the classification task ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=On%20the%20other%20hand%2C%20learning,Then%2C%20postprocessor)). This approach was shown to improve signal quality by effectively *“cutting out contaminated parts”* via an ANN/SVM, then either holding the last good value or using interpolation to fill gaps ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=into%20two%20categories%20by%20the,using%20stored%20reference%20features%20is)) ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=The%20learning,181%3B%20%20182%20Liu)). The classifiers can be trained offline on labeled data, then implemented on-device with efficient code. A well-tuned SVM or small neural net can run in real-time on wearable-grade microcontrollers, as they often only need to evaluate a few dozen features.

- **Neural Network Denoising:** More advanced is using neural networks (e.g. autoencoders or CNNs) to directly filter or reconstruct the signal. A **denoising autoencoder** can be trained to take in a noisy vital-sign segment and output a clean version. For instance, Kwon *et al.* (2022) developed a neural PPG denoiser that learned to separate motion noise from pulse signals ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35336253/#:~:text=Skip%20to%20main%20page%20content,volume%20changes%20in%20a%20noninvasive)). Similarly, a time-delay neural network was used in a system called SniMA to combine current and past data from PPG and motion sensors to predict a clean PPG waveform ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=based%20restoration%20algorithm%20,178)). These methods can outperform linear filters, especially for complex motion patterns, *but* the neural network computations (matrix multiplies, etc.) are heavier. Deploying them in a wearable demands optimization like quantized networks, sparsity, or use of a low-power AI accelerator. Some wearables might send data to a smartphone app where powerful processors handle the neural net processing, preserving the wearable’s battery. As hardware evolves, tiny neural networks might become practical even within the device’s power budget.

## Power-Efficient Design Considerations

All the above algorithms must be **tailored for low-power continuous operation**:

- **Low Complexity:** Algorithms with fewer mathematical operations (or that reuse computations) are favored. For example, infinite impulse response (IIR) filters can achieve narrow-band filtering with just a few multiplies per sample, and LMS adaptive filters converge with simple iterations – these are far more efficient than performing large FFTs or matrix decompositions every second. Simpler algorithms also often mean less memory access, which saves energy.

- **Duty Cycling and Triggering:** The system can conserve power by running intensive algorithms only when needed. If a user is mostly still, a simple filter or even raw signal pass-through might suffice. When motion sensors detect movement above a threshold, the device can “wake up” more sophisticated artifact suppression (like turning on adaptive filtering or spectral analysis) ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=Whether%20PPG%20and%20motion%20artifact,volume%20variation%2C%20and%20thus%20subsequently)) ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=artifacts%20have%20been%20actively%20developed,is%20illustrated%20in%20Figure%2011)). This on-demand approach prevents wasting energy on heavy processing during quiet periods.

- **Hardware Acceleration:** Modern wearable chipsets include DSP blocks and even tiny ML accelerators. By offloading filtering (e.g. using a built-in DSP for convolution) or matrix math for ML, the device can perform complex algorithms at a fraction of the energy cost compared to doing them in software. Some designs incorporate analog preprocessing (such as high-pass filtering out baseline drift in hardware, or using a chopper-stabilized amplifier to minimize motion-induced offset) to reduce the burden on digital processing ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=at%20each%20part%20of%20the,Artifact%20reduction%20techniques)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44325-025-00090-6#:~:text=motion%20artifacts.%20Ensuring%20comfortable%2C%20long,This%20electrode%20significantly%20reduces)).

- **Data Fusion and Compression:** Rather than processing high-rate raw signals from multiple sensors separately, data fusion algorithms combine inputs in a simpler form. For example, if an accelerometer reading is strongly indicative of artifact, a device might use it to decide when to trust the PPG heart rate vs. when to discard or smooth it, instead of continuously performing complex subtraction. Additionally, if a clean vital metric (like heart rate) is obtained, that can be transmitted or logged at a lower rate than raw waveforms, saving power on wireless transmission. This is more of a system strategy but often guided by the signal processing outputs (e.g. confidence metrics of signal quality).

In summary, wearable vital-sign monitors achieve motion artifact suppression through a **suite of signal processing techniques**: from clever filtering and sensor fusion to advanced transforms and adaptive learning algorithms. Simpler methods (like adaptive noise cancellation with accelerometer input) are popular for their effectiveness and low computational cost ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/5/1468#:~:text=wavelet%20transform%2C%20empirical%20mode%20decomposition%2C,artefact%20in%20the%20ECG%20signal)) ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/felec.2021.685513/full#:~:text=Figure%2010B%20%20shows%20an,right%29%20crosses)). More complex methods (ICA, spectral analysis, machine learning) can further improve accuracy, and are deployed when the hardware can support them or in combination with efficient triggers. The end result is a system that maintains accurate continuous monitoring by dynamically cleaning the signal, all while fitting within the tight energy budget of a tiny wearable battery. By carefully balancing algorithm sophistication with efficiency, modern wearables can deliver reliable vital signs even during exercise or daily activities, without frequent recharging.
