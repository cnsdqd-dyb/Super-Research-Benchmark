# Understanding Supplier Promotion in Stratified Admission Systems

**Admission stratification** in supplier management refers to a tiered system where vendors are classified into levels (e.g. Tier 1, Tier 2 or bronze/silver/gold categories) based on performance. New or lower-tier suppliers must meet certain criteria to be “admitted” into higher tiers. *Supplier promotion* is the process of moving a supplier up to a higher tier once they demonstrate superior performance. The key to this promotion is a **performance boundary** – a predefined cutoff in performance metrics that the supplier must exceed to advance.

## Defining the Performance Boundary

The **performance boundary** is essentially a threshold score or set of KPI levels that distinguishes one stratification level from the next. In an admission stratification system, companies use **supplier scorecards** or evaluations to rate vendors across critical criteria (e.g. quality, on-time delivery, cost, innovation, compliance). Each supplier receives a composite score or rating based on these factors ([en.tacto.ai](https://en.tacto.ai/buyer-lexicon/preferred-supplier-program#:~:text=Selection%20process%20and%20qualification%20criteria)). The performance boundary is the minimum score (or KPI level) required for promotion to a higher tier. Suppliers scoring above this boundary are considered top performers eligible for preferred status, whereas those below remain in lower tiers.

## Criteria and Metrics Used for Evaluation

To determine if a supplier meets the promotion threshold, organizations measure performance against **objective criteria**. Common metrics include: 

- **Quality** – defect rates, returns, or PPM (parts per million defective).  
- **Delivery** – on-time delivery percentage and lead time adherence.  
- **Cost and Pricing** – cost competitiveness and savings offered.  
- **Service & Responsiveness** – communication, support, and issue resolution speed.  
- **Innovation/Value-add** – ability to contribute new ideas or improvements.

These criteria are often weighted and combined into an overall performance score ([en.tacto.ai](https://en.tacto.ai/buyer-lexicon/preferred-supplier-program#:~:text=The%20selection%20process%20is%20based,The%20key%20steps%20are)). For example, a company might weight quality at 40%, delivery 30%, cost 20%, and service 10% to compute a single performance index. Such a **composite score** provides a clear benchmark to compare suppliers. High scores indicate reliable suppliers consistently meeting or exceeding expectations, while low scores flag issues like delays or quality problems ([kpidepot.com](https://kpidepot.com/kpi/supplier-performance-scorecard#:~:text=High%20scores%20indicate%20reliable%20suppliers,aiming%20for%20scores%20above%2080)).

## How the Threshold is Determined

Determining the performance boundary involves both data analysis and strategic judgment:

- **Benchmarking and Industry Standards:** Companies often look at **industry benchmarks** and best-in-class performance to set their thresholds. In practice, many firms aim for performance in the *top quartile* of industry metrics when defining “exemplary” suppliers ([kpidepot.com](https://kpidepot.com/kpi/supplier-performance-scorecards#:~:text=or%20compliance,best%20practices%20in%20supplier%20management)). For instance, a score of **80% or above** on the supplier scorecard is commonly considered strong or *exemplary* performance that qualifies a supplier for the highest tier ([kpidepot.com](https://kpidepot.com/kpi/supplier-performance-scorecards#:~:text=or%20compliance,best%20practices%20in%20supplier%20management)). This 80% cutoff aligns with industry best practices and represents a level where suppliers are consistently meeting targets.

- **Historical Performance Distribution:** Organizations analyze the performance scores of their entire supplier base. A natural break or inflection in the score distribution can inform where to set the boundary. For example, if a cluster of suppliers score in the 80s and drop off below 80, the company may choose 80 as the promotion threshold. The goal is to ensure only a select percentage of top-performing suppliers cross the line, keeping the high tier exclusive and meaningful.

- **Company Objectives and Risk Tolerance:** The boundary is also tied to what the buying company considers an acceptable risk and service level. If the business requires very high reliability (e.g. aerospace or medical industries), the threshold for promotion might be set higher (perhaps 90%+ on KPIs or near-zero defects). In more cost-driven environments, the threshold might balance performance with supplier availability. Essentially, management decides the cutoff such that promoted suppliers will **consistently meet the firm’s operational needs** and quality standards. 

- **Multi-metric Minimums:** In some stratification systems, the promotion criteria include achieving **minimum levels on each key metric**, not just an overall score. For example, to move into the top tier, a supplier might need at least 95% on-time delivery and under 500 PPM defect rate for a sustained period. These minimums are set based on internal targets or customer requirements. Suppliers must **clear all hurdles**, ensuring well-rounded performance.

## Balancing Rigor and Inclusivity

Setting the performance boundary is a balancing act. If the threshold is **too high** or rigid, almost no suppliers will qualify, which might limit competition or supply options. Conversely, if it’s **too low**, the “preferred” tier loses meaning and may include mediocre performers. Companies often start with a reasonable cutoff (for instance, a score of 80/100 as mentioned) and adjust over time. Continuous **performance monitoring and annual reviews** help refine these boundaries. If many suppliers surpass the threshold easily, the bar can be raised to encourage further improvement. If few meet it, the company might invest in supplier development or adjust the threshold to have at least several strategic partners in the top tier.

## Continuous Improvement and Review

The performance boundary isn’t static. Admission stratification systems typically involve ongoing supplier evaluation ([en.tacto.ai](https://en.tacto.ai/buyer-lexicon/preferred-supplier-program#:~:text=,contractual%20relationships%20with%20agreed%20conditions)). Procurement and quality teams periodically review supplier scores and the threshold itself. They consider changes in market standards, company strategy, and supplier improvements. This means the criteria and cutoff can evolve – for example, introducing new metrics (like sustainability or ESG scores) or increasing the required score as overall supplier performance improves. The aim is to keep the promotion criteria **aligned with current business priorities and performance levels**.

In summary, the performance boundary for supplier promotion is determined by a combination of **quantitative benchmarks and strategic considerations**. Companies use data-driven scorecards to define what “good performance” looks like, often pegging the promotion threshold to high percentile benchmarks or fixed targets (e.g. >80% composite score) ([kpidepot.com](https://kpidepot.com/kpi/supplier-performance-scorecards#:~:text=or%20compliance,best%20practices%20in%20supplier%20management)). By analyzing historical supplier data and industry standards, they set a cutoff that top suppliers must exceed. This ensures that only suppliers who **consistently deliver superior results** are admitted to the highest stratification level, thereby securing a reliable and high-quality supply base. The exact threshold may vary by industry and company, but it is always chosen to reflect a level of performance deemed critical for strategic partnership.
