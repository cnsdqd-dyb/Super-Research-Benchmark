# Network Clustering and Biodiversity Loss Thresholds

**Clustering coefficient** – a measure of how interconnected a node’s neighbors are (i.e. the prevalence of “triangles” in the network) – has been shown to correlate strongly with ecosystem robustness. In ecological networks (like habitat patches or food webs), a high clustering coefficient means many closed loops of interactions, providing alternative pathways if one link or species is lost. Studies have identified clustering coefficient as a reliable indicator of network robustness **across various species and habitat networks** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X20307470#:~:text=Clustering%20coefficient%20is%20a%20good,species%2C%20networks%2C%20and%20habitat%20loss)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7575089/#:~:text=match%20at%20L75%20robustness%2C%20has,of%20herbivores%20and%20brown%20bears)). In practice, networks with high clustering tend to withstand more node or species losses before collapsing, effectively raising the threshold at which biodiversity loss accelerates. This local redundancy cushions the ecosystem – if one interaction breaks, others in the cluster can maintain functionality – delaying the point of systemic failure.

**Network modularity**, on the other hand, describes the degree to which a network is divided into distinct modules or communities with dense internal connections but sparser ties between modules. High modularity creates **compartmentalization**: species interact mostly within their own module. This structure can **limit cascading extinctions** – if one module suffers species loss, other modules might remain relatively insulated. In other words, modular networks are more likely to confine damage locally rather than allowing it to spread globally. Research on mutualistic networks has observed that when disturbances increase a network’s modularity, the remaining species enjoy *greater protection* from further collapse ([appliednetsci.springeropen.com](https://appliednetsci.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s41109-019-0131-6#:~:text=perturbations%20and%20disruptive%20events%20affect,protection%20to%20the%20remaining%20ecosystem)). Thus, **highly modular networks typically exhibit higher biodiversity-loss thresholds**, as multiple modules would need to fail before the entire system collapses. Conversely, in a low-modularity (highly interconnected) network, an extinction in one part can quickly propagate, lowering the threshold for widespread biodiversity loss.

**In summary, yes – the clustering coefficient and network modularity significantly influence biodiversity loss threshold predictions.** Both metrics describe structural resilience of the network. A **high clustering coefficient** indicates robust local interaction loops that delay collapse ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1470160X20307470#:~:text=Clustering%20coefficient%20is%20a%20good,species%2C%20networks%2C%20and%20habitat%20loss)), and strong **modularity** localizes disturbances, preventing system-wide failure ([appliednetsci.springeropen.com](https://appliednetsci.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s41109-019-0131-6#:~:text=perturbations%20and%20disruptive%20events%20affect,protection%20to%20the%20remaining%20ecosystem)). Models that incorporate these network properties predict that ecosystems with highly clustered, modular interaction networks can tolerate greater habitat or species loss before reaching a tipping point of irreversible biodiversity decline. In short, **more clustered and modular networks tend to have higher critical thresholds for biodiversity loss**, reflecting their greater capacity to absorb disturbances without collapsing ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7575089/#:~:text=match%20at%20L75%20robustness%2C%20has,of%20herbivores%20and%20brown%20bears)).
