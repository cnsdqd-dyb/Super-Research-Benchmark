## Similarity Threshold in Substitute Recommendations

**Defining Substitute Similarity:** In shopping basket (market basket) analysis, a *similarity threshold* is a chosen cutoff value for an item-pair similarity score. Two products must exceed this similarity to be considered “substitutes.” In essence, it's a line separating pairs of items that are similar enough to recommend one in place of the other from those that are not. There is no universal value for this threshold – it depends on how similarity is measured and the goals of the recommendation system. For example, similarity might be based on how often two items appear in similar purchase contexts (even if not bought together) or on shared attributes. Substitutes are typically items serving the same need, **often not purchased together** (e.g. two brands of the same product) ([tickr.com](https://tickr.com/identifying-substitutable-goods-using-large-scale-shopping-cart-basket-data/#:~:text=that%20are%20substitutes%20for%20a,that%20specific%20item%20is%20unavailable)), so their similarity is derived from parallel customer behaviors rather than co-occurrence in a basket.

**Who Sets the Threshold:** The similarity threshold is **not automatically determined by the data** or a generic formula – it’s usually set by the analyst or system designer as a **hyperparameter**. In practice, you **choose** this threshold based on what you consider “similar enough” for your use-case. Often, business rules or domain experts guide this choice ([epjdatascience.springeropen.com](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00297-4#:~:text=Despite%20its%20practical%20importance%2C%20the,11%20%E2%80%93%2022%5D.%20These)). Historically, retailers identified substitute products through **expert knowledge and simple stats** ([epjdatascience.springeropen.com](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00297-4#:~:text=Despite%20its%20practical%20importance%2C%20the,11%20%E2%80%93%2022%5D.%20These)), effectively using human judgment to set what “similar” means for their products. Modern data-driven methods provide continuous similarity scores, but **they still require a cutoff** if you want to classify something as a substitute or not ([epjdatascience.springeropen.com](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00297-4#:~:text=often%20rely%20on%20the%20definition,the%20sales%20data%20remains%20unused)). In other words, the analysis can tell you *how* similar two items are, but **you decide how high that score should be to count as a valid substitute recommendation**.

**How the Threshold Is Determined:** Setting the threshold often involves **experimentation and goals**: 

- **Distribution of Similarities:** Analysts may examine the distribution of item-item similarity scores and pick a threshold where similarity above that point clearly indicates a strong relationship. For example, if using cosine or Jaccard similarity (ranging 0 to 1), one might choose a cutoff like 0.7 if values above 0.7 correspond to very alike products, while lower values might yield unrelated items. The chosen value should separate meaningful substitute pairs from noise.  

- **Business Logic & Precision:** The threshold can be tuned to ensure recommendations make sense to customers. A **higher threshold** (stricter similarity) yields very close substitutes but fewer recommendations, improving precision (relevance) at the cost of coverage. A **lower threshold** includes more items as substitutes, increasing the pool of suggestions but risking some weak or unrelated recommendations. The business might decide, for instance, that substitutes should at least share a category or key features – effectively building that into the similarity measure or threshold criteria. 

- **Empirical Testing:** Often the optimal threshold is found by **trial and error or A/B testing**. You might adjust the threshold and evaluate how users respond – e.g. click-through or conversion rates on substitute suggestions – and then pick the value that balances offering enough alternatives without suggesting poor matches. There’s usually a trade-off between showing too few options and showing irrelevant ones. 

- **Top-N Approach (No Fixed Threshold):**  Many recommendation systems avoid a hard threshold altogether and instead use a **“top N” approach**. They rank all other products by similarity and simply recommend the top few substitutes for each product. In this case, the “threshold” is implicit – it’s effectively the similarity score of the Nth item. This ensures every item has some substitutes to show, even if their similarity scores are relatively low, but it confines recommendations to the most similar options available. For instance, a system might always show **the 5 closest substitutes** for any given product ([tickr.com](https://tickr.com/identifying-substitutable-goods-using-large-scale-shopping-cart-basket-data/#:~:text=an%20unrestricted%20view%20of%20what,illustrate%20the%20variation%20in%20substitutes)). If those top-5 have similarity scores like 0.93, 0.91, ... 0.80, then 0.80 functioned as the cutoff for that item’s substitutes, whereas another product might only have scores down to 0.60 for its 5th best match. This dynamic cutoff adjusts to the item’s context.

**Summary:** The similarity threshold in substitute recommendations is **set by design** – it’s a parameter you control rather than a fixed outcome of the algorithm. You determine it based on what level of similarity you consider sufficient for interchangeability. In practice, this might come from **business requirements, domain expert input, or tuning experiments**. Some systems forgo an absolute threshold and simply present the top-N most similar items as substitutes. Ultimately, the threshold is about balancing relevance and variety: high enough that substitutes truly meet the same need, but not so high that customers rarely see any alternatives. By examining similarity metrics and testing their impact, you set the threshold to align with the desired user experience and business objectives ([epjdatascience.springeropen.com](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00297-4#:~:text=Secondly%2C%20these%20methods%20lack%20specific,the%20sales%20data%20remains%20unused)) ([epjdatascience.springeropen.com](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00297-4#:~:text=Despite%20its%20practical%20importance%2C%20the,11%20%E2%80%93%2022%5D.%20These)).
