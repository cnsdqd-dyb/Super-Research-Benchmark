# Temporal Discounting of Ratings in Review Systems

Review systems often apply **temporal discounting** to rating contributions, meaning older reviews or ratings are given less weight than more recent ones. This approach helps keep the aggregate rating reflective of the current state of the product, service, or content being reviewed. Several factors determine how the temporal discount function is defined in such systems:

- **Nature of Change in the Domain**: The rate at which the underlying item’s quality or user experience changes strongly influences the discount function. If a business or product can **improve or deteriorate quickly**, older reviews become stale faster and thus should be **discounted more steeply**. Conversely, in a stable domain (like a classic book or a long-established product that doesn’t change), older ratings remain relevant longer and require a gentler decay. The core idea is to have a *“rate of forgetting”* that matches the actual pace and type of change in the real world ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=data,114)). Setting the wrong decay rate can either **overlook important historical feedback** or **overemphasize outdated opinions**, so aligning the discount with actual change is crucial.

- **Platform Goals and Philosophy**: Different review platforms choose their temporal weighting based on what they want to emphasize. Some platforms prioritize **recency for up-to-date accuracy**, giving **heavier weight to the latest reviews**. For example, Amazon tends to highlight and rely more on recent customer experiences, under the assumption that they **best reflect the product’s current quality** ([www.tencentmagazine.com](https://www.tencentmagazine.com/review-platforms-product-rankings/#:~:text=Some%20review%20platforms%20give%20more,term%20consistency)). On the other hand, platforms like Yelp aim for a balance between old and new reviews, valuing **long-term consistency** in ratings ([www.tencentmagazine.com](https://www.tencentmagazine.com/review-platforms-product-rankings/#:~:text=Some%20review%20platforms%20give%20more,term%20consistency)). Thus, a platform focused on showcasing **current status** might implement a steep discount function (making new reviews much more influential), while a platform valuing **stability and historical track record** will use a milder discount (ensuring older contributions still count significantly).

- **Common Functional Forms**: The shape of the discount function is typically chosen for both effectiveness and simplicity. Many systems use **exponential decay**, **logistic decay**, or other damping functions to gradually reduce a review’s weight as it ages ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=The%20weighting%20functions%20penalized%20the,105)). An exponential function (where weight might decay proportionally to e^(-λ * age)) has the advantage of not requiring storage of long history – it continuously fades out old data. Logistic or other nonlinear functions can introduce a more **gradual drop-off followed by a plateau**, if a system wants to avoid completely zeroing out older ratings. The specific function is determined by design preferences and how well it fits the expected decay of relevance over time. Importantly, the chosen function will include parameters (like a **decay rate or half-life**) that control how fast the weight decreases. Those parameters are often set through experimentation or domain knowledge – for instance, a **short half-life** if user sentiments change rapidly, or a **long half-life** if changes are slow.

- **Empirical Calibration and Predictive Value**: Data-driven tuning plays a role in determining the temporal discount function. Platform designers may analyze historical data to see how predictive older ratings are of current quality or user satisfaction. If analysis shows that a review older than, say, 5 years has very little correlation with the current state of a business, the system might decide to heavily discount anything older than 5 years. On the flip side, if older feedback still has some predictive power, the discount function will be milder. Often the discount rate is fine-tuned so that the **weighted aggregate rating best matches present realities** (for example, maximizing correlation with **today’s customer satisfaction** or outcomes). In essence, the quantitative impact of time on a review’s usefulness (measured via algorithms or A/B testing) will help determine the exact form and steepness of the discount function.

- **Examples of Temporal Weighting**: To illustrate, one rating aggregator (ONE AllRatings) explicitly weights each review by its age. A review from the last year might be **weighted about three times higher** than a review from seven years ago ([www.oneallratings.com](https://www.oneallratings.com/more-recent-ratings-given-more-weight#:~:text=You%20can%E2%80%99t%20go%20back%20in,to%20calculate%20rating%20star%20scores)). This indicates a **designed decay** over a six-year span such that older contributions carry significantly less influence. The specific multiplier (3× in this case) reflects the platform’s judgment about how much more predictive a recent experience is compared to a very old one. Other systems might not disclose exact numbers but similarly use a **time-based weighting schedule**. Another example is the difference in approach between platforms: as noted, **Amazon’s algorithm gives a strong boost to recent reviews**, whereas **Yelp’s algorithm ensures older reviews still count** to show consistency ([www.tencentmagazine.com](https://www.tencentmagazine.com/review-platforms-product-rankings/#:~:text=Some%20review%20platforms%20give%20more,term%20consistency)). These choices are determined by the platform’s ideas of fairness, resistance to gaming (e.g., not letting a flood of new reviews completely overturn a long-term rating unfairly), and what they believe users find more useful.

In summary, the temporal discount function in review systems is determined by a combination of **domain dynamics**, **platform design goals**, and **empirical considerations**. System designers choose a function (often exponential or similar) and set its parameters based on how quickly the subject of reviews can change, how much they want to emphasize recent feedback versus historical reputation, and what their data suggests about the diminishing relevance of older reviews. By tuning the temporal discount function to the context, review systems aim to keep ratings **relevant, fair, and predictive of current quality**, ensuring users get an up-to-date and reliable summary of opinions.
