# Sensory Interaction and the Ontology of Audience Participation in Digital Art

## From Passive Spectators to Active Participants  
Digital art installations often go beyond visual spectacle to engage multiple senses – sight, sound, touch, and even movement or balance. Unlike traditional art where viewers stand apart, these **interactive, multisensory environments** invite the audience to *do* something: move through a space, touch objects, or even use their bodies as controllers. This immersive engagement transforms the audience from passive onlookers into active participants. In fact, multi-sensory interaction tends to **heighten engagement and emotional involvement**, drawing people into creative participation rather than mere observation ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-9408/8/4/130#:~:text=authenticity,A%20methodological%20approach%20to%20digitisation)). By captivating more of our senses, digital installations encourage us to *become part of the artwork*, not just its audience.  

One clear outcome of this shift is that the line between the **artwork and the viewer begins to blur**. Interactive installations “transform passive viewers into active participants, opening new worlds of engagement and discovery,” as art writers often note. In other words, the audience’s role evolves into that of a co-creator or performer within the piece. For example, in immersive digital exhibitions like those by teamLab, *“visitors are part artwork, part spectator”* ([designwanted.com](https://designwanted.com/teamlab-interview/#:~:text=teamLab%20absorbs%20us%20in%20immersive,ask%2C%20and%20we%20did%20well)) – they step **inside** the art. The installation responds to their presence and actions, so experiencing the art becomes an active, two-way process. This represents a fundamental change in the ontology of art viewing: the audience is no longer external to the work but **embedded within it**.

## Embodied Interaction and Blurred Boundaries  
Because digital installations rely on sensor-based interaction and real-time feedback, they establish a **sensory feedback loop** between the artwork and the participant. When you move, speak, or touch in an interactive installation, the artwork changes in response – maybe the visuals shift, sounds play, or lights react. Crucially, those changes then influence your next sensation or movement, creating a continuous dialogue between person and artwork. In such a loop, **audience and artwork become closely coupled**. Scholars describe how interactivity and sensory feedback in these contexts *“lead to the fundamental dissolution of the putative boundaries that separate the body of the spectator from the physical space of the installation (or performance) and the digital space of the information”* ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=it#:~:text=interactivity%20and%20sensory%20feedback%2C%20leads,20%298)). In other words, the audience’s body and the art environment start to feel like one continuum. 

This **embodied interaction** means that participation is not just intellectual but physical and present. Instead of a viewer gazing at a separate art object, the participant’s bodily movements and sensations are integral to experiencing the piece. The artwork often *recognizes* and incorporates the participant’s presence – for instance, a motion-sensitive installation might surround someone with changing visuals or sound based on where they walk. The result is that the spectator’s **here-and-now bodily presence becomes part of the art’s medium**. A vivid example is **Char Davies’s *Osmose*** (1995), a virtual reality installation controlled by the immersant’s breathing and balance. In *Osmose*, the feeling of *“presence … does not occur as a manifestation from the outside but as an emergence from the inside, through the recipient’s proprioception”* ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=it#:~:text=kind%20of%20presence%20that%20characterises,motor%20and%20imaginative%20terms)). Here the participant’s inner bodily sensations (breath, balance) directly shape the virtual world, so the sense of being present in the artwork arises from one’s own sensory interaction. Such designs make the audience viscerally aware that *their body is part of the artwork*. This blurring of boundaries constructs a new understanding of what it means to participate: the audience is **physically, not just mentally, engaged in the art’s existence**.

## Co-Creation and Ontological Shift in Participation  
In interactive digital art, the **audience’s participation is not optional or secondary – it is foundational**. The artwork often evolves or comes to life only through input from visitors, meaning the traditional artwork-viewer relationship is fundamentally reconfigured. The very **existence and form of the artwork become interdependent with the participant**. As one researcher notes, a digital artwork *“loses its traditional status of ‘thing’ and merges into the recipient’s perception as a media environment and interactive process”*, so *“the artwork’s conditions for existence shift from a formal and material a-priori to an interdependent relationship connecting the artwork and the embodied subject of the experience.”* ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=it#:~:text=18Starting%20from%20the%20analysis%20of,and%20the%20way%20this%20latter)). In simpler terms, the art is no longer a static object independent of who views it – instead, it is a **living process** that includes the participant’s actions and perceptions as essential components. The audience and artwork form one system, each influencing the other. 

This **co-creative role** of the audience means that *experiencing* the art and *creating* the art can become one and the same act. Indeed, theorists argue that in interactive art *“work and reception are con-fused into the same processual existence”* ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=it#:~:text=match%20at%20L274%20interactivity%2C%20work,Couchot%202002%3A%2023)). The audience’s act of engaging (reception) is part of the artwork’s unfolding (work), merging creation and experience into a single process. Through their sensory interactions, participants might trigger visuals, build narrative paths, or generate sounds – effectively **contributing to the artwork’s content or outcome**. The installation often provides a framework or environment, but it’s the audience input that actualizes specific events or images. In this way, **audience participation is ontologically embedded** in the artwork: without participation, the “full” artwork doesn’t materialize at all. 

By engaging the audience’s senses and actions, digital installations **redefine what it means to be an audience**. Participation becomes a mode of existence within the artwork rather than an external activity. The audience member is simultaneously observer and performer, consumer and creator. This fusion constructs a new ontology of audience participation in which the audience is *constitutive* of the art experience. In practical terms, **sensory interaction** grants audiences a form of agency and presence within the artwork’s world. The result is an art experience that is **personalized, evolving, and deeply immersive** – a shared space where meaning is generated collaboratively in real time. Through multisensory, interactive design, digital installations demonstrate that *audience participation is not just a response to art, but part of the art’s very being and definition*.
