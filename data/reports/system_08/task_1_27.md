# Balancing Privacy and Traceability in CBDC Ledgers

## Privacy vs. Traceability: A CBDC Design Dilemma 
Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) must balance individual privacy with the **traceability** needed for financial integrity. On one hand, users and data protection authorities demand cash-like confidentiality in transactions. On the other, regulators insist on **anti-money-laundering (AML)** and counter-terrorism oversight. As one analysis notes, *“Users rank privacy as the top concern; regulators require AML/CFT enforceability.”* ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=Users%20rank%20privacy%20as%20the,Concrete%20choices)) This creates a design dilemma: how to **prevent illicit use** through traceability while still **protecting user data** from mass surveillance.  

## Privacy-Preserving Techniques in CBDC Architecture 
Modern CBDC architectures explore cryptographic solutions to achieve privacy without sacrificing transparency. **Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs)** are a leading technique: they allow transaction validity to be proven *without revealing underlying data* ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/maintaining-privacy-digital-currencies-issued-central-mustafa-syed#:~:text=Blockchain%20has%20given%20us%20many,operate%20in%20the%20near%20future)). In simple terms, ZKPs let a user prove “I followed the rules” (e.g. I have sufficient balance, I’m not double-spending) **without exposing** their identity or transaction details. This can theoretically give central banks a *“cryptographic silver bullet”* for privacy ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=governments%20worldwide%20rush%20to%20deploy,edge%20financial%20institutions)) – regulators can be confident transactions obey the rules, but sensitive details remain hidden from public view. Other privacy-enhancing technologies include **selective disclosure** credentials and **encrypted or pseudonymous identifiers**: for example, using hashed IDs or aliases so that transactors are pseudonymous by default ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=2,default%2C%20enforceable%20when%20needed)). These approaches ensure that **only authorized parties** (like regulated intermediaries or the central bank under legal due process) can link transactions to real identities. In some designs, identity information and transaction flows are kept separate via a *“split-knowledge key architecture,”* so that no single entity can correlate who did what without combining keys or permissions ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=,Offline)). This means everyday transactions can remain private, yet if suspicious activity arises, authorities (with a warrant or appropriate approval) could trace and audit the data. 

**Zero-knowledge proofs** in particular have garnered attention from major central banks like the ECB and Bank of Canada as a way to reconcile privacy with oversight ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/maintaining-privacy-digital-currencies-issued-central-mustafa-syed#:~:text=A%20number%20of%20central%20banks,deeper%20into%20this%20proposed%20technology)). With ZKPs, a CBDC system might, for example, let users prove they are *not exceeding a spending limit or not transacting with blacklisted addresses* – all *without revealing* their identity or the exact amounts ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/maintaining-privacy-digital-currencies-issued-central-mustafa-syed#:~:text=Blockchain%20has%20given%20us%20many,operate%20in%20the%20near%20future)). Such capabilities could give a CBDC **cash-like anonymity** for honest users while still enabling compliance checks. Indeed, privacy-by-design proposals often envision **“pseudonymous by default, enforceable when needed”** operations ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=2,default%2C%20enforceable%20when%20needed)). In practice, this could mean the ledger records transactions under random addresses and encrypted amounts, coupled with cryptographic proofs that the totals balance and rules are met. Regulators would see an *opaque ledger* that they can trust mathematically, and only if necessary (e.g. suspected crime) would a mechanism exist to decode identities or amounts. 

## The Throughput Challenge of Zero-Knowledge Proofs 
While these cryptographic tools sound ideal, they introduce significant **complexity and performance costs**. Generating and validating zero-knowledge proofs is computationally intensive. For example, benchmarks show that creating a zk-SNARK proof for even a basic transaction can take **several seconds on consumer hardware** ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=match%20at%20L59%20,trusted%20setup%20requirements%20and%20quantum)). One CBDC experiment found *“a single zk-SNARK proof for a basic transaction takes 3.5 seconds on average hardware”* – a delay that is *“acceptable for small systems but problematic for national-scale CBDCs processing 10k+ TPS.”* ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=When%20testing%20ZKP%20implementations%20for,16xlarge%20instances)) In other words, if every transaction requires heavy cryptographic proof generation, a country’s payment system could **stall under load**. Current public blockchains with full privacy (like Zcash or Monero) already process far fewer transactions per second than traditional payment networks, partly due to these overheads. A central bank system needs to handle potentially tens of thousands of transactions per second, so the **throughput cap** imposed by complex cryptography is a real concern.

Central banks and researchers acknowledge that *“ZKPs have still a long way to go and are not without challenges.”* ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/maintaining-privacy-digital-currencies-issued-central-mustafa-syed#:~:text=A%20number%20of%20central%20banks,deeper%20into%20this%20proposed%20technology)) High computational costs and longer transaction latencies are among the chief issues. In trials, advanced privacy prototypes often run into scalability limits – for instance, the BIS’s *Project Tourbillon* (a trial of privacy-preserving CBDC transfers) successfully implemented payer anonymity with modern cryptography, but *“practical throughput needs further work”* despite the privacy primitive being solid ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=privacy%20while%20enforcing%20holding%20limits,needs%20further%20work%20but%20the)). In short, **privacy usually comes at the cost of performance**. Every added layer of encryption, zero-knowledge proof, or mixing scheme tends to reduce how many transactions per second the system can process or how quickly transactions confirm. This doesn’t mean privacy features are infeasible, but it does mean designers face a trade-off: **to gain privacy, you accept some efficiency loss** (at least with today’s technology). The **complexity** of ZK proofs and similar techniques can act as a **brake on throughput**, forcing difficult decisions about how much privacy to enable in a first-generation CBDC.  

That said, there is active work to improve performance. Optimized algorithms, hardware acceleration, and more efficient proof systems (like zk-STARKs, bulletproofs, and recursive SNARKs) are rapidly reducing proof overhead ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=Zero,balancing%20cryptographic%20security%20with%20practical)). One promising approach is **batching or aggregating proofs**: for example, **recursive proof composition** can bundle multiple transactions’ proofs into one, greatly cutting down verification load and data size on the ledger ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=When%20testing%20ZKP%20implementations%20for,16xlarge%20instances)). Similarly, *roll-up* architectures or **CoinJoin** mechanisms combine many transfers into a single cryptographic proof or transaction, amortizing the cost. These strategies have shown dramatic savings – e.g. combining proofs reduced on-chain data by >90% in tests ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=a%20basic%20transaction%20takes%203,16xlarge%20instances)) – but they also introduce some delay (waiting to batch transactions) and added system complexity. In summary, zero-knowledge techniques *can* be scaled up with careful engineering, but **today’s highest-security privacy tech still doesn’t match the raw throughput of a simple ledger**. Designers must work around these limits with innovative architecture choices.

## Architectural Strategies to Optimize Privacy and Traceability 
To navigate the privacy–traceability trade-off, CBDC architects are exploring **hybrid approaches** that blend policy, design decisions, and selective cryptography:

- **Tiered Privacy by Transaction Size/Type:** Many proposals suggest allowing a degree of *anonymity for low-value or certain transactions*, while requiring more transparency for large or high-risk transfers ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=Privacy%20and%20compliance)). For example, the ECB has explored **“anonymity vouchers”** – a novel mechanism granting users a limited budget of private, untraceable transactions ([www.coindesk.com](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2019/12/17/anonymity-vouchers-could-bring-limited-privacy-to-cbdcs-ecb-report#:~:text=The%20ECB%E2%80%99s%20%E2%80%9Cnovel%20new%20concept%E2%80%9D,laundering%20%28AML%29%20enforcement)) ([www.coindesk.com](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2019/12/17/anonymity-vouchers-could-bring-limited-privacy-to-cbdcs-ecb-report#:~:text=Anonymous%20vouchers%2C%20the%20report%20states%2C,%E2%80%9D)). Each user periodically receives non-transferable cryptographic vouchers that can be spent to **mask a transaction** from the ledger’s AML scrutiny ([www.coindesk.com](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2019/12/17/anonymity-vouchers-could-bring-limited-privacy-to-cbdcs-ecb-report#:~:text=The%20anonymity%20vouchers%2C%20issued%20to,their%20transactions%2C%20the%20report%20states)). If they run out of vouchers, additional transactions must be fully traceable. This effectively *“limits the amount of CBDC that can be transferred anonymously”* without tracking every user’s spending ([www.coindesk.com](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2019/12/17/anonymity-vouchers-could-bring-limited-privacy-to-cbdcs-ecb-report#:~:text=However%2C%20if%20Alice%20does%20not,batches%20by%20the%20AML%20Authority)). Small day-to-day payments could thus feel like cash (private), whereas large or frequent transfers would automatically fall under scrutiny once the anonymity allotment is exhausted. Such **tiered models** mirror the idea of cash-like privacy for small payments and auditability for the rest.

- **Pseudonymity and Selective Disclosure:** Rather than broadcasting personal identities on a ledger, users can be represented by **randomized addresses or tokens**. Only regulated intermediaries (e.g. banks or payment providers) know the identity behind an address, and even they may not see the full transaction history beyond what’s needed. The core ledger might store only **hashed or encrypted identity references** ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=2,default%2C%20enforceable%20when%20needed)). If law enforcement needs to investigate a specific alias, they can compel the intermediary to disclose the user (with proper legal process). In addition, **selective disclosure credentials** allow users to prove statements about themselves *without revealing all details*. For instance, a user’s wallet could prove “I am over 18” or “I passed KYC checks” to the CBDC system via a zero-knowledge proof, instead of revealing their name or ID ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=,compliance%20attestations%3B%20selective%20disclosure%20credentials)). This way, compliance checks (like age-restricted payments or sanction-list screening) can be enforced **without general identity leakage**. The system remains **traceable in a targeted way** — if a crime is suspected, investigators can follow an alias and, through court-supervised means, uncover the person behind it — but mass surveillance of all transactions is prevented by design.

- **Auditable Privacy and Data Escrow:** Some architectures incorporate **“auditable privacy”** which means all transactions are *cryptographically blinded* by default but still produce an audit trail that can later be unlocked if needed ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=Privacy%20engineering%20checklist%3A)) ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=,Offline)). For example, transaction details could be **encrypted** with keys split among multiple authorities. No single party (neither the central bank alone nor any commercial bank) can read transactions in plain text ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=,Offline)). However, if suspicious activity is detected or during an audit, a **multi-party protocol** could be used to decrypt specific data (e.g. requiring consent from a privacy trustee, the central bank, and perhaps a court order). This creates a **check-and-balance**: privacy is the default, but traceability is possible under strictly controlled conditions (with logs and oversight whenever the “sealed” data is opened). Additionally, keeping tamper-evident logs of any identity revelation ensures that any access to private data leaves an indelible trail ([www.7blocklabs.com](https://www.7blocklabs.com/blog/cbdc-consultancy-architecture-choices-privacy-tradeoffs-and-interop#:~:text=match%20at%20L169%20,budgets%3B%20red%E2%80%91team%20for%20re%E2%80%91identification%20risk)), discouraging abuses. This *“trust-but-verify”* approach attempts to give both privacy for honest users and forensic traceability for investigators.

- **Efficiency-Driven Cryptography Choices:** As seen in recent research, it’s not always necessary to use the heaviest cryptography for every aspect of privacy. Innovative designs use a **mix of cryptographic techniques** to reduce overhead. For instance, one prototype CBDC ledger concealed transaction amounts with simple **Pedersen commitments** (hiding values behind cryptographic commitments) and then used a lightweight **Schnorr signature** proof to ensure no money was secretly created ([eprint.iacr.org](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1496#:~:text=Some%20information%20could%20still%20be,payee)). This avoided the need for a complex zero-knowledge circuit to prove “no over-issuance,” *greatly reducing computational overhead* ([eprint.iacr.org](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1496#:~:text=commitment%20scheme%20is%20applied%20to,This)). Combined with batching techniques like **CoinJoin** (merging multiple users’ transfers into one group transaction), such a system can obfuscate who paid whom with far less cryptographic cost than full per-transaction ZKPs ([eprint.iacr.org](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1496#:~:text=Schnorr%20signature,time%20of%20Coinjoin%20is%20analysed)). The trade-off is that some information (like exact timing or grouping of transactions) might leak and *privacy isn’t absolute*, but the **performance remains high**. In practice, central banks might opt for these pragmatic compromises: using heavy zero-knowledge proofs **only for specific high-privacy features** (e.g. proving compliance or mixing funds in and out of an anonymity pool), while handling the bulk of transactions with lighter techniques or even conventional ledger entries. This keeps the system efficient while still plugging the most sensitive privacy gaps.

## Conclusion: Optimizing the Trade-off 
In summary, **CBDC architectures can be designed to optimize the privacy–traceability balance**, but not without trade-offs. Careful layering of cryptographic privacy features and policy limits can give users *much more privacy than a fully transparent blockchain*, yet still allow oversight when truly needed. Techniques like zero-knowledge proofs, pseudonymous addressing, and anonymity vouchers **demonstrate that privacy and compliance can coexist** in a controlled way ([www.coindesk.com](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2019/12/17/anonymity-vouchers-could-bring-limited-privacy-to-cbdcs-ecb-report#:~:text=The%20ECB%E2%80%99s%20%E2%80%9Cnovel%20new%20concept%E2%80%9D,laundering%20%28AML%29%20enforcement)) ([www.coindesk.com](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2019/12/17/anonymity-vouchers-could-bring-limited-privacy-to-cbdcs-ecb-report#:~:text=Anonymous%20vouchers%2C%20the%20report%20states%2C,%E2%80%9D)). However, today’s reality is that stronger privacy often comes at the cost of **higher complexity and lower throughput**. Implementing robust anonymity (especially at scale) **will impact performance** – as evidenced by prototypes where privacy features add seconds of processing per transaction ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=When%20testing%20ZKP%20implementations%20for,16xlarge%20instances)) or require new infrastructure for key management and auditing ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/maintaining-privacy-digital-currencies-issued-central-mustafa-syed#:~:text=A%20number%20of%20central%20banks,deeper%20into%20this%20proposed%20technology)). 

Going forward, the **throughput limitations** imposed by zero-knowledge proofs and similar tech are a key focus for researchers. With ongoing advancements, the gap is expected to narrow – faster algorithms, better hardware, and optimized designs are steadily improving throughput **year over year** ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=Zero,balancing%20cryptographic%20security%20with%20practical)). It’s likely that first-generation CBDCs will adopt a **hybrid approach**: they might not offer **fully zero-knowledge anonymity for every transaction**, but they can incorporate selective privacy features (for low-risk payments or identity protection) combined with backstop traceability. Over time, as cryptography becomes more efficient, that balance can be adjusted to grant more privacy without sacrificing speed. 

In essence, **there is no one-size-fits-all answer**. Some privacy can be built into CBDCs via smart architecture (yes, the balance can be optimized), but absolute privacy with zero-knowledge security **does** exact a cost in throughput given current technology. Central banks will have to decide, explicitly, how much privacy is enough and worth the trade-off. The good news is that a spectrum of solutions exists between the extremes of totally transparent ledgers and completely opaque ones. By combining policy measures (like limits and oversight) with technical measures (like ZKPs and pseudonyms), CBDCs can achieve **“privacy by design”** to a significant degree – just not without careful design choices to mitigate the performance hits. The ultimate goal is a CBDC system where *routine transactions feel as private as cash*, while the system retains the tools to **trace illicit activity** in exceptional cases. Achieving that balance is possible, but it requires navigating the limitations of today’s cryptography and computing power with innovative architecture and clear governance.
