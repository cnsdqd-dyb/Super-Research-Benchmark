## Balancing Anticoagulation and Bleeding Risk in ECMO

During ECMO support, unfractionated heparin is continuously infused and *carefully titrated* to maintain a narrow therapeutic window that prevents clot formation (thromboembolism) without causing excessive bleeding ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=During%20extracorporeal%20membrane%20oxygenation%20,quality%20data)). **Heparin dosing is individualized and adjusted in real-time** based on frequent coagulation monitoring. Typically, an initial heparin bolus of ~50–100 units/kg is given at cannulation, followed by a continuous infusion of roughly 7.5–20 units/kg per hour ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9782867/#:~:text=ELSO%20recommends%20starting%20with%20a,8)). The infusion rate is then *fine-tuned* using bedside and laboratory tests of anticoagulation, such as the activated clotting time (ACT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), or anti-factor Xa levels ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=Anti)). These tests guide the heparin dose to keep anticoagulation in a **moderate range**: for example, an ACT around *180–200 seconds* (much lower than the ~400–480 seconds used during short-term cardiopulmonary bypass) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=number%2C%20hypothermia%2C%20hypofibrinogenemia%2C%20and%20coagulation,Xa)), or an aPTT roughly *1.5–2.5 times normal* (often target ~60–80 seconds) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=The%20aPTT%20test%20is%20a,As%20fibrin)). Many centers even aim for the lower end of this range – or temporarily reduce heparin – if a patient has high bleeding risk, with some protocols targeting aPTT of *40–60 seconds* in bleeding-prone situations ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=The%20aPTT%20test%20is%20a,As%20fibrin)). Conversely, if there are signs of clot formation in the circuit or patient (e.g. rising oxygenator pressures or thrombus observed), the heparin infusion may be uptitrated within the safe range to ensure adequate anticoagulation.

**Frequent monitoring and responsive dose adjustment** are key to this balance. Bedside ACT checks can be done **hourly** in many units for rapid feedback, and laboratory aPTT or anti-Xa levels are measured *at regular intervals* (e.g. every 4–6 hours, or per institutional protocol) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=Currently%2C%2094%25%20%28109%2F116%29%20of%20ELSO,argues%20against%20the%20use%20of)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=Anti)). Anti-factor Xa assay is considered a more direct measure of heparin activity and many centers aim for an anti-Xa level of about *0.3–0.7 IU/mL* during ECMO ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=Anti)). Each test has limitations (e.g. ACT can be influenced by anemia, low platelets, etc., and aPTT results vary with different reagents ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=%5B37%20%5D.%20A%20standard%20laboratory,of%20assay%20used%20for%20aPTT))), so clinicians often use a **combination of indicators** and clinical judgment. The goal is to maintain *enough* anticoagulation to keep the ECMO circuit **free of thrombi** while minimizing patient bleeding. This can be challenging, as critically ill ECMO patients often have coagulopathy or inflammation that affects heparin response ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=duration%20of%20ECMO%2C%20heparin%20dose%2C,only%20partial%20functional%20measures%20of)). Therefore, teams **continuously reassess** the heparin dose: if even mild bleeding occurs (e.g. at cannulation sites or surgical wounds), the heparin infusion may be lowered or briefly paused (and if needed, reversed with protamine), whereas any evidence of clotting leads to prompt dose increases or additional interventions.

**Institutional protocols and multidisciplinary oversight** help manage this delicate equilibrium. Most ECMO centers have protocolized target ranges and adjust heparin dosing in *real time* to stay within those bounds. For example, a protocol might specify maintaining an ACT ~180 seconds or an aPTT of ~60 seconds, adjusting the infusion by small increments if values drift out of range. If a patient is at *particularly high risk of bleeding* (such as post-surgery or with recent hemorrhage), some centers will accept subtherapeutic targets or even consider **brief periods of heparin-free ECMO**, with extremely vigilant circuit monitoring ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9782867/#:~:text=that%20bleeding%20may%20be%20a,that%20lower%20or%20even%20no)). (Modern heparin-bonded ECMO circuits and careful surveillance can sometimes permit short heparin interruptions, though this is done only in exceptional cases.) On the other hand, if there is a strong prothrombotic tendency (e.g. **hitting clotting in the oxygenator**), the team may run heparin at the higher end of the target range or add antithrombin supplementation to improve heparin’s efficacy. Throughout an ECMO run, the balance is continuously reassessed – it’s a dynamic trade-off where *both under- and over-anticoagulation carry serious risks*. Notably, studies have observed that major bleeding events on ECMO are associated with worse outcomes than minor thrombotic events ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9782867/#:~:text=patients%20,5%2C6)). This drives clinicians to err on the side of **lower heparin intensity when in doubt**, emphasizing bleeding avoidance while still preventing critical thrombosis. In summary, heparin dosing during ECMO is managed by *tight titration* within a defined target range, **constant monitoring**, and proactive adjustments – all to strike the safest possible balance between thrombosis and bleeding ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=During%20extracorporeal%20membrane%20oxygenation%20,quality%20data)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6971875/#:~:text=The%20aPTT%20test%20is%20a,As%20fibrin)).
