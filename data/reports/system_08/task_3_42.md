# Carbon Emissions by Mode and Optimal Transport Capacity

## Emissions Distribution Across Travel Modes  
Carbon emissions from transportation are **unevenly distributed across travel modes**. Road transport dominates: cars, buses, and trucks together produce roughly three-quarters of all transport CO₂ emissions ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-transport#:~:text=Road%20travel%20accounts%20for%20three,comes%20from%20trucks%20carrying%20freight)). In fact, **passenger vehicles (mostly cars)** alone contribute about 45% of transport’s emissions ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-transport#:~:text=Road%20travel%20accounts%20for%20three,comes%20from%20trucks%20carrying%20freight)). By contrast, lower-carbon modes like **rail** account for only around 1% ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-transport#:~:text=Aviation%20%E2%80%94%20while%20it%20often,6)). Other modes sit in between – for example, aviation produces ~11% and shipping ~10% of transport emissions ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-transport#:~:text=Aviation%20%E2%80%94%20while%20it%20often,6)). This means a **disproportionate share of emissions comes from a few high-carbon modes**, especially personal cars and airplanes. Studies have even found a heavy-tailed pattern in travel emissions: *a tiny fraction of trips can generate the majority of emissions*. For example, in one analysis only 2.7% of (long-distance) trips were responsible for nearly 69% of passenger travel CO₂ emissions ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-024-01561-3#:~:text=Our%20finding%20of%20a%20disproportionately,trips%20and%20miles%20in%202018)). In short, **private car travel and long-haul flights are major carbon contributors**, whereas trains, public transit, and active modes (walking/cycling) have much smaller footprints per passenger.

## Impact on Optimal Capacity Configuration  
Given this skewed carbon distribution, designing an **optimal transport capacity** means **shifting emphasis toward low-carbon travel modes**. Since high-emission modes take up a large slice of the carbon pie, planners can achieve outsized emission cuts by reallocating capacity to cleaner alternatives. In practice, this means **expanding infrastructure and services for low-carbon modes** while **limiting capacity growth for carbon-intensive ones**. For example, investing in **high-capacity public transit and rail systems** can absorb trips that would otherwise be made by car or plane, significantly reducing per-passenger emissions. Replacing a car trip with a train ride can slash emissions by on the order of 80% ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint#:~:text=Public%20transport%20is%20usually%20your,7)), and using rail instead of a short-haul flight cuts emissions by as much as ~86% ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint#:~:text=Public%20transport%20is%20usually%20your,7)). Likewise, improving **walking and cycling infrastructure** for local travel allows many short car trips to be avoided – using a bike instead of a car for a short journey reduces that trip’s emissions by ~75% ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint#:~:text=Over%20short%20to%20medium%20distances%2C,3)). By **boosting the capacity and convenience of these cleaner modes**, travellers are nudged toward them, gradually shifting the overall mode share to a lower-carbon mix.

On the flip side, an optimal strategy avoids simply adding capacity to high-emission modes. Expanding **road capacity for cars** or building new airports without restraint can be counterproductive from a climate perspective. More highway lanes tend to induce more driving demand – effectively **“filling up” the new capacity with additional car trips, leading to higher emissions** ([t4america.org](https://t4america.org/2021/10/20/say-hello-to-induced-demand/#:~:text=driving%2C%20which%20leads%20to%20more,increase%20in%20lane%20miles%20leads)). This induced demand means that **widening roads often results in more vehicles, more fuel burned, and greater CO₂ output**, undermining emission-reduction goals. Instead, managing road travel through measures like congestion pricing, high-occupancy vehicle lanes, or improved land-use planning can prevent excess car use and encourage travelers onto greener modes. In essence, **capacity planning must internalize carbon costs**: if a mode is very carbon-intensive, the optimal plan will constrain its growth or require cleaner technology, whereas low-carbon modes should see robust capacity investment.

## Strategies for Low-Carbon Capacity Allocation  
In light of the above, policymakers and planners configure transport capacity with an eye on carbon efficiency:  

- **Prioritize Low-Emission Modes:** Increase the **capacity of trains, trams, buses, and rapid transit** networks. These modes have far lower emissions per passenger-kilometer than single-occupancy cars or planes ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint#:~:text=Public%20transport%20is%20usually%20your,7)), so enabling more people to use them dramatically lowers total emissions. This can involve building new rail lines, dedicated bus lanes, or better transit frequency and coverage.  

- **Expand Active Travel Infrastructure:** Develop safe, extensive **walking and cycling networks**. For short trips, walking and biking produce virtually no carbon emissions. Providing sidewalks, bike lanes, and bike-sharing programs gives travelers a viable, low-carbon alternative for local mobility – turning many drive-alone trips into zero-emission trips ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint#:~:text=Over%20short%20to%20medium%20distances%2C,3)).  

- **Avoid Carbon-Intensive Expansion:** **Resist adding excessive road and airport capacity** unless absolutely necessary. New highways tend to fill up with extra traffic (induced demand), which directly increases GHG emissions ([t4america.org](https://t4america.org/2021/10/20/say-hello-to-induced-demand/#:~:text=driving%2C%20which%20leads%20to%20more,increase%20in%20lane%20miles%20leads)). A more optimal approach is to manage demand on existing roads (through traffic management, pricing, or encouraging carpooling) rather than simply building more lanes. Similarly, supporting regional rail or coach services can often handle travel growth without expanding short-haul flight capacity.  

- **Align with Electrification and Efficiency:** Incorporate emerging **low-carbon technologies** into capacity plans. For instance, facilitating the shift to electric buses and cars (with charging infrastructure) can redistribute the “emission load” from tailpipes to a cleaner electricity grid, lowering overall transport emissions. As the energy grid decarbonizes, electric vehicles make even car travel much cleaner, changing the emission distribution. Optimal capacity planning takes this into account by ensuring charging capacity and grid support for EVs, and perhaps slightly less emphasis on mode shift if vehicles themselves become clean. However, even with electrification, **energy and space efficiency** make public and active transport crucial – an electric train or bus moving many people is still more efficient than numerous electric cars.  

In summary, **carbon emission distribution strongly guides how we configure transport capacity across modes**. Because emissions are concentrated in certain modes (notably personal cars and aviation), an optimal, climate-friendly capacity plan redistributes resources toward greener modes and high-efficiency systems. By doing so, it addresses the biggest emission sources first and maximizes the carbon reduction “bang for the buck.” This means **building ample capacity for low-carbon travel options and avoiding investments that lock in high-carbon travel patterns**. The result is a transportation network designed to meet mobility needs while keeping carbon emissions as low as possible – for example, cities with robust transit and bike networks, well-utilized rail lines for intercity travel, and fewer incentives to drive long distances. Such a configuration leverages the differences in carbon intensity between modes to reduce the overall footprint of travel without sacrificing connectivity ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/travel-carbon-footprint#:~:text=Public%20transport%20is%20usually%20your,7)) ([ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-transport#:~:text=Road%20travel%20accounts%20for%20three,comes%20from%20trucks%20carrying%20freight)). Ultimately, aligning capacity with carbon-efficient modes is a key strategy for reaching climate targets in the transport sector while still moving people and goods effectively.
