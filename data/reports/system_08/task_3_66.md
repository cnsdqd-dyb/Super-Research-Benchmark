# Kinetic Control in Rare Earth Extraction and Process Economics

## Kinetic vs. Equilibrium Control in Rare Earth Separation  
Rare earth element (REE) separations are notoriously difficult and **time-consuming** because the REEs have very similar chemistry ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168127318300199#:~:text=industrial,However%2C%20the%20kinetics%20of)). Traditional extraction models often assume near-equilibrium conditions in each step, but **kinetic control** can dominate certain stages. A *kinetically controlled step* is one where the reaction or mass transfer rate (rather than just equilibrium thermodynamics) limits how fast the process proceeds. In rare earth extraction from **waste materials** (like e-waste, spent magnets, or mine tailings), such kinetic limitations are common — for example, slow leaching rates due to diffusion in solid waste matrices or sluggish solvent extraction transfer rates. These kinetic bottlenecks mean that the process can’t reach equilibrium instantly, requiring **longer contact times or more intense conditions** to achieve the desired REE recovery and purity.  

## Impact on Throughput and Equipment Requirements  
When an extraction step is under kinetic control, it directly **reduces throughput** unless compensated by process design. In solvent extraction, for instance, limited mass-transfer rates per stage mean you may need more stages or longer residence times to achieve the same separation ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168127318300199#:~:text=The%20kinetics%20of%20rare%20earth,and%20ultimately%20the%20cost%20of)). Slower kinetics also often necessitate **larger equipment** (to hold material for longer durations) or additional units in parallel to process the same amount of material. All of this increases **capital costs**. As one study notes, the kinetic aspects of REE separations are *“at least as important as thermodynamic selectivity in determining the throughput”*, and faster kinetics allow materials to flow more quickly through the system ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168127318300199#:~:text=However%2C%20the%20kinetic%20aspects%20of,or%20more%20rare%20earth%20species)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168127318300199#:~:text=The%20kinetics%20of%20rare%20earth,and%20ultimately%20the%20cost%20of)). In short, a slow, diffusion-limited leaching or extraction step can become a **rate-limiting bottleneck**, requiring more time or infrastructure to achieve production targets. This means **higher costs** for tanks, mixers, or reactors and potentially more **energy input** (e.g. heating, grinding, or stirring to speed up the kinetics).  

## Effect on Process Economics for Waste REE Sources  
Yes – **kinetic control steps strongly affect process economics** in rare earth recovery from waste. Because waste-based feedstocks (like coal ash, phosphogypsum, or electronic scrap) often have REEs in forms that are harder to extract, the kinetics of leaching or extraction can be slow. For example, REE leaching from clays or tailings might be **diffusion-controlled**, meaning only a fraction of the REEs come out quickly while the rest trickle out slowly ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-163X/13/12/1503#:~:text=REEs%20and%20cations%20of%20interest,diffusive%20leaching%20than%20REE%20species)). To overcome this, processors might need to **grind materials finely**, **increase temperature**, or use chemical additives – all of which add cost. If such kinetic limitations are not addressed, the process must run longer or with lower throughput, driving up **operational costs** per unit of REE recovered. In contrast, optimizing or accelerating those kinetic steps can **improve efficiency and reduce costs**. As researchers have emphasized, understanding and improving kinetics is an often overlooked route to boost separation performance and cut costs ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168127318300199#:~:text=industrial,However%2C%20the%20kinetics%20of)). 

**Including kinetic limitations in extraction models** is crucial for realistic economic assessments. When kinetics are accounted for, the model may show the need for longer cycle times or more stages, alerting engineers that the baseline process might be too slow to be economical. This can influence decisions like whether to invest in **process intensification** (for instance, using agitation, microwaves, or catalysts to speed up reactions) or to choose a different separation method altogether. A clear example is a recent study where applying a tailored magnetic field accelerated the solvent extraction kinetics of dysprosium; this **speed-up allowed lower energy and chemical use, directly reducing costs and making REE recycling from end-of-life products more attractive** ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-025-02393-7#:~:text=is%20that%20the%20extraction%20also,life%20products%20more%20attractive)). In other words, faster kinetics improved the viability of recovering rare earths from waste by **shrinking processing time and resource consumption**.

## Conclusion  
In summary, kinetic control steps absolutely **affect the economics** of rare earth separation from waste streams. **Sluggish kinetics can increase capital and operating costs** by requiring more equipment, time, or energy, thus hurting economic feasibility. Conversely, if you can improve or bypass kinetic bottlenecks, you can **increase throughput and reduce unit costs**, enhancing the overall economics of REE recycling or extraction from secondary sources. Therefore, engineers and researchers pay close attention to kinetics in extraction models to design cost-effective rare earth recovery processes ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168127318300199#:~:text=The%20kinetics%20of%20rare%20earth,and%20ultimately%20the%20cost%20of)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-025-02393-7#:~:text=is%20that%20the%20extraction%20also,life%20products%20more%20attractive)). The bottom line is that **better kinetic control (or faster reaction rates) translates to more practical and economically viable rare earth separations**, especially when dealing with challenging waste-derived feedstocks.
