# Balancing rTMS Efficacy and Seizure Risk in Depression

## Indications and Therapeutic Response Thresholds

Repetitive TMS is indicated for **major depressive disorder**, especially in treatment-resistant cases where patients haven’t responded to standard antidepressants ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4994792/#:~:text=,Level%203)). In such cases, the **potential benefits of rTMS** (approximately 40–60% response rates in treatment-resistant depression) justify its use ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9063593/#:~:text=compulsive%20disorder%2C%20and%20smoking%20cessation%2C,patients%20undergoing%20TMS%2C%20device%20selection)). Achieving a therapeutic antidepressant effect with rTMS requires delivering **sufficient stimulation intensity and frequency**. To ensure an adequate dose, clinicians calibrate the device to each patient’s motor threshold (the minimum intensity that elicits a motor response) and then stimulate at a suprathreshold level. In practice, **stimulation is typically set at ~120% of the resting motor threshold** for high-frequency rTMS, which provides an intensity above the minimal necessary for clinical efficacy ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1388245724003766#:~:text=To%20minimize%20seizure%20risk%2C%20adequate,baseline%20testing%20is%20imperative%2C%20the)). For example, a standard protocol targets the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex at **10 Hz with 120% motor threshold intensity**, delivering about 3,000 pulses per session ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5846193/#:~:text=Magnetic%20field%20intensity%20relative%20to,up%20study)). This dosage is considered a **therapeutic response threshold** – high enough to induce lasting neural changes and improve depressive symptoms, as studies show that intensities **above 100% of motor threshold** and adequate pulse numbers per week yield better antidepressant outcomes ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096206/#:~:text=headache%20,not%20reported)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5846193/#:~:text=Magnetic%20field%20intensity%20relative%20to,up%20study)). In summary, rTMS is used in depression when medication results are insufficient, and it is administered at a carefully calibrated dose (e.g. 120% MT at 10–18 Hz) to cross the threshold needed for clinical response. 

## Seizure Risk and Safety Boundaries

Although rTMS is generally very safe, the **most serious risk is an induced seizure**, which remains exceedingly rare with approved parameters (estimated in the range of ~0.01%–0.1% incidence) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4994792/#:~:text=The%20most%20serious%20rTMS%20adverse,spontaneous%20incidence%20in%20the)). To **balance efficacy with safety**, rTMS protocols adhere to well-defined safety boundaries that keep stimulation well below the level that would trigger a seizure. In fact, international safety guidelines note that conventional rTMS should never approach the intense parameters used in *magnetic seizure therapy* (MST), where seizures are intentionally induced. For reference, MST typically uses about **25 Hz stimulation at 100% machine output in a 10-second train** to provoke a seizure; **standard rTMS treatments stay far under this combination of intensity, frequency, and train duration** ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9094636/#:~:text=parameters%20of%20stimulation%20used%20for,below%20this%20combination%20of%20parameters)). High-frequency rTMS for depression employs shorter pulse trains and mandatory rest intervals to prevent excessive neural excitation. For example, a 10 Hz protocol uses 4-second trains with ~26 s pauses, and even higher frequencies (such as 18 Hz in deep TMS) use **very brief trains (≈2 s) with long inter-train intervals** ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5846193/#:~:text=Magnetic%20field%20intensity%20relative%20to,up%20study)). This pacing limits the total pulses per burst (e.g. ~40 pulses/train at 10 Hz, or 36 pulses at 18 Hz) and gives the cortex time to recover between stimulation bursts ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5846193/#:~:text=Magnetic%20field%20intensity%20relative%20to,up%20study)). By capping train length and incorporating pauses, the **overall stimulation density** stays within safe limits, greatly reducing seizure risk while still delivering a sufficient cumulative dose of ~1,500–3,000 pulses per session for antidepressant effect ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5846193/#:~:text=Magnetic%20field%20intensity%20relative%20to,up%20study)).

Patient **screening and session monitoring** provide additional safety buffers. Before rTMS treatment, patients are evaluated for any factors that lower seizure threshold – such as a personal or family history of epilepsy, recent alcohol or drug use, or sleep deprivation – and these are addressed or avoided during the rTMS course ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096206/#:~:text=,has%20to%20be%20ensured)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9094636/#:~:text=threshold%20may%20increase%20the%20probability,increased%20risk%20compared%20to%20the)). Patients are instructed to get adequate sleep and refrain from substances like alcohol or stimulants before sessions to minimize any lowering of their seizure threshold ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096206/#:~:text=,has%20to%20be%20ensured)). Throughout the treatment, staff remain vigilant for any **warning signs of excessive cortical excitation** (for instance, involuntary motor twitches during stimulation), which could indicate parameters are too intense ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096206/#:~:text=Safety%20parameters%20of%20stimulation%20defined,a%20warning%20for%20increased%20cortical)). If any seizure were to occur (again, an extremely infrequent event), protocols dictate stopping treatment and re-evaluating the stimulation parameters before proceeding ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096206/#:~:text=Safety%20parameters%20of%20stimulation%20defined,a%20warning%20for%20increased%20cortical)).

In summary, **rTMS for depression is used in patients who need a non-medication therapy and is delivered at a dose above a critical threshold for antidepressant response but below the threshold of undue excitation**. The standard parameters (e.g. ~120% MT intensity, ≤10–20 Hz frequency, with brief trains and pauses) are deliberately chosen to maximize therapeutic brain stimulation while **staying well inside safety boundaries** to virtually eliminate the risk of seizures ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9094636/#:~:text=parameters%20of%20stimulation%20used%20for,below%20this%20combination%20of%20parameters)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4994792/#:~:text=The%20most%20serious%20rTMS%20adverse,spontaneous%20incidence%20in%20the)). With these precautions, rTMS offers an effective treatment for depression that balances robust neural activation against a very low probability of serious adverse events. The result is a favorable risk–benefit profile: patients receive a potent antidepressant intervention that meets the needed response threshold, and the strict safety limits keep the chance of a stimulation-induced seizure extraordinarily low (on the order of only a few cases per tens of thousands of sessions) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4994792/#:~:text=The%20most%20serious%20rTMS%20adverse,spontaneous%20incidence%20in%20the)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096206/#:~:text=epileptic%20seizures%29%20disorders,areas%20such%20as%20cerebellum%20for)).
