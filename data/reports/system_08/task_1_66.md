# Markov Chain Characteristics of Activity Sequences

Human daily activity sequences often exhibit **Markovian characteristics**, meaning the next activity in a person’s day is largely dependent on their current (or most recent) activity state. In other words, activity patterns have a short memory: what you do next is closely tied to what you are doing now, rather than a long history of past actions. Empirical studies support this Markov-chain-like behavior. For example, researchers have found that the **type and timing of an upcoming activity depend strongly on the type and duration of the last activity** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=predicting%20the%20next%20activity,the%20duration%20of%20previous%20activities)). Such findings imply that a low-order Markov process (often first-order, considering just the previous activity) can capture much of the sequential structure in daily routines. In mobility modeling, even simple Markov Chain models (with states like *home*, *work*, *other*) have achieved high accuracy in predicting an individual’s next location or activity when calibrated per person ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=Since%20daily%20movements%20are%20driven,states%20of)). This **Markov chain character** – the memoryless or limited-memory property of activity transitions – makes these models straightforward and effective in replicating observed patterns of behavior.

However, real-world activity sequences are not solely governed by immediate past activities; they are also shaped by contextual factors. Markov models that assume fixed transition probabilities work well under stable conditions, but they **treat the pattern-generation process as inherently stochastic and stationary**, without direct inputs for external influences like socioeconomic context. This raises the question of how reliable such predictions remain when those external conditions change.

## Role of Socioeconomic Attributes in Activity Patterns

Individual and household **socioeconomic attributes** (like employment status, presence of children, income level, age, etc.) play a significant role in determining activity-travel patterns. In fact, *who you are* often determines *how you travel and what activities you do*. Recent research using a Markov-chain-based mixture model for clustering daily activity patterns found **distinct classes of behavior (e.g. work-oriented days, recreation-oriented days, school-run-oriented days)**, and these classes **correspond to specific socio-demographic groups** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214367X21000259#:~:text=Socio,to%20determine%20daily%20activity%20scheduling)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214367X21000259#:~:text=corresponds%20to%20specific%20socio,people%20in%20urban%20transportation%20planning)). For instance, one cluster of activity sequences was characterized by regular work commutes, another by shopping/leisure activities, and a third by school drop-off/pick-up routines – each cluster aligning with different life-cycle stages and household types (workers with no young children, adults in shopping/leisure lifestyle, parents with school-age children, etc.) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214367X21000259#:~:text=built%20to%20test%20hypothetical%20relationships,off.%20Each%20pattern)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214367X21000259#:~:text=corresponds%20to%20specific%20socio,people%20in%20urban%20transportation%20planning)). This underscores that **people with different socio-demographic profiles tend to have systematically different activity sequences**. 

Socioeconomic attributes essentially condition the *transition probabilities* between activities. A person’s likelihood of going from “home” to “work” in the morning, or from “work” to “gym” in the evening, for example, is influenced by factors like their job flexibility, family commitments, income (which might enable certain leisure activities), and so on. Therefore, **activity sequences are not homogeneous across a population** – they vary with demographics and context ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214367X21000259#:~:text=Socio,to%20determine%20daily%20activity%20scheduling)). Modelers often account for this by segmenting the population into homogeneous groups or by conditioning the model on these attributes. Indeed, a travel-behavior forecasting framework in Belgium demonstrated that it could **“catch the behavioral effects of predictor variables that vary by group”** by using separate Markov-chain-based sub-models for different segments ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0968090X1630095X#:~:text=)). In other words, if we acknowledge that socioeconomic differences yield different Markov chains for activity transitions, we can improve predictive accuracy by tailoring the model to those differences.

## Impact of Changing Socioeconomic Attributes on Prediction Reliability

Because socio-demographic factors heavily influence activity patterns, **changes in those attributes over time or across scenarios can lead to changes in the underlying Markov transition probabilities**. This has direct implications for prediction reliability. A Markov chain model trained under one set of socio-economic conditions may become **less reliable if applied to a population with different or evolving attributes**, unless the model is adjusted. For example, consider an individual who transitions from being single to having young children – their daily schedule (and thus activity sequence) will likely shift from a recreation-oriented pattern to a school-drop-off-oriented pattern. A prediction model that doesn’t account for this change (and continues to use the old transition probabilities of the single, recreation-oriented lifestyle) will mis-predict the person’s activities with increasing error. In general, when a population’s characteristics shift – e.g. an aging population, changes in employment rates, land-use developments, or policy changes – the **previously observed activity sequences and transitions may no longer hold**.

Studies have indeed observed that activity patterns shift alongside socio-economic and environmental changes. One longitudinal analysis of Tokyo over 30 years found that **daily activity-transition probabilities changed in response to factors like transportation infrastructure improvements, land-use developments, and demographic trends** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=development%20of%20public%20transportation%20%28e,classify%20regions%20according%20to%20the)). For instance, as public transit expanded and city-center land use evolved, people became less likely to remain at home in the next time interval and slightly less likely to engage in work/school at certain times ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=observed%2C%20although%20the%20blue%20pattern,This%20means%20that%20human)). These shifts correlated with **contextual factors** (urban form, transit, etc.), implying that the Markov chain of activities was not stationary over time ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=development%20of%20public%20transportation%20%28e,classify%20regions%20according%20to%20the)). The authors note that other factors such as an aging population and economic trends likely also contributed to changing activity patterns, even though those were outside the scope of their analysis ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=activity%20patterns%20,classify%20regions%20according%20to%20the)). The key takeaway is that **the probabilities governing activity sequences are not fixed**: they evolve when underlying conditions (personal or societal) evolve.

Therefore, if one naively uses a Markov chain model calibrated on past behavior to forecast future behavior **without accounting for socio-economic changes**, the **prediction reliability can degrade**. The Markov model might confidently predict a continuation of old patterns that no longer reflect people’s actual choices under new conditions. This is essentially a **transferability problem** – the model’s accuracy transfers poorly to a new context because the Markov chain character was tied to the original context’s statistics. In short, the **Markovian assumption alone doesn’t guarantee robustness to external changes**. The model needs to somehow incorporate those external drivers of change to remain reliable.

## Adapting Models for Socioeconomic Changes

To maintain or improve prediction reliability under changing socio-economic conditions, the Markov chain approach must be **augmented or adapted**. One straightforward strategy is to incorporate socio-demographic attributes *into* the model – effectively conditioning the Markov transitions on those attributes. In practice, this can mean segmenting the population and estimating separate transition matrices for each segment (e.g. different matrices for different age groups, employment status, or household types) or even dynamically updating the transition probabilities as attributes change. Research shows that doing so yields better fidelity. For example, segmenting by region (an indirect proxy for certain socio-economic and land-use characteristics) and constructing distinct Markov chains per region improved the generation accuracy of activity sequences ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=be%20presented%20here,15)). Likewise, the earlier example from Belgium used group-specific sub-models to reflect heterogeneity across socio-demographic groups ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0968090X1630095X#:~:text=)). By **aligning the model’s transition probabilities with the appropriate context or group**, predictions become more reliable when conditions differ.

Another approach is to use **time-varying (nonhomogeneous) Markov chains** or other extensions that allow transition probabilities to change according to explanatory variables (time of day, policy scenario, etc.). In the literature on activity-based travel models, alternatives like utility-based models or rule-based simulations are favored for scenario forecasting specifically because they can take inputs like “new land-use policy” or “changed income distribution” and produce modified activity patterns ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=models%2C%20such%20as%20CEMDAP%20,process%20models%2C%20such%20as%20TASHA)). These models embed causal variables (land use, socio-demographics, etc.) in the prediction process. A pure Markov chain model that doesn’t include such variables would have to be re-calibrated with new data for the new scenario, since it cannot *on its own* extrapolate how a change in an external factor should alter the sequence probabilities. In essence, **the Markov chain model needs contextual information to handle scenario changes**. If it remains context-blind, its reliability suffers whenever the underlying context shifts.

In summary, **the Markov chain character of activity sequences does affect prediction reliability under changing socioeconomic attributes**. The Markov property itself (capturing short-term dependencies) isn’t a flaw – indeed, it’s a useful trait of human activity patterns – but a Markov chain model calibrated under one set of socio-economic conditions can become **out-of-sync with reality if those conditions change**. To ensure reliable predictions, one must update the model’s transition structure to reflect new socio-demographic realities or incorporate those attributes directly into the predictive framework. Studies have shown that accounting for socio-demographic heterogeneity and contextual factors leads to more robust models, whereas ignoring them can yield biased or inaccurate forecasts ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214367X21000259#:~:text=Socio,to%20determine%20daily%20activity%20scheduling)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=development%20of%20public%20transportation%20%28e,classify%20regions%20according%20to%20the)). Thus, when applying Markov-chain-based predictions to a scenario with different or evolving socioeconomic attributes, it is critical to adjust the model accordingly – either via segmentation, conditioning on attributes, or using hybrid approaches – to maintain predictive reliability. 

**Key takeaways:** The **memoryless (Markov) structure** of activity sequences means current state is a strong predictor of next state ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=predicting%20the%20next%20activity,the%20duration%20of%20previous%20activities)), but **who the person is and the context they live in heavily influence those state-to-state probabilities** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214367X21000259#:~:text=Socio,to%20determine%20daily%20activity%20scheduling)). If those socioeconomic contexts change, a static Markov model’s reliability will drop. Incorporating changing attributes into the model (or re-estimating the Markov chain under new conditions) is necessary to keep predictions accurate ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=models%2C%20such%20as%20CEMDAP%20,process%20models%2C%20such%20as%20TASHA)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214367X2300162X#:~:text=development%20of%20public%20transportation%20%28e,classify%20regions%20according%20to%20the)). In practice, **Markov-chain-based models should be used with caution for scenarios unlike the calibration data** – without adjustment they risk **baking in old patterns that may no longer hold**, reducing the credibility of their forecasts.
