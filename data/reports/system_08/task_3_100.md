# Beyond Anthropocentrism: Genome Editing Ethics and Cross-Species Justice

## From Anthropocentric Ethics to Cross-Species Justice  
Traditional bioethics has been largely *anthropocentric*, focusing on human well-being and rights. Moral frameworks and laws (like human rights or medical ethics) have centered on humans as the primary subjects of justice. Non-human life – animals, plants, ecosystems – has often been considered only in relation to human interests (for example, protecting the environment to benefit human health). This human-centered outlook, sometimes called **human exceptionalism**, treats humanity as above or apart from the rest of nature ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850483/#:~:text=equally%20bioethically%20demanding%3A%20both%20are,In%20this)). In contrast, **cross-species justice** (or *multispecies justice*) calls for extending moral consideration and fairness across species boundaries. It challenges the hierarchy that places humans at the top, and instead asks how our obligations and ethical principles can include other living beings. In recent years, philosophers and ethicists have argued that justice “must decentralize the human” – in other words, we need new ethical frameworks that **displace human exceptionalism** and *“centralize more-than-human”* interests alongside human ones ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850483/#:~:text=demonstrate%20the%20limits%20of%20human,human)). This shift means recognizing that animals, and even entire ecosystems, have moral value not just as tools for human use but as entities deserving respect and care in their own right.

## Genome Editing and the Power to Alter Other Species  
The rise of **genome editing technologies** (like CRISPR-Cas9) has made these questions urgent. With tools that allow us to **directly modify the DNA of any organism**, humans have an unprecedented power over other forms of life. Scientists are already editing the genes of many species – from livestock and lab animals to wild insects and plants – for various purposes. For example, researchers have created disease-resistant farm animals, engineered mosquitoes to curb malaria, and considered gene-editing coral or endangered species to help them survive climate change ([aeon.co](https://aeon.co/essays/should-we-intervene-in-evolution-the-ethics-of-editing-nature#:~:text=The%20emergence%20of%20precision%20gene,edited%20to%20cope%20with%20drought)) ([aeon.co](https://aeon.co/essays/should-we-intervene-in-evolution-the-ethics-of-editing-nature#:~:text=pollution%20and%20consume%20toxins%2C%20even,spare%20%2019%20from%20collapse)). These applications blur the boundaries between species and entwine our fate with that of countless other creatures. As one essay put it, humanity has unintentionally become “the planet’s greatest evolutionary force,” and now we face *“an urgent and difficult question”*: If many species cannot adapt to rapid, human-driven environmental change, **should we intervene in their evolution to save them?** ([aeon.co](https://aeon.co/essays/should-we-intervene-in-evolution-the-ethics-of-editing-nature#:~:text=It%20wasn%E2%80%99t%20our%20intention%20that,deliberately%20intervening%20in%20their%20evolution)) ([aeon.co](https://aeon.co/essays/should-we-intervene-in-evolution-the-ethics-of-editing-nature#:~:text=evolutionary%20force%3B%20yet%20the%20fact,deliberately%20intervening%20in%20their%20evolution)). In other words, genome editing gives us the **God-like capability** to reshape life itself – but with that power comes ethical responsibility. 

**Genome editing ethics** examines not only what we *can* do to the genes of humans or other organisms, but what we *should* do. Initially, much of the debate revolved around human health and safety (e.g. editing human embryos to prevent disease) and the potential risks of releasing gene-edited organisms into the environment. However, a striking feature of early discussions is how *narrow* their focus often was. A 2019 review of the academic literature on editing non-human animals found **“an underrepresentation of animal interests”** – most arguments for or against animal genome editing were framed around human benefits (like agriculture or biomedical research) or general risks, rather than the impacts on the animals themselves ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331989725_The_ethics_of_genome_editing_in_non-human_animals_a_systematic_review_of_reasons_reported_in_the_academic_literature#:~:text=disciplinary%20diversity%20in%20the%20contributing,%E2%80%98The%20ecology%20and%20evolution%20of)) ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331989725_The_ethics_of_genome_editing_in_non-human_animals_a_systematic_review_of_reasons_reported_in_the_academic_literature#:~:text=animal%20welfare%2C%20animal%20dignity%2C%20environmental,)). In other words, the ethical conversation was frequently anthropocentric, concerned chiefly with how genome editing might help or harm people, while treating animal welfare or integrity as a secondary matter. This gap has prompted calls for a **broader ethical lens**. As the review’s authors conclude, we need more voices and disciplines involved to **incorporate animal interests** into the debate and “further discuss the aims and methods of public involvement” in guiding genome editing’s future ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331989725_The_ethics_of_genome_editing_in_non-human_animals_a_systematic_review_of_reasons_reported_in_the_academic_literature#:~:text=disciplinary%20diversity%20in%20the%20contributing,%E2%80%98The%20ecology%20and%20evolution%20of)).

## Redefining Ethical Duties Beyond Humans  
**Moving beyond anthropocentrism in genome editing ethics means reframing our duties** to include the well-being and rights of other species affected by our interventions. One key area is **animal welfare.** If we edit the genomes of animals – whether in labs, farms, or wild ecosystems – a cross-species justice perspective demands that we consider how those animals are impacted, not just how humans benefit. For instance, gene editing could be used to improve farmed animals’ health and reduce suffering (such as breeding pigs resistant to disease, so they don’t need painful treatments). Done responsibly, this might **serve both human interests and the animals’ interests**, aligning with a more-than-human ethical outlook. However, there is also the risk of using genome editing to further exploit animals – for example, engineering livestock solely to increase productivity at the cost of the animal’s natural behaviors or health. Ethicists caution that **treating animals as mere objects or tools** in this way is unjust. They argue that animals have their own *telos* (an inherent nature and needs) which deserves respect. Tampering with an animal’s genome “could impinge on an animal’s dignity and thereby harm that animal even if its welfare is improved” if, say, the change forces the creature to live in a way that violates its instincts or identity ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331989725_The_ethics_of_genome_editing_in_non-human_animals_a_systematic_review_of_reasons_reported_in_the_academic_literature#:~:text=genome%20editing%20could%20impinge%20on,an%20animal%E2%80%99s%20dignity%20and)) ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331989725_The_ethics_of_genome_editing_in_non-human_animals_a_systematic_review_of_reasons_reported_in_the_academic_literature#:~:text=could%20impinge%20on%20animals%E2%80%99%20dignity,animal%20is%20a%20failure%20to)). In light of this, independent ethics bodies have urged that **animal welfare and integrity be placed at the center** of any plans to introduce genome editing in agriculture – not as an afterthought ([www.nuffieldbioethics.org](https://www.nuffieldbioethics.org/news-blog/act-now-to-ensure-animal-welfare-is-at-the-heart-of-plans-to-introduce-genome-editing-into-farmed-animal-breeding-says-independent-ethics-body/#:~:text=genome%20editing%20into%20farmed%20animal,on%20the%20Government%20to%20put)). By ensuring that reducing suffering and respecting the animal’s nature are primary goals, we begin to **align biotechnology with cross-species justice** rather than narrow human profit. 

Another aspect is the consideration of **animal rights or moral status**. Some philosophers argue that possession of a “human” genome is not a valid dividing line for basic rights or dignity – it’s the capacities of a being (such as the ability to feel pain, to have interests, or to exercise agency) that matter morally ([www.implications-philosophiques.org](https://www.implications-philosophiques.org/heritable-human-genome-editing-the-bioethical-battle-for-the-basis-and-future-of-human-rights/#:~:text=human%20genome%2C%20which%20is%2C%20in,71)). If we adopt this view, then *some non-human animals merit similar fundamental protections as humans* because they share many of these capacities (for example, primates, whales, pigs, or mice all feel pain and can suffer). Indeed, the more we learn about genetics and biology, the clearer it becomes that humans are not magically separate from other animals – we are part of a continuum of life. Genome editing itself underscores this continuity: scientists routinely transfer genes between species (e.g. using jellyfish genes to make a monkey glow, or human genes to create mouse models of disease). Such practices force us to confront the ethical **“species boundary”** directly. If we can engineer hybrids or chimeras (organisms with mixed human and animal cells), it challenges the black-and-white idea that humans are entirely unique. For example, researchers have inserted human stem cells into monkey embryos to explore disease treatments. This raises uncomfortable questions: could a chimeric animal develop human-like consciousness or traits, and if so, **how should we treat it**? A strictly anthropocentric ethic offers little guidance here, whereas a cross-species justice approach insists we err on the side of compassion and rights for any being with significant signs of sentience or personhood. In practical terms, scientists are already developing guidelines to limit suffering and avoid creating animals with ambiguous moral status (such as not allowing a chimeric embryo to develop a humanized brain beyond a certain point). The underlying principle is that **we must expand our moral circle** – if we create animals more like us, we owe them greater protection, but even those unlike us deserve consideration simply because they *can be harmed* by our actions.

## Ecological Justice and the Shared Biosphere  
Cross-species ethics also extends beyond individual animals to **whole species and ecosystems**. With genome editing, humans might deliberately alter or even drive the extinction of species in the wild. For instance, researchers have developed gene drives (a genetic mechanism) aimed at *crashing populations* of malaria-carrying mosquitoes by spreading lethal genes – a strategy to save human lives and possibly spare other animals (like birds or mammals) from mosquito-borne diseases. But is it **just** to eradicate an entire species, even a disease-causing one? An anthropocentric view might answer yes if it benefits humanity and our closer companions. A more ecocentric or cross-species justice view would weigh the mosquito’s role in its ecosystem and the precedent of humans deciding unilaterally which lives have value. It asks us to consider the intrinsic value of all species, not only their utility or threat to humans. Even seemingly “pest” creatures are part of a larger ecological community; removing them could have ripple effects on predators, prey, and environmental balance. Thus, some ethicists urge caution and humility: using gene editing to solve one problem (like disease) could inadvertently cause others, and we have a responsibility to *all* affected creatures, not just ourselves. 

On the other hand, genome editing might be used in **proactive conservation** – a form of justice for species imperiled by human activity. We are in an age of mass extinction largely caused by human-induced climate change, habitat loss, and pollution. From a justice perspective, humans have *wronged* many wildlife populations; one could argue we have an obligation to make amends or at least prevent further harm. Gene editing offers tools to assist other species: for example, giving endangered trees resistance to an introduced blight, or helping corals survive warmer oceans ([aeon.co](https://aeon.co/essays/should-we-intervene-in-evolution-the-ethics-of-editing-nature#:~:text=The%20emergence%20of%20precision%20gene,edited%20to%20cope%20with%20drought)) ([aeon.co](https://aeon.co/essays/should-we-intervene-in-evolution-the-ethics-of-editing-nature#:~:text=pollution%20and%20consume%20toxins%2C%20even,spare%20%2019%20from%20collapse)). Some scientists talk about *assisted evolution* or *resurrection biology* (de-extinction) as ways to restore what humans have destroyed. However, even benevolent interventions raise ethical questions. We must ask **who gets to decide** which species to save or revive and how, and whether such genetic fixes address root causes or just treat symptoms of deeper problems (like climate change). A cross-species justice framework would insist that our efforts respect the **good of the organisms and ecosystems themselves**, not just human sentimental preferences. For instance, reviving a long-extinct animal (like the mammoth) might satisfy human curiosity, but it could be cruel to the created animals or disruptive to contemporary ecosystems if done carelessly. Truly post-anthropocentric ethics would prioritize interventions that *help species flourish in their natural context*, and would involve international, interdisciplinary deliberation – including ethicists, ecologists, and local communities – to judge what is fair and beneficial in the web of life. As one bioethics analysis of global challenges noted, we must avoid frameworks that **“privilege the protection of human wellbeing, with animals and environments valued only to the extent that they support human flourishing”**, because this mindset is both harmful and inaccurate ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850483/#:~:text=equally%20bioethically%20demanding%3A%20both%20are,In%20this)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850483/#:~:text=the%20multispecies%20justice%20model%20discussed,frameworks%20discussed%20above%20would%20seemingly)). Instead, what is needed is an outlook where *more-than-human actors* (animals, microbes, forests, rivers) are seen as stakeholders in justice. This could mean, for example, giving legal rights or personhood status to certain animals or natural entities, or adopting the **“One Health”** principle that human, animal, and environmental health are inseparable ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850483/#:~:text=work%20done%20specifically%20with%20a,human)) – and extending it to a **“One Justice”** principle that recognizes interdependence and fairness across species.

## A New Mindset for Biotechnology Ethics  
At its heart, rebuilding cross-species justice through genome editing ethics is about **changing our mindset**. The advent of powerful gene-editing tools is not just a technical revolution, but a moral crossroads – a chance to rethink how we view humanity’s place in nature. Rather than approaching gene editing as simply a new means to assert control or achieve human ends, thought leaders suggest we see it as *“an opportunity for a change of mind”* ([www.scribd.com](https://www.scribd.com/document/929441303/Should-We-Intervene-in-Evolution-the-Ethics-of-Editing-Nature-Aeon-Essays#:~:text=assistance,we%20are%20to%20edit%20other)). Such a change involves cultivating *humility, responsibility,* and *empathy* toward other living beings. For millennia, humans have altered other species through domestication and breeding, often without regard for their suffering or ecological consequences. CRISPR-based editing is faster and more precise, **but the ethical stakes are higher than ever** because our interventions could be more pervasive and permanent. We face questions of **intergenerational justice** (the long-term effects on future humans and non-humans) and even **evolutionary justice** (how much we should interfere in life’s natural evolutionary processes). Grappling with these requires looking beyond our narrow self-interest. As the authors of a recent bioethics paper on global issues put it, “it is crucial to note that for an ethically sustainable definition and application of justice as a principle, new frameworks are needed… **without human exceptionalism**,” integrating social justice with environmental and animal justice in one coherent view ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850483/#:~:text=threatens%20our%20ecosystems%2C%20it%20undermines,queer)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850483/#:~:text=demonstrate%20the%20limits%20of%20human,human)). 

In practical terms, **genome editing ethics is prompting reforms** in policy and research norms that reflect cross-species considerations. For example, research guidelines now often include ethical review for animal experiments that weigh animal welfare heavily (not just scientific merit). Regulators debating the release of gene-edited organisms require ecological risk assessments and sometimes public consultation, acknowledging that the *public* often has broader concerns about animal integrity and naturalness than scientists might ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331989725_The_ethics_of_genome_editing_in_non-human_animals_a_systematic_review_of_reasons_reported_in_the_academic_literature#:~:text=disciplinary%20diversity%20in%20the%20contributing,%E2%80%98The%20ecology%20and%20evolution%20of)). International bodies like UNESCO and the Convention on Biological Diversity have started discussing frameworks for “biodiversity justice” in the context of genetic technologies. There is also a growing movement to grant certain intelligent animals (great apes, cetaceans, etc.) legal rights, partly fueled by a recognition that **genetics blurs the line** that was once used to justify human-only rights. Even human rights law is being reexamined: some scholars argue that if we ever encounter or create non-human beings with human-like agency (whether advanced AI or bio-engineered animals), our rights paradigm should evolve to include them ([www.implications-philosophiques.org](https://www.implications-philosophiques.org/heritable-human-genome-editing-the-bioethical-battle-for-the-basis-and-future-of-human-rights/#:~:text=increasing%20homogenisation%20in%20the%20human,maintained%20between%20all%20such%20beings)). All these developments indicate a **broadening of our moral framework**, infused by the reality that we share a common genetic heritage with other life and a common planet.

## Conclusion: Foundations for a Post-Anthropocentric Future  
In summary, genome editing is acting as a catalyst to **rebuild the ethical foundations of justice in a cross-species context**. By forcing us to ask how our innovations affect not only ourselves but also animals, plants, and ecosystems, it reveals the shortcomings of an anthropocentric ethic. To move forward responsibly, we must anchor our technologies in an ethic of *inclusivity and respect for life*. This means recognizing animals as stakeholders with interests – not property to mold at will – and valuing the integrity of species and ecological communities. It means scientists and policymakers working with ethicists, communities, and even considering the “voice” of the voiceless species when making decisions. Fundamentally, it calls for **expanding the circle of justice** to encompass all creatures capable of being helped or harmed by human action.

Such a transformation is both practical and philosophical. Practically, it leads to guidelines and laws that ensure humane treatment of gene-edited animals, rigorous environmental safeguards, and perhaps limits on what goals are pursued (for instance, rejecting trivial or cruel genetic modifications). Philosophically, it challenges us to redefine concepts like *“dignity,” “rights,” and “justice”* in ways that do not hinge on being human. As one legal scholar noted, human rights need not depend on “the possession of a human genome” – a concept that is scientifically blurry and morally exclusionary – but could rest on capacities or principles that **extend moral equality to *all* beings with those capacities** ([www.implications-philosophiques.org](https://www.implications-philosophiques.org/heritable-human-genome-editing-the-bioethical-battle-for-the-basis-and-future-of-human-rights/#:~:text=human%20genome%2C%20which%20is%2C%20in,71)). In a future where we might share the world with gene-edited animals, human-animal chimeras, and synthetic organisms, justice **“beyond anthropocentrism”** ensures we strive for the flourishing of *all* life, not just our own. Genome editing ethics, at its best, is helping lay these foundations by illuminating our interconnectedness and our moral duty to use new powers wisely and compassionately. In essence, it asks us to **become stewards rather than masters**, and to govern biotechnology in a way that honors the rich tapestry of life – a tapestry in which humans are but one of many important threads.
