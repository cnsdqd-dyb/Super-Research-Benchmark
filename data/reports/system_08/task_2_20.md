# WDM for Enhanced On-Chip Bandwidth and Efficiency

## Bandwidth Expansion with WDM  
Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) allows multiple optical data channels, each at a different wavelength, to share a single waveguide. This dramatically increases the communication capacity of on-chip photonic interconnects by essentially **multiplying the bandwidth** by the number of simultaneous wavelengths. For example, an on-chip photonic link can carry 8 parallel data streams on 8 different wavelengths, achieving an aggregate throughput roughly 8× higher than a single-wavelength link ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/abstract.cfm?URI=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=with%20a%2040nm%20CMOS%20VLSI,integration%20details%20and%20supporting%20link)). Optical waveguides have an extremely high carrier frequency and broad bandwidth, so they can support many such channels without the frequency crowding issues of electrical wires. Indeed, leveraging WDM yields *“unmatched aggregate bandwidth and scalable density”* in silicon-photonic interconnects ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/abstract.cfm?URI=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=enables%20silicon%20photonics%20implementations%20with,effective%20silicon%20optical%20devices%20%5B3%E2%80%937)). In practice, current on-chip WDM demonstrations use on the order of 4–16 wavelengths, which can turn a single waveguide into a multi-terabit/sec link if each channel carries tens of Gb/s. This massive bandwidth expansion is especially beneficial for multi-core processors and Network-on-Chip (NoC) architectures that need high throughput for core-to-core communication. Studies have shown that WDM-based optical NoCs provide significantly higher network bandwidth and lower latency than single-wavelength designs ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7693728/#:~:text=router%2C%20which%20is%20consistent%20with,delay%20and%20throughput%20according%20to)). In short, WDM can **substantially expand the communication bandwidth** of on-chip interconnects, far beyond what electrical or single-channel optical links can achieve, by sending many data channels in parallel over one physical link. 

## Energy Efficiency Benefits  
In addition to higher bandwidth, WDM can improve the **energy efficiency** of on-chip communications. Sending multiple data channels through a shared optical waveguide means the infrastructure (waveguide, couplers, etc.) and certain losses are amortized across many bits, often reducing the energy per bit. Each wavelength still requires its own modulator and detector, but these devices can be compact and low-power in silicon photonics. Crucially, using several moderate-speed optical channels is often more energy-efficient than pushing a single channel to extreme data rates. By parallelizing data transfer on different wavelengths, WDM avoids the sharp rise in power that typically accompanies very high single-channel bitrates (for example, receiver sensitivity and circuit power requirements worsen at higher speeds ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/abstract.cfm?URI=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=Parallel%20optical%20links%20have%20been,On%20the%20other%20hand%2C%20wavelength)) ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/abstract.cfm?URI=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=channels%20and%20by%20increasing%20the,On%20the%20other%20hand%2C%20wavelength))). Instead, multiple slower channels can achieve the same aggregate rate with lower total power. In one prototype WDM link, **eight 10 Gb/s channels** were transmitted on a single waveguide with each channel achieving an on-chip energy cost of only about **1.3 pJ per bit** (excluding the external laser power) ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/abstract.cfm?URI=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=with%20a%2040nm%20CMOS%20VLSI,integration%20details%20and%20supporting%20link)) ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/abstract.cfm?URI=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=match%20at%20L625%20average%20of,solid%20state%20photonic%20modules)). This is a highly energy-efficient figure for on-chip data transfer, demonstrating how WDM can deliver high bandwidth *and* low energy per bit. Optical interconnects also eliminate the dynamic power dissipation from charging long RC lines, a major factor in electrical links, which further improves energy efficiency. Overall, WDM enables **much higher bandwidth-per-energy** than conventional interconnects – one study notes that silicon WDM links can provide *“low-power”* high-bandwidth communication that electrical links struggle to achieve ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/abstract.cfm?URI=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=enables%20silicon%20photonics%20implementations%20with,effective%20silicon%20optical%20devices%20%5B3%E2%80%937)). It’s worth noting that the total energy must include the lasers supplying the wavelengths, but by using efficient laser sources (or sharing them among many channels) and optimizing modulators, on-chip WDM networks can significantly reduce the energy per transmitted bit compared to electrical networks-on-chip of similar performance.

## Crosstalk Constraints and Limitations  
The **challenge and limiting factor** for WDM scalability in practice is **inter-channel crosstalk**. With many closely spaced wavelengths propagating together, signals can interfere with each other through several mechanisms. One issue is **filter and component crosstalk**: on-chip multiplexers/demultiplexers (often microring resonators or AWGs) have finite isolation, so not all of a given wavelength’s power stays entirely in its intended channel – a small portion can leak into adjacent channels. Similarly, when light traverses optical routers or waveguide crossings in a network, a fraction can unintentionally couple into the wrong waveguide or wavelength, creating noise in other channels ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7693728/#:~:text=for%20WDM,based%20ONoCs%20is%20greatly%20restricted)). Another concern is **nonlinear optical crosstalk** in the silicon waveguides. When multiple high-speed signals co-propagate, nonlinear effects (especially four-wave mixing, FWM) can generate new frequency components that overlap with legitimate channels ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7693728/#:~:text=for%20WDM,based%20ONoCs%20is%20greatly%20restricted)). This *accumulation of crosstalk noise* degrades the optical signal-to-noise ratio (OSNR) and causes signal distortion, which in turn raises bit error rates. As a result, **WDM’s benefits are bounded by crosstalk** – beyond a certain point, adding more wavelengths (or increasing network size) will cause more interference than the system can tolerate, negating further bandwidth gains. 

Critically, channel crosstalk constraints mean there is a *practical limit* to how many WDM channels can be used effectively on chip. If too many wavelengths are packed too closely, the required channel spacing or filter sharpness becomes prohibitive, and the crosstalk noise can overwhelm the signals. For example, one analysis of a photonic torus NoC with 8 wavelengths per link found that **network size** could only grow to about *\[6×5\]* nodes (≈30 nodes) before the worst-case crosstalk noise power equaled or exceeded the signal power ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7693728/#:~:text=scalability%20are%20also%20revealed,optimized%20crossbar%20router%20is%20employed)). In that scenario, scaling the network further (or implicitly, adding more wavelengths per link or more endpoints) would render communication unreliable. This illustrates how crosstalk effectively **limits the scalability** of WDM-based on-chip networks. Designers must leave enough wavelength spacing or use advanced modulation/filtering schemes to keep crosstalk below tolerable levels, which in practice caps the number of WDM channels (often a dozen or so on chip with current technology). It also necessitates power budgeting – each additional channel requires sufficient optical power to maintain acceptable OSNR after crosstalk, which can drive up laser power and reduce energy efficiency if not managed carefully.

Despite these constraints, **moderate WDM channel counts** are highly effective and still well within crosstalk limits when engineered properly. Many experimental photonic interconnects use 4–8 wavelengths with negligible channel interference by spacing wavelengths and using high-Q resonators for multiplexing. At those levels, WDM yields a robust boost in bandwidth with manageable crosstalk penalties. It’s when pushing towards dense WDM (dozens of closely spaced channels) on chip that crosstalk becomes the dominant concern. In summary, WDM **substantially expands bandwidth and can improve energy per bit**, but only **up to the point** where inter-channel interference remains controlled. Beyond that, additional wavelengths contribute diminishing returns or even performance loss. As one study succinctly noted, while WDM-based optical NoCs outperform single-wavelength networks in throughput and latency, this comes *“at the cost of a more serious restriction of network scalability induced by crosstalk noise.”* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7693728/#:~:text=simulation%20results%20that%20WDM,scalability%20induced%20by%20crosstalk%20noise)) Designers must balance the number of WDM channels and network size against these crosstalk constraints to **maximize bandwidth and energy efficiency** without compromising signal integrity. In practice, when carefully constrained, WDM is a powerful tool – delivering order-of-magnitude bandwidth gains and lower energy/bit – but its full potential on-chip is bounded by the need to keep channels from interfering with each other.
