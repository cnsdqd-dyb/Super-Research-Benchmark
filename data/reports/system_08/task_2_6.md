**Thermal Expansion Mismatch and Internal Stresses:** Metal matrix composites (MMCs) often pair a ductile metal with a stiff ceramic reinforcement, which typically have very different coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE). When the composite is heated or cooled, the matrix and reinforcement expand or contract by unequal amounts, causing them to constrain each other. This CTE mismatch generates significant internal stresses at the phase boundaries ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)). In other words, a **stress concentration field** develops around each inclusion or fiber, especially at the **interface**, because that’s where the differential expansion is physically accommodated. The larger the CTE difference and temperature change, the higher the induced thermal stress at the interface ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=As%20there%20is%20large%20difference,the%20properties%20of%20composite%20materials)). 

**Interface Cracking Due to CTE Mismatch:** If the thermal stress from expansion mismatch exceeds the local strength (either the bond strength at the interface or the yield strength of the adjacent matrix), damage will initiate at the interface. In fact, thermal cracks tend to **nucleate readily along the particle/matrix or fiber/matrix interfaces** as a direct result of CTE mismatch-induced stress ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)). The interface is a natural weak link under these differential strains – a large thermal stress can pry the reinforcement and matrix apart (debonding) or crack the surrounding matrix. Studies have shown that thermal mismatch stresses in MMCs can be high enough to **surpass the yield stress of the metal**, leading to interface debonding or nearby crack formation ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)). For example, composites with relatively weak interfaces often exhibit fiber/matrix debonding and interfacial cracking under thermal cycling, whereas those with stronger interfaces force the matrix to deform plastically instead ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0266353896001728#:~:text=analysis%20%28EPMA%29,decrease%20in%20most%20mechanical%20properties)). **Either way, the CTE mismatch is the driving force**: in both scenarios the primary cause of damage is the internal stress from differential expansion ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0266353896001728#:~:text=formation%20of%20interfacial%20reaction%20products,decrease%20in%20most%20mechanical%20properties)). The newly formed interface cracks represent areas of stress relief at the bond, but they in turn create **stress concentration** at their crack tips, which can facilitate further crack growth under continued cycling.

**Thermal Cycling Fatigue and Crack Evolution:** Under repeated thermal cycling, these mismatch-induced stresses act cyclically, and **damage accumulates at the interfaces** with each temperature swing ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=in%20CTEs,25)). Even if only micro-cracks or partial debonding form in early cycles, subsequent heating/cooling cycles will reopen and extend these cracks, gradually coalescing them into larger interface separations ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=The%20SiCp%2FAl%20composites%20employed%20for,important%20to%20get%20a%20better)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=in%20CTEs,25)). This **evolution of interface cracking** is directly driven by the CTE mismatch: each cycle’s differential expansion pulls or pushes on the interface crack, incrementally driving it deeper or along the interface. Consequently, the local stress field continuously readjusts – initially concentrated at intact interface regions, then shifting to the growing crack fronts. Over many cycles, a network of interface cracks can develop, significantly **weakening the composite**. In practice, the thermal expansion mismatch is one of the **primary drivers of interface fatigue damage** in MMCs, often dictating the composite’s lifetime under thermal cycling. Large CTE mismatches and wide temperature swings greatly accelerate interface crack growth and stress concentrations, making CTE mismatch-induced cracking the dominant failure mechanism under thermal fatigue ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0266353896001728#:~:text=formation%20of%20interfacial%20reaction%20products,decrease%20in%20most%20mechanical%20properties)). In short, the greater the CTE mismatch, the more severe and rapid the development of interface cracking and associated stress concentration fields will be during thermal cycling fatigue, degrading the composite’s mechanical performance over time.
