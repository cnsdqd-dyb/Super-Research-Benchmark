# Convex Responsibility Attribution and Moral Judgment Stability

## Responsibility Attribution Functions and Convexity  
**Responsibility attribution functions** map an agent’s contribution to an outcome into a measure of moral responsibility or blame. In an AI context, this function evaluates how much an AI’s decision is “responsible” for a given consequence. The **convexity** of such a function refers to its shape: a convex responsibility function increases at an accelerating rate. In practical terms, small contributions by the AI increase its attributed blame only slightly, while larger contributions cause blame to rise sharply. For example, if an AI’s involvement in a harmful outcome doubles, a convex function might more than double the blame assigned to it. This convex growth means extreme involvement is penalized much more heavily than partial involvement – **partial responsibility yields less blame than the sum of two smaller parts** (the hallmark of convexity). In contrast, a concave or linear function would assign more proportional blame even for minor contributions, lacking that accelerating effect. 

**Why does convexity matter?** A convex responsibility function is **single-peaked and monotonic**, which has important implications for moral evaluations ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00199-020-01246-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L345%20If%20W,if%20and%20only%20if)). It ensures that as an AI’s culpability grows, the moral judgment (e.g. whether the AI’s action is acceptable or blameworthy) changes in one direction without oscillation. There are no multiple local maxima of “blame” – instead, blame steadily increases and peaks when the AI is fully responsible. This smooth, one-directional increase creates a clear tipping point in how we judge the AI’s action.

## Stability Boundaries in Moral Decision Spaces  
An **AI decision space** is the range of possible actions or outcomes an AI system can produce, often visualized as a multi-dimensional space (with dimensions like harm caused, intentions, context, etc.). A **moral judgment stability boundary** is the threshold or dividing line in that space where moral evaluation changes category. In other words, on one side of this boundary the AI’s decisions are judged morally acceptable (or carry low blame), and on the other side they are judged morally unacceptable (or highly blameworthy). A judgment is **“stable”** within a region if small changes to the decision don’t flip its moral evaluation. The boundary marks the point at which the judgment *does* flip – essentially a moral tipping point.

Because a convex responsibility function accelerates blame assignment, it naturally **creates a pronounced threshold in the decision space**. For a range of decisions where the AI’s responsibility is low, the convex function keeps the blame low – the moral judgment remains stable and lenient across that region. As the AI’s responsibility increases toward a critical point, blame suddenly ramps up, reaching a level that triggers a different moral judgment. This critical point is the **stability boundary**: below it, the AI is given the benefit of the doubt (judged as not fully culpable), but once this point is crossed, the judgment shifts decisively to blame or moral condemnation. In effect, convexity produces a **clear “line in the sand”** in the AI’s decision space. Within each side of that line, moral assessments stay consistent (stable), but crossing the line causes a qualitative change in judgment.

This phenomenon is analogous to the **threshold effects** discussed in moral philosophy. For instance, *threshold deontology* holds that a moral rule (like “do not harm innocents”) is absolute until the harm of not violating the rule becomes catastrophically high; beyond that **threshold**, the rule is overridden ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103123000562#:~:text=number%20from%20the%20same%20kind,lies%20above%20a%20particular%20threshold)). Up to a point, adding more benefit for breaking the rule doesn’t change the judgment – the act is still condemned – but once the benefit crosses a high threshold, the judgment flips to permitting the act. Similarly, a convex responsibility function yields a **tipping point**: as long as the AI’s contribution to harm is below threshold, the moral judgment (“permissible” or “forgivable”) holds steady, but past that threshold of responsibility, the judgment swiftly changes to “impermissible” or “blameworthy.” In short, convexity ensures a **crisp boundary** between moral outcomes, preserving judgment stability on either side of that boundary.

## Convexity’s Role in Defining Stable Moral Boundaries  
The convex shape of the responsibility attribution function is what **determines where and how sharply** the moral judgment boundary appears. Because blame grows slowly at first and then rapidly, there is a **buffer zone** of stability: small errors or minor involvement by the AI don’t immediately trigger a harsh moral verdict. This makes moral judgment **robust to small perturbations** – the AI’s decisions remain on the same side of the moral dividing line as long as changes in responsibility are slight. The convex function essentially **widens the “gray area”** of partial responsibility where the moral judgment doesn’t yet tip over. The boundary itself occurs at the point of rapid blame escalation, meaning once the AI’s responsibility passes that convex threshold, the moral classification shifts definitively. 

Mathematically, convexity often leads to **unique extreme solutions**, which translates here into unequivocal moral categories. A formal result in game theory, for example, shows that if a welfare or payoff function is strictly convex, the stable equilibrium outcomes tend to be **corner solutions (extremes) rather than mixes** ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00199-020-01246-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L345%20If%20W,if%20and%20only%20if)). By analogy, a convex moral responsibility function drives the evaluation toward clear-cut judgments (extreme “blameless” vs “blameworthy” assignments) separated by a single boundary, rather than a muddle of partial, unstable judgments. The **decision boundary in an AI’s moral decision space will itself be convex (or roughly linear)**, meaning it’s a smooth, well-defined surface. This makes the boundary **stable**: within each partition of the space, the moral judgment doesn’t suddenly oscillate with minor changes. An AI’s decision can move around within the “morally acceptable” region without crossing into condemnation, until it nears the boundary; and once past the boundary, the decision clearly falls in the “morally unacceptable” region with little ambiguity.

In contrast, were the responsibility attribution function non-convex or concave, the moral boundary could be irregular or **less stable**. A concave function (diminishing rate of blame) might assign significant blame even for very small missteps and then plateau, making the **boundary fuzzy** – even slight involvement by the AI could immediately push it into morally questionable territory. There would be little stable safe zone, and the transition from moral to immoral could be **erratic or multi-staged** if the function had multiple inflection points. Convexity avoids these problems by **concentrating the “jump” in moral judgment at one clear threshold**. 

**In summary**, the convexity of responsibility attribution functions shapes a stable moral landscape for AI decisions. It ensures that moral judgments remain consistent within broad regions of the decision space and that there is a well-defined boundary (tipping point) where those judgments change. The accelerating nature of a convex blame function delays drastic moral condemnation until a critical point, yielding a **robust separation between acceptable and unacceptable decisions**. Thus, convexity effectively determines the **location and sharpness of the stability boundary** of moral judgments: it produces a strongly defined threshold beyond which an AI’s action transitions from being judged “okay” to “not okay,” thereby stabilizing moral evaluations on either side of that line. This clear boundary, born from convex attribution, makes moral judgments in AI decision-making **more predictable and resilient** to small variations in circumstances ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103123000562#:~:text=number%20from%20the%20same%20kind,lies%20above%20a%20particular%20threshold)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00199-020-01246-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L345%20If%20W,if%20and%20only%20if)). Each decision either comfortably lies in the morally safe zone or clearly crosses into the blameworthy zone, with convexity ensuring that this division is as stable and principled as possible.
