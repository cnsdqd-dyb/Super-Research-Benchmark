# Optimizing Efficiency-Linearity Trade-off in mm-Wave PA Design

## The Efficiency vs. Linearity Challenge at mm-Wave  
Millimeter-wave RF power amplifiers inherently face a trade-off between efficiency and linearity. Traditional “linear” amplifier classes (A/AB) provide good linearity but lower efficiency, while saturated or switching classes (e.g. Class E/F) achieve higher efficiency at the cost of significant non-linearity ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386119468_A_review_of_different_structures_of_power_amplifiers_to_improve_linearity_and_efficiency#:~:text=amplify%20a%20signal%20while%20maintaining,Usually%2C%20classic)). In high-frequency (mm-wave) transmitters, this compromise is critical: modern communication signals (like 5G mm-wave) have high peak-to-average power ratios and wide bandwidths, demanding both high power-added efficiency (PAE) and low distortion. A PA operating inefficiently wastes energy and may overheat, but a highly efficient PA driven into saturation can introduce distortion (AM/AM and AM/PM) that degrades signal quality. Thus, designers seek architectures that **maintain strong efficiency over a range of output power** (for varying signal envelopes) **while keeping the amplifier response linear** to meet spectral and modulation requirements ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386119468_A_review_of_different_structures_of_power_amplifiers_to_improve_linearity_and_efficiency#:~:text=need%20some%20methods%20to%20improve,results%20of%20efficiency%20and%20linearity)) ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386119468_A_review_of_different_structures_of_power_amplifiers_to_improve_linearity_and_efficiency#:~:text=amplify%20a%20signal%20while%20maintaining,Usually%2C%20classic)). The goal is to **optimize this balance**, especially in mm-wave front-ends where power budgets and linearity specs are both stringent. 

## Dynamic Load Modulation (Doherty Architecture)  
One of the most effective circuit approaches to optimize the efficiency-linearity trade-off at high frequencies is the use of **dynamic load modulation architectures**, with the **Doherty power amplifier (DPA)** being a prime example. The Doherty PA employs a main (carrier) amplifier and an auxiliary (peaking) amplifier that engage at different power levels to modulate the load impedance seen by the main device ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/8/1551?type=check_update&version=1#:~:text=The%20Doherty%20PA%20,diagram%20of%20a%20classic%20DPA)). This technique keeps the main amplifier in an efficient operating region even when output power is backed off, dramatically improving back-off efficiency compared to a traditional single-ended PA. In fact, Doherty operation is well-known to **enhance efficiency at power back-off** (lower output levels) without requiring overly complex extra networks ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/8/1551?type=check_update&version=1#:~:text=class%20G%20and%20Doherty%20improved,input%20and%20output%20passive%20networks)). By using a peaking amplifier that kicks in for high amplitudes, the composite amplifier can handle large peaks linearly while maintaining higher efficiency during lower-amplitude periods of the signal. This makes it ideal for modern modulated waveforms. 

At mm-wave frequencies, designers have adapted the Doherty concept and similar load-modulation schemes to realize these benefits. For example, combining multiple transistor cells in a **transformer-based power combiner** configured as a Doherty or parallel amplification network can yield **high back-off efficiency and good linearity even at 60 GHz** ([bohrium.dp.tech](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/604b418891e0117f82f3ddda#:~:text=https%3A%2F%2Fdoi.org%2F10.1109%2FISICIR.2016.7829705%202016,off%20efficiency%20and%20linearity)). In one 60 GHz PA design, several unit amplifiers operating in Class-AB (a compromise bias class for linearity) were combined via a transformer network – this achieved improved output power and **maintained efficiency in back-off along with linear operation** ([bohrium.dp.tech](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/604b418891e0117f82f3ddda#:~:text=transformer,off%20efficiency%20and%20linearity)). Generally, **hybrid PA architectures like Doherty (and outphasing/LINC)** have become popular in mm-wave and microwave PAs specifically to **meet linearity requirements without sacrificing efficiency** ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386119468_A_review_of_different_structures_of_power_amplifiers_to_improve_linearity_and_efficiency#:~:text=need%20some%20methods%20to%20improve,results%20of%20efficiency%20and%20linearity)). The Doherty approach stands out because it is relatively practical to implement and it directly targets the efficiency degradation at back-off, which is the crux of the trade-off for high-PAPR signals. By intelligently splitting the amplification between a main and peaking path, the PA can remain near its peak efficiency over a wider output power range, thereby **optimizing the overall efficiency for modulated signals while still amplifying linearly** (the main path stays mostly linear until the peaking path contributes at high power) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/8/1551?type=check_update&version=1#:~:text=class%20G%20and%20Doherty%20improved,input%20and%20output%20passive%20networks)).

## Biasing and Device Technology Considerations  
In implementing such efficiency-enhanced architectures at mm-wave, careful biasing and device selection also play a role. Often the main amplifier is biased in **Class AB** – this bias point provides a middle ground between Class A (most linear, least efficient) and Class B (more efficient, less linear), helping ensure a **baseline linearity with improved efficiency** ([bohrium.dp.tech](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/604b418891e0117f82f3ddda#:~:text=transformer,off%20efficiency%20and%20linearity)). The auxiliary amplifier can be biased in a lower conduction angle (e.g. Class C) since it only operates during signal peaks. Using compound semiconductor technologies (like GaAs or GaN HEMTs) at mm-wave can further improve efficiency due to their high $f_T$ and voltage-handling, though linearizing those can still require the Doherty or similar schemes. Device stacking and harmonic tuning (continuous modes) are additional circuit design techniques to stretch efficiency without breaking linearity, but these tend to be used in conjunction with the overarching Doherty or outphasing architecture.

Another approach related to bias is **envelope tracking (ET)** or dynamic supply biasing. In ET, the PA’s supply voltage is modulated in tandem with the signal envelope, so the transistor sees just enough voltage for the required output at each moment. This keeps the transistor out of deep saturation at low signal levels (improving linearity) and cuts power waste (improving efficiency). Envelope tracking has been employed at microwave frequencies and, in some research, even in mm-wave transmitters. It similarly **addresses the trade-off by boosting efficiency during low-power periods without letting the PA distort the signal**. However, implementing ultra-fast envelope modulators for mm-wave bandwidths is challenging. Thus, Doherty PAs (which are largely passive load modulation) have been more common at mm-wave, as they don’t require high-speed power supply modulation.

## Linearization Techniques to Complement PA Design  
While advanced PA architectures like the Doherty significantly improve the intrinsic efficiency-versus-linearity balance, **additional linearization techniques are usually needed** to meet strict communication standards. In practice, after designing a high-efficiency PA, engineers apply **digital predistortion (DPD)** or analog linearizers to **correct any residual nonlinearity** ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/8/1551?type=check_update&version=1#:~:text=another%20type%20of%20distortion%20that,In%20addition%2C%20spectral%20regrowth%20is)). Predistortion actively pre-shapes the input signal with the inverse of the PA’s distortion, so that the combined result at the output is a clean, linear amplification. This allows the PA to be driven closer to its saturation (efficient operating point) without violating linearity requirements on emissions and error vector magnitude. For example, researchers demonstrated a 15 GHz Doherty PA integrated with an **RF analog predistortion linearizer** to enhance linearity; this combined approach yielded improved efficiency from the Doherty architecture while still meeting linearity specs at high frequency ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/8/1551?type=check_update&version=1#:~:text=132,CrossRef)). In general, **modern mm-wave transmitters rely on predistortion** (digital or analog) as a **crucial complement** to circuit techniques – the PA can be optimized for efficiency and power, and the predistortion cancels out distortion, achieving an overall optimal trade-off.

Feedback or feed-forward linearization can also be used, though at mm-wave frequencies these are harder to implement due to stability and bandwidth limitations. Nevertheless, **the prevailing strategy** is: **design the PA with an efficiency-enhancing architecture (like Doherty or outphasing) and then apply digital predistortion** to iron out nonlinearities ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386119468_A_review_of_different_structures_of_power_amplifiers_to_improve_linearity_and_efficiency#:~:text=improve%20linearity%20requirements,14%5D%20use%20linearization)). This two-pronged approach is how designers **maximize power efficiency and maintain linearity simultaneously** for high-frequency signal processing. The result is a mm-wave front-end that can deliver the necessary output power and efficiency for, say, 64-QAM or OFDM signals, **without spectral regrowth or constellation distortion beyond allowable limits**.

## **Conclusion:** Best-Practice Approach  
In summary, **the best approach to optimize the efficiency–linearity trade-off in mm-wave RF front-ends is to use an efficiency-enhanced PA architecture (notably the Doherty amplifier or similar dynamic load modulation scheme) combined with appropriate linearization**. The Doherty PA has proven highly effective for high-frequency, high-PAPR signals – it keeps efficiency high at backed-off power levels **by dynamically modulating the amplifier’s load**, all while the main device operates relatively linearly over most of the range ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/8/1551?type=check_update&version=1#:~:text=class%20G%20and%20Doherty%20improved,input%20and%20output%20passive%20networks)) ([bohrium.dp.tech](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/604b418891e0117f82f3ddda#:~:text=transformer,off%20efficiency%20and%20linearity)). When augmented with techniques like digital predistortion to clean up residual distortion, this approach enables **high linearity** (meeting stringent modulation requirements) **and high average efficiency** for mm-wave signal processing. Essentially, the PA is designed to be **“efficiently linear”**: the hardware architecture takes care of efficiency during power back-off, and signal processing fixes any nonlinear blemishes. This combined strategy is widely adopted in modern mm-wave transceiver design, allowing next-generation wireless systems to achieve both the power efficiency and signal fidelity they require.
