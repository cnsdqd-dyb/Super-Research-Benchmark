# International Cultural Policy Frameworks  
International conventions establish fundamental guarantees that cultural heritage is respected alongside economic goals. Notably, UNESCO’s **Convention on the Diversity of Cultural Expressions (2005)** affirms that cultural goods have both economic and cultural value and “must therefore not be treated as solely having commercial value” ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A32006D0515#:~:text=BEING%20CONVINCED%20that%20cultural%20activities%2C,as%20solely%20having%20commercial%20value)). This treaty gives countries the right to adopt measures (e.g. local content quotas, subsidies, language requirements) that promote domestic cultural industries and diversity ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A32006D0515#:~:text=%28i%29%20%20,such%20activities%2C%20goods%20and%20services)). Likewise, UNESCO’s **Intangible Cultural Heritage Convention (2003)** calls on states to safeguard traditional knowledge and practices through identification, education, and community involvement, ensuring these living heritages endure even as they contribute to contemporary creative sectors. Such international frameworks explicitly acknowledge the *dual nature* of creative industries – as engines of economic growth and as carriers of cultural identity ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A52016IP0486#:~:text=C.%20%20,3%C2%A0000%20years%20of%20shared%20cultural)) – and legitimize policies balancing both aspects on a global scale.  

# National Laws and Cultural Regulations  
At the national level, governments provide institutional guarantees through cultural policies and legal protections. Many countries enforce **cultural content quotas** in media (for example, requiring a percentage of local music or films on air) and uphold the principle of a “**cultural exception**” in trade agreements, which allows treating cultural products differently from other goods ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A32006D0515#:~:text=BEING%20CONVINCED%20that%20cultural%20activities%2C,as%20solely%20having%20commercial%20value)). These regulations ensure that local heritage and expressions have space in markets dominated by global entertainment. **Heritage protection laws** also safeguard cultural sites, artifacts, and art forms – often requiring that any commercial use of these assets does not diminish their integrity. In addition, states establish bodies like **Ministries of Culture, arts councils, or heritage boards** to oversee and enforce these rules. Through such laws and agencies, countries can mediate the market’s drive for profit, making sure that creative industry practices (from content production to tourism) respect and preserve cultural traditions ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?from=SK&uri=CELEX%3A52014IR5515#:~:text=,for%20example%20the%20manufacture)). These institutional measures create a policy environment where economic development in creative sectors goes hand-in-hand with protecting cultural diversity and legacy.  

# Public Funding and Institutional Support  
Direct public investment is another guarantee to balance economics with heritage. Governments and international bodies channel funding into cultural programs, heritage conservation, and creative projects that might not survive on pure market logic. For example, the EU explicitly urges its members to **earmark adequate budgets for culture**, noting that public funding greatly “contributes significantly to cultural diversity” ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A52016IP0486#:~:text=considerably%20from%20public%20funding%20for,budgets%20for%20public%20funding%20for)). Many countries offer **grants, subsidies, and tax incentives** for films, music, crafts, and other arts that reflect national or local heritage. These funds support the **long-term value chain** – from training artists to producing and distributing cultural content – ensuring traditional art forms and narratives continue to thrive economically. Public cultural institutions such as museums, archives, and cultural centers also play a key role: they preserve heritage and provide platforms for artists, while often generating jobs (e.g. in tourism, education, and events). Notably, even competition policies allow exceptions for culture; in Europe, certain state aid rules are relaxed to permit subsidies for cultural industries and heritage preservation ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A52016IP0486#:~:text=Commission%20Regulation%20,and%2C%20in%20some)). By investing in heritage-based creativity, public institutions help sustain the economic viability of cultural expressions that might otherwise be undervalued in the market.  

# Community Involvement and Benefit‐Sharing  
Inclusive governance is an essential guarantee for aligning economic benefit with cultural heritage. International norms now emphasize that **heritage communities** – the people who create and uphold cultural traditions – must be central in any related industry. UNESCO’s heritage conventions require engaging these communities in decision-making and safeguarding efforts. This helps ensure that when a cultural practice becomes part of a value chain (such as handicrafts sold globally or performances for tourism), it is done with local consent and control. Studies show that integrating **intangible cultural heritage** with creative industries can succeed when local people are active participants and beneficiaries ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9898737/#:~:text=UNWTO%20Study%20on%20Tourism%20and,At%20the)). Concretely, this is achieved through mechanisms like cooperatives of artisans, community-managed cultural tourism, and consultation processes for using indigenous culture in media. **Intellectual property rights and benefit-sharing frameworks** further guarantee long-term balance: creators are legally empowered to profit from their work, which incentivizes preservation of cultural content. UNESCO has noted the “importance of intellectual property rights in sustaining those involved in cultural creativity” ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A32006D0515#:~:text=EMPHASIZING%20the%20vital%20role%20of,progress%20of%20society%20at%20large)). Many countries protect traditional cultural expressions via trademarks or geographical indications (for example, certifications for authentic crafts or regional specialties), so that communities gain economic value from their heritage. By securing fair compensation and recognition for cultural creators at the grassroots, these measures align economic incentives with the continued practice and transmission of heritage.  

**In summary**, a web of institutional guarantees – from global conventions and national cultural policies to public funding and community rights – underpins sustainable creative industry value chains. These guarantees ensure that economic development does not come at the expense of cultural heritage, but rather that both reinforce each other. Creative industries are thus encouraged to draw on the richness of cultural traditions while providing livelihoods, with laws and institutions in place to maintain an appropriate balance between market success and the stewardship of cultural identity ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?from=SK&uri=CELEX%3A52014IR5515#:~:text=,in%20complex%20ways%20to%20support)) ([eur-lex.europa.eu](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A52016IP0486#:~:text=C.%20%20,3%C2%A0000%20years%20of%20shared%20cultural)).
