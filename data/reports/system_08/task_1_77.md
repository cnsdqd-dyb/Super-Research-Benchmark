# Thermodynamic Limits on Circular Economy Efficiency

**Yes.** The principle of Gibbs free energy minimization (a manifestation of the second law of thermodynamics) imposes fundamental limits on how efficient and “circular” an economy can be. In simple terms, spontaneous processes tend to **lower the Gibbs free energy** of a system, which usually means increasing entropy (disorder) in the process ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_process#:~:text=In%20thermodynamics%20%2C%20a%20spontaneous,energy%20of%20the%20%205)). This has direct consequences for recycling and reuse in a circular economy, because **reversing entropy** (turning dispersed waste back into organized resources) always requires an input of high-quality energy. Below we break down why entropy and free-energy considerations constrain the scalability and efficiency of circular systems:

## Gibbs Free Energy, Entropy, and Spontaneity 
- **Spontaneous processes move toward lower free energy:** In thermodynamics, a process will occur spontaneously if it leads to a decrease in free energy (ΔG < 0) under the given conditions. For example, mixing substances or allowing a chemical reaction to go to completion typically releases free energy and increases overall entropy ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_process#:~:text=In%20thermodynamics%20%2C%20a%20spontaneous,energy%20of%20the%20%205)). A classic observation is that without external input, systems naturally evolve toward **thermodynamic equilibrium**, which often means **maximum entropy** for an isolated system.  
- **Non-spontaneous (endergonic) processes need energy input:** If a change would *raise* the free energy (ΔG > 0), it won’t happen on its own. Instead, the reverse process (which lowers free energy) is what occurs spontaneously ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_process#:~:text=,net%20change%20taking%20place%20over)). To drive a non-spontaneous change – for instance, turning random scrap material back into pure, ordered form – we must supply external work or energy ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endergonic_reaction#:~:text=In%20chemical%20thermodynamics%20%2C%20an,for%20the%20reaction%20to%20happen)). In other words, **you can’t get a decrease in entropy or increase in free energy without paying an energy cost**. This is why recycling, remanufacturing, or restoring materials to a high-quality state always consumes energy. 

## Entropy Generation and Irreversibility in Recycling 
- **Energy degradation is unavoidable:** The second law of thermodynamics dictates that whenever we use energy to do work (like separating waste or purifying materials), some of that energy **irreversibly dissipates as low-grade heat** ([www.resilience.org](https://www.resilience.org/stories/2018-04-18/the-2nd-law-of-thermodynamics-the-gaping-hole-in-the-middle-of-the-circular-economy/#:~:text=In%20this%20case%2C%20the%20use,Sorted)). You cannot fully recover or reuse this dissipated energy – it’s the “thermodynamic price” paid to decrease entropy locally. For example, you might burn fuel or use electricity to recycle metals or plastics; in doing so, you inevitably produce waste heat that flows into the environment and increases entropy there. This loss of useful energy (often called **exergy destruction**) is *unavoidable* in any real process ([www.resilience.org](https://www.resilience.org/stories/2018-04-18/the-2nd-law-of-thermodynamics-the-gaping-hole-in-the-middle-of-the-circular-economy/#:~:text=In%20this%20case%2C%20the%20use,Sorted)).  
- **No perfectly reversible cycles in practice:** A truly circular process with *no* entropy increase (analogous to a reversible Carnot cycle) is an idealization. In reality, all industrial and economic processes are **irreversible** – they involve friction, mixing, heat losses, and chemical irreversibilities that generate entropy ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=consider%3A%20On%20the%20one%20hand,all%20the%20dissipated%20energy%20and)) ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=absolutely%20no%20energy%20being%20dissipated,Then%20the%20total%20entropy)). As one analyst put it, *“in practice energy is always dissipated and it is simply not possible to reuse all the dissipated energy; thus energy is constantly needed as input”* to keep a circular system running ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=absolutely%20no%20energy%20being%20dissipated,Then%20the%20total%20entropy)). Even **nature’s cycles** aren’t exempt – ecological “circular economies” (like nutrient cycles) are powered by a constant influx of low-entropy energy from the sun, and they dump waste heat to the environment ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=dropping%20all%20its%20leaves%2C%20which,In%20its%20circle%20of)) ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=match%20at%20L167%20reservoir%20of,dissipate%20enormous%20amounts%20of%20energy)). In short, maintaining order in one part of a system demands entropy be exported somewhere else.

## Diminishing Returns in Material Recovery  
- **Rising entropy means harder recovery:** As materials are used and dispersed (whether diluted in mixtures, corroded into compounds, or scattered in products and landfills), their entropy increases. **Reversing that dispersion** – e.g. extracting a metal from dilute e-waste or capturing CO₂ from air to make fuel – becomes progressively more energy-intensive the more diluted or disordered the materials are. The effort (energy, resources) needed to recover materials **grows nonlinearly as you try to reclaim a higher percentage** of them ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=In%20this%20context%20it%20is,providing%20independent%20science%20advice%2C%20to)). Initially, recycling the easy, concentrated fraction saves energy compared to using virgin resources. But as you push for near-100% recovery, you end up chasing very dispersed remnants of material, which costs *disproportionately* more energy to collect and reprocess ([www.resilience.org](https://www.resilience.org/stories/2018-04-18/the-2nd-law-of-thermodynamics-the-gaping-hole-in-the-middle-of-the-circular-economy/#:~:text=By%20the%20end%20of%20one,of%20our%20plastic%20left)) ([www.resilience.org](https://www.resilience.org/stories/2018-04-18/the-2nd-law-of-thermodynamics-the-gaping-hole-in-the-middle-of-the-circular-economy/#:~:text=)). There comes a point where **the energy required to recover the next small fraction exceeds the energy saved** by recycling – a direct consequence of entropy.  
- **100% recycling is thermodynamically impossible:** Because of these diminishing returns, there is an entropy-driven limit to circularity. The European Academies Science Advisory Council noted that *“Recovery can never be 100%… Recovery and recycling of dispersed materials require energy and resources, which increase in a nonlinear manner as the percentage of recycled material rises (owing to the second law of thermodynamics: entropy causing dispersion)”* ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=In%20this%20context%20it%20is,providing%20independent%20science%20advice%2C%20to)). In other words, **some portion of materials will always be lost or too costly to fully recover** because trying to eliminate that last bit of disorder would require infinite energy. This doesn’t mean we shouldn’t recycle; rather, it means there’s a practical optimum recycling rate for each material before entropy (and costs) make it infeasible to go further ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=entropy%20needs%20to%20be%20considered%2C,providing%20independent%20science%20advice%2C%20to)).

## Impact on the Scalability of a Circular Economy  
- **Continuous energy input is required:** A circular economy doesn’t violate thermodynamic laws, but it **relies on external energy to sustain the cycle**. You can keep materials in use longer and loop them through reuse/remanufacturing, but at each loop you must input new energy to restore material quality and drive the process. This is why even a circular industrial system must be coupled with a source of renewable or external energy – effectively using low-entropy energy to pump out the entropy that accumulates in each cycle. If that energy comes from fossil fuels or non-renewable sources, the process isn’t truly sustainable (it’s effectively converting concentrated fuel into waste heat while recycling materials).  
- **Efficiency is bounded by entropy:** No matter how well-designed, a large-scale circular economy will always have **less than 100% efficiency** in terms of material and energy recovery. **Gibbs free energy minimization sets the direction** of natural processes toward disorder, so we’re constantly working *uphill* against that trend to collect and reuse resources. There is a hard limit to how much energy can be conserved or how perfectly materials can be cycled because some entropy must be produced and released in each step ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=absolutely%20no%20energy%20being%20dissipated,Then%20the%20total%20entropy)) ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=entropy%20needs%20to%20be%20considered%2C,providing%20independent%20science%20advice%2C%20to)). In practical terms, this means we should not imagine that all waste can be recycled indefinitely with no loss; rather, we must design systems to minimize entropy production where possible, use energy efficiently, and accept that some fraction of materials/energy will always exit the cycle as degraded waste.

## **Conclusion:** Entropy Constraints Require Realistic Expectations 
Gibbs free energy minimization (and the associated increase of entropy in spontaneous processes) indeed **constrains the scalable efficiency** of circular economies. We can get **closer** to circularity with smart engineering, renewable energy, and better product design (e.g. designing for easy reuse/recycling), but we cannot **eliminate** the thermodynamic costs. Every loop of material reuse comes with an entropy toll – some energy dissipated, some material quality lost – that must be paid to keep the cycle going. A truly closed-loop, 100% efficient circular economy would require reversible processes and no entropy gain, which is unattainable in practice. In summary, thermodynamics doesn’t make circular economies futile, but it does mean **there are limits to circular efficiency**: achieving sustainability will also require input of clean energy and sensible limits on consumption so that the unavoidable entropy produced remains within ecological bounds ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=entropy%20needs%20to%20be%20considered%2C,providing%20independent%20science%20advice%2C%20to)). The second law thus serves as a reminder that “circular” must be pursued *with* physics in mind – we can recycle and reuse much more, but we can’t get something for nothing when it comes to energy and entropy.
