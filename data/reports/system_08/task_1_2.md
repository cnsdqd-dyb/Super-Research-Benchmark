## Local Chemical Order and Dislocation–Twin Boundary Interactions in HEAs

In high-entropy alloys (HEAs), local chemical ordering (short-range order) creates spatial fluctuations in composition and bonding, which **directly affect how dislocations interact with twin boundaries during plastic deformation**. Unlike a uniform solid solution, an HEA’s disordered lattice exhibits patches of ordering or clustering that **“roughen” the energy landscape** for defect motion ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=In%20what%20follows%20we%20systematically,The%20multitude%20of)). This has several consequences for dislocation–twin interactions:

- **Variable Twin Boundary (Stacking Fault) Energy:** Local compositional fluctuations cause the energy of stacking faults and twin boundaries to vary from place to place ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=In%20what%20follows%20we%20systematically,The%20multitude%20of)). In practical terms, the cost to form or move a twin boundary is not constant. As a partial dislocation creates a twin (essentially a coherent stacking fault), the energy required can be higher or lower depending on the local atomic arrangement. This **heterogeneous twin boundary energy** means some regions favor twin formation while others resist it.

- **Wave-Shaped Dislocation Lines:** Because of the above energy variability, dislocations do not move uniformly. **Dislocation lines become wavy or roughened** as different segments encounter different local ordering environments ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927025622003652#:~:text=)). For example, molecular dynamics simulations of a CoCrFeMnNi HEA show that a screw dislocation approaching a twin boundary cannot constrict and pass through in unison as it would in a pure metal. Instead, the dislocation’s line undulates and crosses the twin boundary in a segment-by-segment (asynchronous) manner due to random local energy fluctuations ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927025622003652#:~:text=)). In contrast, a dislocation in a chemically homogeneous metal (like pure Ni) would remain straight and synchronize its crossing of the twin boundary.

- **Increased Resistance to Twin Growth:** Local chemical order also **penalizes the thickening of twins**. As each new atomic layer is added to a deformation twin (twin boundary migrates), it disrupts the pre-existing local order on that slip plane. This imposes an extra energy cost beyond the usual stacking-fault energy ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=match%20at%20L297%20unconventional%20twin,Similar%20trend)). In effect, the twin boundary energy in an HEA is “unconventional” – it **rises as the twin grows**, because each successive layer must break underlying chemical order ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=match%20at%20L297%20unconventional%20twin,Similar%20trend)). Consequently, once a thin twin forms, it is harder for dislocations to further extend (thicken) that twin. Twin growth is no longer as easy as in a random solid solution; it requires overcoming the additional energy barriers associated with destroying local order layer by layer ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=unconventional%20twin%20boundary%20energies%20entail,Similar%20trend)). This often limits twins to nano-scale thickness and diverts dislocation activity back to slip or the formation of new twins elsewhere.

- **Segmented, Stop-and-Go Motion:** Overall, the **dislocation motion in an HEA becomes a stop-and-go process**. Dislocation segments may get temporarily **trapped by locally ordered regions** (which act like nanoscale obstacles) and then release (detrap) when sufficient stress is applied ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=In%20what%20follows%20we%20systematically,The%20multitude%20of)). This intermittent progress contrasts with the smooth glide in a uniform material. The presence of multiple principal elements and chemical short-range order means dislocations must **navigate a complex landscape**, affecting where and how they can cut through or bulge past a twin boundary.

**In summary, local chemical ordering in HEAs makes the interaction between dislocations and twin boundaries highly non-uniform.** It introduces a rugged energy landscape with **local variations in twin boundary (stacking fault) energy**, causing dislocation lines to **bow and advance unevenly** when encountering or creating twin boundaries ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927025622003652#:~:text=)). Each layer of a twin formed by dislocation slip incurs an extra energy penalty from breaking local order, **hindering twin thickening** and altering the usual twinning mechanism ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=unconventional%20twin%20boundary%20energies%20entail,Similar%20trend)). This means that during plastic deformation, dislocations in HEAs often interact with twin boundaries in a more complex, piecewise fashion – they may transmit through or nucleate twins in some regions easily while being impeded in others – unlike the relatively straightforward dislocation–twin interactions seen in chemically simpler alloys. The net effect is enhanced strength (due to these obstacles) but also sustained ductility, as the material can activate multiple deformation modes (slip and twinning) in different microscale regions ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=In%20what%20follows%20we%20systematically,The%20multitude%20of)). This unique interplay between local chemical order, dislocation motion, and twin boundary energetics is a defining factor in the mechanical response of high-entropy alloys under stress.
