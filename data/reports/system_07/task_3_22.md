The Riemannian metric of universality measures acts as a *gauge* for how far a local justice tradition can be stretched before it loses its recognisable shape.  
Concretely:

1.  **Universality as a curvature bound**  
    Think of the space of all conceivable “justice procedures” (hearings, proportionality tests, reparative rituals, etc.) as an infinite-dimensional manifold.  
    A universality measure is a Riemannian metric g on that manifold.  Its sectional curvature K plays the role of a tolerance threshold:  if K is very negative almost every geodesic diverges, so two neighbouring traditions move apart exponentially fast; if K is non-negative, geodesics converge and traditions can be deformed into one another without tearing the fabric of legitimacy.  
    Empirically, negative curvature corresponds to legal cultures that treat any foreign element as a categorical distortion; non-negative curvature is found in systems that explicitly allow “proportionality balancing” or “living-tree” interpretation.

2.  **Conformal transformation = local rescaling of normative weight**  
    A conformal transformation multiplies the metric by a positive scalar Ω(x)² that varies from one locality x to another.  
    In justice terms this is exactly what proportionality doctrine does:  it keeps the *angles* (logical relations between principles) fixed while rescaling the *lengths* (stringency of the rule).  The old rule “the state may never restrict religious dress” is replaced by the conformally equivalent “the state may restrict religious dress only to the extent that the restriction is necessary and proportionate”.  The topology of rights is unchanged, but their *normative density* is locally rescaled.

3.  **The Yamabe-type constraint**  
    Not every Ω(x) is admissible.  The scalar curvature R[g] has to satisfy the Yamabe equation  
        –4(n–1)/(n–2) ΔΩ + R Ω = R̃ Ω^(n+2)/(n–2)  
    where R̃ is the target curvature after rescaling.  
    Translated:  the *global* legitimacy budget (R̃) must equal the *local* cultural work (ΔΩ) plus the *intrinsic* resistance of the tradition (R).  If the local tradition is highly curved (strongly particularistic), the Laplacian ΔΩ must be large—i.e. the community has to invest a lot of interpretive energy—to keep the transformed tradition within the universal bound.  When that energy is missing, the conformal factor collapses (Ω → 0) and the tradition is abandoned or declared unconstitutional.

4.  **Geodesic completeness as “access-to-justice”**  
    A conformally transformed metric is geodesically complete only if Ω(x) does not go to zero faster than 1/distance.  In practical terms:  if the proportionality test thins the norm so much that citizens can no longer *reach* a remedy (the geodesic hits a boundary in finite time), the transformation is rejected as violating the right to a fair trial.  Courts therefore monitor the asymptotics of Ω(x)—they ask whether the “margin of appreciation” shrinks faster than the distance to the European Court in Strasbourg.

5.  **Spectral universality**  
    The eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator built from g form a “universality spectrum”.  Under conformal transformation the spectrum changes, but its *short-distance* behaviour (the Weyl asymptotics) is invariant.  Legal scholars exploit this:  they compare the *high-frequency* part of two justice systems—how they handle mass of small claims, traffic fines, administrative penalties—to decide whether the conformal warp is merely cosmetic or a deep rupture.  If the Weyl coefficients coincide, the foreign procedure is admitted as an equivalent metric even though the low-frequency landmarks (constitutional identity) look different.

Hence the Riemannian metric of universality does not *prevent* conformal transformation of local justice traditions; it *quantifies* the price—measured in curvature, scalar curvature, and spectral shift—that the tradition must pay to stay within the manifold of legitimate law.
