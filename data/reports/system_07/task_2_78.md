Virtual reality (VR) narrative reconstructs the perceptual boundaries of traditional space-time by dissolving the fixed coordinates of physical reality and replacing them with fluid, user-generated experiential frameworks. This reconstruction operates across four interlocking dimensions:

1. **Spatial Autonomy vs. Directed Attention**  
Traditional cinema and theatre imprison the spectator in a single, pre-selected point of view; the frame itself is a temporal contract that dictates when and where the eye may travel. VR cancels the frame. The 360° panoramic shell places the observer inside the image, turning the gaze into a roving camera whose every saccade re-writes the montage in real time. Yet this freedom is not absolute: creators re-assert narrative control through “spatial guidance systems” (light cones, 3-D audio beacons, character motion vectors) that covertly re-inscribe a diegetic directionality inside the apparently borderless world. Space becomes a palimpsest—simultaneously open and stealthily authored.

2. **Layered or “Stacked” Space-Time**  
Metaverse-style narratives often nest multiple ontological strata—what we might call “doll-house universes”—inside one another. A user can step through a door in what is perceived as “present” space and emerge in a memory, a simulation, or a counter-factual future without a cut or fade. Because the transition is enacted by the user’s own proprioceptive motion (walking, reaching, turning), the discontinuity is felt as continuity: the body’s kinesthetic clock keeps ticking while the narrative clock jumps. The result is a non-Euclidean time-sheet in which causal order is no longer an external editing logic but an internal, exploratory topology.

3. **Proprioceptive Re-wiring and the “Phantom Body”**  
In classical media, the body of the spectator is a forgotten constant; screen time is measured in minutes, not in heartbeats. VR headsets remap the sensorimotor loop: head-tracking couples angular velocity to visual flow, haptics give virtual objects a pseudo-mass, and redirected walking techniques can make a 5 m² living room feel like an infinite desert. Consequently, the user’s vestibular system becomes a co-author of space-time metrics. A narrative beat can be stretched simply by slowing the ratio of physical-to-virtual motion, or compressed by amplifying it. Story duration is no longer absolute (22 min episode, 120 min film) but relativistic: elapsed narrative time = k × bodily displacement, where k is tunable per scene.

4. **Chronotopic Fragments and Attentional Montage**  
Because the VR world is boundless yet cognitive resources remain finite, memory landmarks must be injected as “chronotopic fragments”: distinctive spatial objects (a glowing piano, a cracked mirror) that act as spatio-temporal anchors. Instead of Eisenstein’s intellectual montage of colliding images, VR offers a montage of attentional shifts inside a single continuous space. The user’s gaze-string—what they look at, in what order—becomes the edit list. Thus the traditional cut is replaced by a saccade; the fade-to-black by a slow dimming of voxelated light that may or may not be obeyed if the user simply turns away. Narrative coherence is no longer guaranteed by the artefact but by the psychophysical trail the user burns through it.

In short, VR narrative liquefies the once-rigid axes of “here / there” and “now / then,” re-casting them as parameters that can be bent by designer code and user physiology in tandem. Space becomes a performable object; time becomes an elastic function of motion; and the story event is no longer a cell on a storyboard but a node in a cloud of possible trajectories that collapses into experience only at the moment the user’s body passes through it.
