1. Ontological excess  
   The Everettian wave-function never selects one branch; every macro-outcome is realized.  Determinism is therefore restored at the level of the universal state, but at the price of exploding the ontology.  A knower who wants to *know* the future must, in principle, specify not only the initial conditions but also which branch he will occupy.  That indexical fact is not contained in the global state; hence even perfect Laplacean information leaves the future under-determined *for him*.

2. Epistemic inaccessibility by decoherence  
   From the “frog” perspective (Tegmark) the observer is decohered from the other branches.  The off-diagonal terms that would be needed to verify the existence (let alone the content) of those worlds are exponentially suppressed.  Thus the additional ontological structure is permanently sealed from any empirical check.  Deterministic knowledge traditionally requires that every element that causally contributes to a future event be, in principle, observable.  The many-worlds scheme violates that requirement: the causal chain includes events in inaccessible sectors.

3. Loss of a unique past  
   Because the branching is retroactive, the observer’s own memory is compatible with many distinct macro-histories that have diverged in the unobserved past.  The determinist program assumes a unique, globally consistent past that can be reconstructed from present data.  If the universal state contains a superposition of incompatible pasts, all of which give rise to the same memory, the very notion of a determinate “fact” that can be known breaks down.

4. Indexical uncertainty even with full physical information  
   Even when the observer possesses the entire universal wave-function (the “bird” view), he still faces *self-locating* uncertainty: he does not know *which* copy in the branching tree he *is*.  This uncertainty is not due to missing physical data; it is irreducible.  Deterministic knowledge, however, is supposed to eliminate all uncertainty once the physical facts are fixed.  The residual indexical uncertainty is therefore a direct counter-example to the epistemic completeness claim of determinism.

Taken together, these points shift the epistemic target: instead of a single true, maximally informative description, the best an embedded agent can achieve is a *branch-indexed* probability measure that is forever incomplete from his own standpoint.  Deterministic knowledge is not logically impossible—it still exists in the bird’s-eye mathematical formalism—but it becomes cognitively empty because the knower can never bring the global, branch-relative truth into a single accessible perspective.  In that sense the cognitive inaccessibility of parallel worlds does not merely complicate deterministic knowledge; it exposes it as an epistemic ideal that is in principle unachievable for any physically situated observer.
