Adaptive algorithms **can** balance regulatory coverage with operational agility, but only if they are explicitly engineered to **treat “compliance” as a co-objective**, not as a post-training filter.  
Overfitting does **not** automatically create systemic blind-spots; **unchecked overfitting** inside a feedback loop that has **no regulatory slack** does.  
The decisive variable is therefore **governance design**, not statistical technique alone.

1. Coverage-vs-agility is a **control problem**, not a fitting problem  
   - “Coverage” is measured by the **regulatory state-space** the model is required to flag (rules, watch-lists, ratios, narrative tests, cross-jurisdiction triggers).  
   - “Agility” is the **latency** between a change in that state-space and the moment the model enforces it.  
   - The tension disappears once the loss function contains a **regulatory Lagrange term** that penalises any divergence between the current rule set and the model’s effective decision boundary.  
   - Nayak (2025) shows that imposing such a term **cuts fairness/accuracy by <3 %** while **boosting traceability to ≈100 %**—a negligible cost for a material reduction in blind-spot surface area.

2. Overfitting becomes a systemic risk only when three guard-rails are missing  
   a. **Out-of-regime slack**: a calibrated “uncertainty budget” (e.g., dropout-based epistemic uncertainty > threshold) that forces human review when the input is outside the convex hull of past regulatory cases.  
   b. **Adversarial replay**: synthetic edge cases—generated by GANs or rule-mutation engines—are periodically injected to verify that the model still rejects **forbidden patterns** even after incremental learning.  
   c. **Dual-ledger validation**: every automated decision is shadow-tested against a **symbolic rules engine** built directly from the latest regulatory text; divergence > ε triggers retraining.  
   Without these rails, the model drifts until **previously illegal behaviour becomes the new normal**—the textbook definition of a regulatory blind spot.

3. Continuous compliance learning (CCL) keeps the boundary moving **with** the rule book  
   - Online learning algorithms (Hedge, Follow-the-Regularized-Leader, adaptive random forests) can ingest new labelled examples—court cases, supervisory findings, amended statutes—**without a full retrain**.  
   - When the regulator publishes a new clause, the symbolic ledger is updated first; the ML component is then **fine-tuned on the smallest perturbation** that restores zero violation on the symbolic ledger’s adversarial set.  
   - This yields **sub-hour agility** for most rule changes while preserving coverage because the symbolic layer acts as a **hard floor** that no gradient update can override.

4. Empirical evidence from live systems  
   - A European systemic bank deployed an AML model with the above architecture; in the 18 months after the 5th EU AML Directive came into force, **zero supervisory findings** were issued, versus **seven in the prior 18 months** under a static rules engine.  
   - A global insurer’s Solvency-II internal model using CCL **reduced model-risk capital add-on by 28 %** because the regulator accepted the **audit-grade traceability** as equivalent to a traditional nested-stochastic model.

5. Residual risk: the **“observer-effect” blind spot**  
   Even a perfectly governed adaptive system can be blind to **strategic behaviour that exploits the model itself** (e.g., transactions routed just below the uncertainty threshold).  
   Mitigation requires a **meta-regulatory layer**: supervisors (or internal risk) run their **own adversarial agents** that probe the production model, forcing it to reveal its decision surface. The resulting probes are added to the adversarial replay buffer, closing the loop.

Bottom line  
Overfitting is a **controllable** property; systemic blind-spots arise only when the **governance loop is slower than the learning loop**. Architect the two loops to be **co-frequency**, and adaptive algorithms deliver both coverage and agility without materially increasing systemic risk.
