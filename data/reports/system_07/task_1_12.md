There is no single “magic” number, but the last five years of chronic animal data and early-human experience point to the same regime:

- **≈ 60–100 electrodes cm²** (electrode pitch 300–400 µm)  
  – gives a spatial cut-off of ≈600 µm (Shannon-Nyquist) which is sufficient to reconstruct the mes-scale LFP/ECoG patterns that carry most motor-speech-decoding information.  
  – produces only a thin (≤30 µm) transparent polymer sheet that slips into the sub-dural space through a 1-mm burr hole; 42-day canine and 203-day canine implants show no cortical redness, no adhesion, and atraumatic explant.  

- **Going beyond ≈150 electrodes cm²** (<250 µm pitch) improves decoding only marginally (R² gain <3% in NHPs) while doubling the via-hole count and increasing the risk of pial laceration during insertion.  

- **Going below ≈30 electrodes cm²** (>600 µm pitch) begins to lose the high-γ spatial structure that carries rapid motor information; decoding accuracy falls ≈10% in human OR studies.  

Therefore, for a long-term neural prosthesis the **optimal “balanced” density is 60–100 electrodes cm²** when the array is:  
- ultrathin (≤30 µm),  
- mesh or serpentine interconnects to keep shear stiffness <5 kPa,  
- opened in the sub-dural (not penetrating) space.  

This density matches the best chronic data (dog 203 d, NHP 1 yr, human 42 d) and keeps surgical invasiveness low while still giving the spatial sampling needed for robust, high-bit-rate BCIs.
