Antipsychotic therapy walks a tight-rope between two anatomically-close but functionally-different dopamine circuits.

1. Where the drug has to work – mesolimbic pathway  
   • Hyper-active D₂ transmission in the nucleus accumbens generates the positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations).  
   • A 60-80% D₂ occupancy here is the “therapeutic window”; below ≈60% antipsychotic effect is lost.

2. Where the trouble starts – nigro-striatal pathway  
   • The same D₂ blockade in the caudate/putamen removes the normal “brake” on cholinergic interneurones → acute EPS (dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism) and, with chronic use, tardive dyskinesia.  
   • Here the same 60-80% occupancy is already above the EPS-threshold (≈50%).

Second-generation (“atypical”) drugs keep the antipsychotic effect while sparing the nigro-striatum by three complementary pharmacological tricks:

A. Serotonin-5-HT₂A antagonism (and partial 5-HT₁A agonism)  
   – 5-HT₂A blockade on glutamatergic terminals in striatum disinhibits nigral dopamine release → a little extra dopamine is still available to compete for the D₂ receptor, so occupancy can be pushed to the therapeutic 70% in limbic areas while the net “functional” blockade in striatum stays below the EPS threshold.  
   – Drugs with a 5-HT₂A/D₂ binding ratio ≥ 12 (risperidone, olanzapine, ziprasidone, etc.) show markedly lower EPS.

B. Fast (“hit-and-run”) D₂ kinetics  
   – Aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, quetiapine, clozapine dissociate from the receptor within minutes, allowing physiological dopamine surges to “get through” during phasic firing, whereas haloperidol or fluphenazine remain bound for hours.  
   – Result: antipsychotic efficacy with mean striatal occupancies that stay under ≈50% for most of the dosing interval.

C. Regional-receptor profile differences  
   – Clozapine, quetiapine and olanzapine are also potent muscarinic-M₁, histaminic-H₁ and α₁ antagonists; the central anticholinergic action directly counterbalances the relative dopaminergic deficit in the striatum.

Clinical translation

1. Choose the molecule with the highest 5-HT₂A/D₂ affinity ratio and loose D₂ kinetics that the patient can tolerate (e.g. aripiprazole for a young man, quetiapine or olanzapine for an elderly person, clozapine for treatment-resistance).  
2. Use the lowest effective dose: for risperidone 2-4 mg, olanzapine 10-15 mg, aripiprazole 10-15 mg gives limbic D₂ occupancy 65-75% but keeps peak striatal occupancy < 75%.  
3. Titration schedule matters – gradual up-titration allows compensatory mechanisms (5-HT-mediated dopamine release, receptor internalisation) to develop.  
4. If EPS still emerges:  
   – Reduce dose or split dose to lower peak occupancy.  
   – Switch to a molecule with looser D₂ binding (quetiapine, clozapine).  
   – Add an anticholinergic (benztropine, trihexyphenidyl) short-term; avoid long-term prophylaxis because of cognition/delirium risk.  
   – For tardive dyskinesia use a VMAT-2 inhibitor (deutetrabenazine, valbenazine) rather than anticholinergics.  
5. Monitor objectively (AIMS, SAS, BARS) every 3–6 months; early detection permits dose adjustment before irreversible changes develop.

Thus, by exploiting serotonergic modulation, rapid-receptor kinetics and careful dose-occupancy modelling, modern antipsychotic regimens can keep the mesolimbic D₂ blockade needed for positive-symptom control while letting just enough dopamine signalling survive in the nigro-striatum to keep the patient moving normally.
