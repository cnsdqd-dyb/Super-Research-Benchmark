The “reasonable” cancellation grace period is not a single number that fits every transaction; it is a sliding scale that the law expects to be fixed by looking at three sets of variables:

1. What the customer is entitled to by statute or regulation  
2. How much preparation the seller has to do before the order becomes irreversible  
3. How much value is lost if the customer walks away after that moment  

Because these variables are different in every sector, the test is always “proportionate to the foreseeable prejudice of the other party.”  Courts and regulators translate that test into the following practical checklist:

A. Start with the statutory floor  
   - Distance-selling / e-commerce: 14 calendar days from the day the customer receives the goods (EU Consumer Rights Dir., UK CCR 2013, etc.).  
   - Pure services: 14 days from the day the contract is concluded, but only if performance has not begun with the customer’s prior express consent.  
   - Door-to-door / home-solicitation: 30 days is presumed reasonable in many U.S. states and in the Uniform Consumer Sales Acts.  
   - FTC “30-day Rule” for mail/Internet orders: if no shipment date is promised, the merchant has 30 days to ship; after that the customer may cancel for a full refund.  

B. Shorten the period if the seller’s cost rises steeply with time  
   - Perishable or tailor-made goods, event tickets, travel, restaurant tables: the period is compressed to the point at which the seller can no longer mitigate (often 24 h, 48 h, 72 h or 7 days).  
   - Digital content that is instantly downloadable: the right can be lost as soon as download begins, provided the customer gave explicit prior consent and acknowledged loss of the right.  

C. Lengthen the period when the customer needs time to verify the goods  
   - Insurance policies, lease agreements, distance mortgages: 14–30 days is common, and some jurisdictions give 60 days.  

D. Expressly state the cut-off in the offer  
   - The contract may fix any period that is not “unfair” under consumer-law standards (i.e. not so short that it deprives the consumer of an effective right, not so long that it exposes the supplier to limitless risk).  
   - If the clause is silent, judges fall back on “a reasonable time in the circumstances,” usually interpreted as the shortest interval in which the seller could have re-sold the cancelled item or service.  

E. Build in a short technical buffer for buyer’s remorse  
   - Many e-commerce platforms voluntarily give 30 min – 2 h “mistake window” so the order can be cancelled before it hits the warehouse; this is not a legal requirement but reduces charge-backs and goodwill claims.  

F. Document exceptional-circumstances overrides  
   - Force-majeure, illness, transport strikes, etc. are normally accepted as grounds to waive the cut-off, provided the customer gives prompt notice and evidence.  

Bottom line: the “reasonable” grace period is determined by (i) the minimum period the law insists on for that type of contract, plus (ii) any extra time needed to keep the clause fair in light of the seller’s exposure, minus (iii) any shorter period the customer validly agrees to once the seller has given clear, upfront disclosure.
