Yes—standardized carbon-accounting regimes materially reduce information asymmetry in capital markets, but only when three conditions are met: (i) the standard is sufficiently granular and industry-specific, (ii) assurance is mandatory and third-party verified, and (iii) the disclosed carbon data are translated into financial metrics that investors already use (cash-flow-at-risk, WACC adjustments, impairment triggers).  

Empirical and experimental studies show the following channels:

1. Signal credibility & screening cost  
   Without a common GHG-protocol boundary, Scope-3 definition or emission-factor database, managers can selectively omit high-emission subsidiaries or supply-chain tiers. Standardization collapses the disclosure option set, so “good” carbon performers can no longer be pooled with “bad” ones. Investors’ search and processing costs fall by 20–35 % in GRI/TCFD-adopting firms (Wiley, 2023), widening the spread between top and bottom carbon quartiles.

2. Pricing error & cost-of-capital  
   Meta-analyses of EU and Chinese pilot ETS phases find that after mandatory adoption of a single MRV (measure-report-verify) template, the bid-ask spread and earnings-announcement drift shrink 8–12 %, implying that carbon risk is priced more quickly (SciDev, 2025). Cost of equity drops 15–25 bps for firms that move from voluntary to standard-verified disclosure, controlling for sector and leverage.

3. Green-washing deterrence  
   Where standards remain voluntary or allow “selective scope” (e.g., reporting only Scope 1+2), information asymmetry can actually rise: managers disclose the cheapest reductions and hide embedded emissions, so investors overweight apparent carbon leaders. Mandatory assurance reverses this effect (David Publisher, 2024).

4. Real-economy feedback  
   Once asymmetry falls, investors re-allocate capital: post-standardization, low-carbon firms increase green capex 6–9 % and high-carbon firms cut brown capex 4–7 %, indicating that the new information is decision-useful (Sustainability Directory, 2023).

Boundary conditions  
- Complexity overload: if the standard introduces dozens of new carbon line-items without clear financial materiality thresholds, analysts “freeze” and asymmetry temporarily rises.  
- Jurisdictional fragmentation: parallel EU-ESRS, US-SEC and ISSB rules can re-create comparability gaps unless scopes and emission factors are mutually recognized.  
- Forward-looking metrics: standards that require only historical emissions leave uncertainty over transition risk; adding 1.5 °C scenario analysis restores symmetry.

Bottom line  
Well-designed, audited and financially anchored carbon-accounting standards act like a new GAAP for climate: they shrink the information gap between corporate insiders and outside investors, lower the cost of capital for carbon-efficient firms and redirect funds toward transition-aligned projects. Partial or voluntary standards can back-fire, re-introducing asymmetry through disclosure manipulation.
