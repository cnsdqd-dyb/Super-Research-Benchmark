The dominant strategy is to create a “biphasic” surface that simultaneously

1. builds and maintains a molecularly thin, cartilage-mimicking lubricating film, and  
2. limits the size and bio-reactivity of any particles that are still released.

Practically this is done by grafting a soft, hydrophilic, zwitterionic polymer brush (most often poly-2-methacryloyloxyethyl-phosphorylcholine, PMPC) onto the hard bearing surface (cross-linked UHMWPE, CoCr, ceramic or TiN-coated Ti).

- The brush traps synovial fluid water and proteins within seconds, giving a fluid-film <100 nm thick that keeps friction <0.05 and almost eliminates adhesive wear.  
- Because the graft layer itself is very soft and slowly consumed, the few particles that are generated are sub-micron, highly hydrated and protein-coated; they are therefore too small and too inert to trigger macrophage activation/osteolysis.  
- The underlying substrate is left intact, so the long-term mechanical strength and fatigue life of the implant are preserved.

This “soft-on-hard” brush approach resolves the classical competition: the lubricating film is continuously regenerated (so friction stays low) while the inevitable wear debris is rendered biologically harmless, giving both high durability and excellent biocompatibility.
