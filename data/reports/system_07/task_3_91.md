Destination cognition is not built in one step; it is iteratively “written” by two successive, but partially overlapping, information cycles:

1. The pre-trip “virtual cycle” (mainly on-line, low-risk, high-control)  
2. The on-site “experiential cycle” (physical, high-stimulus, low-control)

The two cycles do not simply add up; they talk to each other.  
What the tourist already “knows” from the virtual cycle becomes the interpretive frame for the sensory data collected in the experiential cycle, while the surprises, contradictions or confirmations encountered on-site are fed back to re-edit the original mental model.  
The final destination cognition is the stabilized narrative that remains when the two cycles reach a subjectively satisfactory fit.

Below is a process model that shows how the interaction unfolds.

--------------------------------------------------
A. THE VIRTUAL CYCLE – “writing the first draft”
--------------------------------------------------
Stimulus  
│ 360° video, VR headset, AR overlay, 3-D sound, haptic or even olfactory plugs  
│  
Perceptual filtering  
│ → selective attention is guided by self-controlled navigation (gaze, teleport, menu choices)  
│  
Mental imagery formation  
│ → the brain runs an “embodied simulation” (same premotor & sensory areas used in real movement)  
│ → imagery is stored as an “event file” (place + affect + action possibility)  
│  
Expectation & emotional tagging  
│ → dopaminergic anticipation circuitry is activated more strongly than during the real visit  
│ → the place is already “pre-lived”, so the tourist arrives with a proto-cognitive map and a valence tag (“this will be awesome / safe / difficult”)  
│  
Behavioural intention  
│ → booking, itinerary fine-tuning, social media sharing, further information search  
└-------------------------------------------------

--------------------------------------------------
B. THE ON-SITE CYCLE – “editing the draft”
--------------------------------------------------
Sensory bombardment  
│ olfaction, proprioception, thermoception, kinaesthetics, social density – channels unavailable on-line  
│  
Prediction error check  
│ the tourist continuously matches incoming data with the virtual event file  
│ → confirmation: “Exactly like in the VR!” → strengthens original nodes, produces “recognition reward”  
│ → disconfirmation: “It smells like diesel, not pine” → triggers re-categorisation  
│
Affective swing  
│ positive surprise → elevation & story-worthiness  
│ negative surprise → cognitive dissonance or “romantic withdrawal” (tourist ignores the mismatch)  
│
Behavioural adjustment  
│ on-the-spot re-planning (length of stay, spending, route, photo spots)  
│ social narration (live posting) starts to re-broadcast the edited model to peers  
└-------------------------------------------------

--------------------------------------------------
C. FEEDBACK LOOPS THAT MERGE THE TWO CYCLES
--------------------------------------------------
1. Priming loop  
   Rich VR → stronger mental imagery → heightened attention to matching cues on-site → deeper encoding of those cues → denser memory.

2. Familiarity-reduces-novelty loop  
   High spatial presence in VR → lower psychophysiological stress (lower cortisol) on arrival → more cognitive resources left for elaboration rather than orientation → destination cognition becomes more nuanced instead of being dominated by “wow” or “fear”.

3. Discrepancy-resolution loop  
   When mismatch is moderate (not traumatic) the tourist engages in “creative elaboration” (re-frames, photographs a different angle, tells a reconciling story). The final memory often contains elements of both the virtual preview and the reconciled on-site version – a blended reality.

4. Rehearsal-and-consolidation loop  
   After the trip the tourist often re-visits the virtual environment (YouTube 360°, hotel walk-through, etc.). Now the virtual scene serves as a retrieval cue for autobiographical memory, further fusing the two sources into one coherent narrative.

--------------------------------------------------
D. RESULTANT DESTINATION COGNITION STRUCTURE
--------------------------------------------------
The tourist ends up with a three-layer cognitive object:

Layer 1 – Sensory schema  
   colours, landmarks, soundscape, smell, tactile details (mostly from on-site, but some “implanted” by high-fidelity VR)

Layer 2 – Narrative schema  
   causal story (“I crossed this bridge at sunset…”) integrating virtual anticipation + real experience

Layer 3 – Evaluative schema  
   overall attitude, attachment, intention to re-visit or recommend; heavily influenced by the valence of the confirmation/disconfirmation sequence

--------------------------------------------------
E. PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS FOR MARKETERS & DESIGNERS
--------------------------------------------------
1. Build “honest” VR: include ordinary or even mildly negative cues (crowds, weather) to prevent large negative prediction errors.  
2. Insert “anchors” that will be easy to recognise on-site (distinctive tree, floor mosaic) – they become powerful recognition-reward triggers.  
3. Allow post-visit re-access to the same virtual scene; it functions as a memory consolidation and word-of-mouth generator.  
4. Use dynamic social elements in VR (avatars, crowd noise) – research shows they enhance mental imagery and later social para-interaction on-site.  
5. Provide AR overlays during the real visit (e.g., phone camera shows the same temple at night) to blend the cycles in real time.

--------------------------------------------------
BOTTOM LINE
--------------------------------------------------
Destination cognition is not the sum of “what I saw on-line” plus “what I saw in reality”.  
It is the narrative that survives the dialogue between the two: a continuously edited, emotionally tagged, self-relevant story that starts in the headset and ends in the souvenir.
