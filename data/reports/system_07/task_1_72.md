Scenario design in environmental risk stress testing directly shapes capital adequacy calibration by determining the **severity, scope, and transmission channels** of environmental shocks. The way scenarios are constructed—whether they reflect physical risks (e.g., floods, droughts), transition risks (e.g., carbon pricing, policy shifts), or compound risks—**influences the magnitude of projected losses**, and thus the **capital buffers** institutions are expected to hold.

### Key Mechanisms:

1. **Loss Projection and Capital Buffer Sizing**  
   Scenarios define the **adverse but plausible shocks** used to estimate credit, market, and operational losses. More severe or granular scenarios (e.g., region-specific physical risk events or sharp carbon price shocks) can **inflate loss estimates**, leading to **higher Pillar 2 capital requirements** under ICAAP. For example, a stress test using a **high carbon price trajectory** may show significant deterioration in high-carbon sectors, prompting **capital add-ons** to cover tail-risk losses.

2. **Transmission Channel Modeling**  
   Scenario design must explicitly model **how environmental risks feed into financial risk**—via default rates, collateral devaluation, or earnings volatility. If scenarios fail to capture **second-round effects** (e.g., supply chain disruptions or policy feedback loops), they may **underestimate capital needs**. The EBA and BIS emphasize that **compound risks** (e.g., physical + transition) should be integrated to avoid **under-calibration** of capital.

3. **Time Horizon and Capital Planning**  
   Short-term scenarios (1–3 years) are used for **stress testing capital adequacy**, while long-term scenarios (10+ years) inform **business model resilience**. If long-term scenarios show **persistent earnings erosion** or **stranded assets**, regulators may require **higher capital ratios** or **restrictions on dividend payouts**, even if short-term stress tests pass.

4. **Scenario Granularity and RWA Adjustments**  
   Granular scenarios (e.g., sector-specific, geography-specific) allow for **more precise calibration of risk-weighted assets (RWAs)**. This can lead to **climate-adjusted RWAs**, effectively increasing capital requirements for **environmentally exposed portfolios**, even within Pillar 1.

5. **Model Validation and Supervisory Challenge**  
   Weak or generic scenarios (e.g., using only NGFS orderly transition) may be **challenged by supervisors**, leading to **model overrides** or **higher capital floors**. Conversely, **robust, institution-specific scenarios** that reflect **tail-risk events** (e.g., delayed transition + extreme weather) can **justify lower capital add-ons**, if they demonstrate **adequate loss absorption**.

---

### Bottom Line:
**Scenario design is not just a modeling input—it is a regulatory lever.**  
Poorly designed scenarios can **understate risk**, leading to **inadequate capital buffers**. Conversely, **severe, forward-looking, and institution-specific scenarios** can **justify higher capital requirements**, or **steer capital reallocation** away from climate-vulnerable exposures. In short, **the scenario is the calibration**.
