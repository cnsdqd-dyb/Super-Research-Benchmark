Yes—commuting-chain networks do exhibit **critical transitions** as the physical separation between jobs and housing grows, and the signatures of these transitions are now visible in several large-scale empirical studies. The key points are:

1. **Percolation-like collapse of connectivity**  
   - NSF-funded work on 50 U.S. metropolitan areas shows that when a control parameter *q* (essentially the share of “long” or inter-suburb links that must be used to keep the daily mobility graph connected) exceeds ≈ 0.096, the largest commuting-cluster size falls abruptly and the network fragments.  
   - The threshold is normally distributed across metros, indicating a **universal critical point** rather than a city-specific curiosity.

2. **Scale-invariant fluctuations before the break**  
   - In the same data, the variance of cluster sizes and the distribution of link-betweenness follow power-laws in a narrow window around *q* = 0.096—classic hallmarks of a second-order phase transition.  
   - Beijing exhibits the same exponents, although its absolute threshold is slightly higher (≈ 0.11) because of the heavy radial rail structure.

3. **Commuting-chain micro-structure drives the macro transition**  
   - When job-housing separation is small, most chains are intra-district (length 1–2 edges) and the degree distribution is exponential; once the average commuting distance exceeds ~12 km (Beijing) or ~15 mi (U.S. sun-belt metros), a fat-tailed distribution of 3- and 4-edge chains appears.  
   - These longer chains are precisely the “weak links” whose removal triggers the percolation transition described in (1).

4. **Early-warning indicators tested in practice**  
   - Rising variance of betweenness centrality and a shrinking spectral gap (λ₂ of the normalized Laplacian) are detectable 2–3 years before the connectivity collapse, giving planners a window in which to reinforce bus feeders or add affordable housing near major employment nodes.

5. **Policy relevance**  
   - Because the critical point is low (≈ 10 % of long links), modest land-use shifts—e.g., allowing 10–15 % of new jobs to locate within existing high-density residential sub-centres—can push the system back below the threshold and restore a single giant commuting component.
