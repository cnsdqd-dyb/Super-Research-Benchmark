CBDC smart-contract “policy tools” can, in principle, let a central bank move the money-supply dial with surgical precision: negative interest that accrues by the millisecond, stimulus that expires if not spent within 14 days, liquidity injections that are released only after 5 000 SMEs have digitally signed a loan agreement.  
The same code, however, is a single-point amplifier of error. A one-line typo in the spending-eligibility routine or a mis-calibrated wallet-balance limit is copied to every node, executed automatically and—because revocation is deliberately hard—cannot be unwound. The Polygon–IRON episode (US $262 million collateral frozen by an immutable contract) is an unregulated-market preview of what a *Systemic Operational Risk Event* (SORE) looks like when the asset is the monetary base itself.

So the tools do **not** “balance” control and risk in any self-correcting way; they simply move the frontier outward in both directions. The central bank gains *micro-leverage* (real-time, account-level steering), but it also acquires *macro-fragility* (country-wide, simultaneous mis-direction of funds). Whether this trade-off is acceptable depends on governance overlays that are still largely missing:

1. **Kill-switches**: legal authority and technical path to roll back or upgrade contracts without destroying finality.  
2. **Gradualism**: hard-coded ceilings on how much money any single smart policy rule can move per day/week.  
3. **Dual-track architecture**: monetary-policy logic kept in an upgradable, permissioned layer; user-level programmability (coupons, stamps, expiry) isolated in disposable “sandbox” contracts.  
4. **Mandatory simulation forks**: any policy rule must run for N days on a full-scale shadow ledger seeded with anonymised production data before main-net release.  
5. **Liability assignment**: central bank balance-sheet explicitly books a “smart-contract error reserve” so that losses are socialised transparently rather than emerging as ad-hoc confiscation or devaluation.

Without these guard-rails, the probability distribution of policy outcomes develops a fat tail: the same code that lets the central bank fine-tune aggregate demand can also fine-tune a depression. In short, programmable money is not inherently unstable, but it is *unforgiving*; the accident-prevention layer has to be designed *into* the currency, not hoped for after the fact.
