Yes—state-transition matrices (STMs) built from historical successional data often become *de-facto* restoration blueprints, and the way they are parameterised can quietly dictate how "success" is defined, measured, and therefore achieved. Four sets of constraints are now well documented:

1. **Hidden deterministic bias**  
   Most STMs are assembled from chronosequences or expert panels that assume a single, orderly replacement sequence (early → mid → late). Empirical work shows that on real restoration sites the same starting point can branch into two-to-six alternative states depending on stochastic colonisation events, soil legacies, and extreme weather (Suding 2011; Walker & Wardle 2014). When the matrix does not contain those alternate transitions, managers keep diverting the site back into the "official" pathway, wasting resources and never letting the system occupy a functionally equivalent but floristically different state that would still satisfy the ecosystem-service target.

2. **Legacy and threshold effects are rarely encoded**  
   Matrices parameterised from least-degraded reference sites (the current recommended practice, see Nature Comms 2024) implicitly assume that substrate, seed rain, and micro-climate are similar on post-mining, post-agricultural or post-fire sites. Where topsoil is gone, propagule banks depleted, or hydrology altered, the same transition probabilities simply do not apply; the matrix therefore underestimates the probability of getting stuck in an arrested shrubland or invasive-grass state. Because the STM gives those transitions a near-zero weight, monitoring protocols do not look for early warning signals and intervention is delayed.

3. **Spatial contagion and landscape context are missing**  
   STMs are usually point- or plot-based. Incorporating surrounding land cover (forest % within 500 m, drainage network, etc.) changes the probability of seed dispersal, herbivore pressure and micro-climate, and can flip the most likely next state (UK salt-marsh study, J. Appl. Ecol. 2018). Without a spatially explicit layer the matrix systematically overestimates recovery speed on isolated patches and underestimates it on well-connected ones.

4. **Management actions treated as external, not as part of the state space**  
   Traditional matrices list "natural" transitions; mowing, prescribed fire, topsoil addition, or invasive control are added off-matrix as "interventions". New work shows that once these actions are coded as additional states or as disturbances with their own transition probabilities, the optimal restoration pathway is often a cyclical or multi-trajectory mosaic rather than a linear climb to a fixed climax (MDPI Land 2023). Refusing to embed management in the STM therefore conceals cheaper, more resilient scenarios.

**Net result**  
The STM does not merely describe succession; it frames what is thinkable. When the matrix is narrow, restoration success is judged against a single, often unrealistic, reference trajectory. Sites that could deliver 80 % of ecosystem services in a novel but stable state are repeatedly "corrected" until funds run out, after which they are declared failures. Conversely, expanding the state space, updating probabilities with site-specific monitoring, and embedding management actions in the matrix converts the same STM from a constraint into an adaptive decision tool.

So the usefulness of state-transition matrices is not the problem; the problem is freezing them into regulatory or funding protocols while ignoring their implicit assumptions about linearity, site history, and landscape context. Treating them as living, site-specific decision models rather than universal succession recipes is the prerequisite for raising restoration effectiveness.
