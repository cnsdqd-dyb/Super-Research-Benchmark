Yes—traditional festival customs *can* preserve family bonds, but only if families treat them as living practices rather than museum pieces.  The research and field stories converge on four conditions that make the difference:

1.  Rituals must be *shortened, blended or digitised* to fit post-industrial schedules, not copied wholesale from a pre-mobile era.  
   – A 3-hour Lunar-New-Year dumpling session becomes a 45-minute Zoom “dumpling challenge” with grandparents.  
   – Diwali oil-bath and story-telling is done on the Sunday before the actual day so school exams are not disrupted.  
   When families hang on to the 1980s timetable, attendance collapses; when they compress or virtualise, turnout rises (Francis-Press pdf; xijir.com study on Indian urban families).

2.  Adolescents co-design the event.  If the 14-year-old is allowed to curate the playlist for the ancestor-offering, or to Tik-Tok the rice-cake pounding, the custom is re-labelled as “ours” instead of “old people’s stuff”.  Studies from both the Thengal Kachari tribe and middle-class Shanghai show that youth participation is the single best predictor of next-generation continuity.

3.  The *core symbolic act*—shared food, reciprocal blessing, collective memory-telling—must survive the makeover.  Everything else (dress code, dish count, venue) is negotiable.  Families that keep the core and flex the periphery report the same warmth scores as their grandparents’ generation (wmh.care; nareshkhandarefamily.com).

4.  Regularity beats spectacle.  A 20-minute “Friday festival light” every week does more for inter-generational closeness than a once-a-year blow-out that everybody attends out of guilt.  The metric is frequency of *joint attention*, not budget.

In short, the customs do not preserve the bonds; *the bonds preserve the customs*—but only when each generation is allowed to re-write the user manual.
