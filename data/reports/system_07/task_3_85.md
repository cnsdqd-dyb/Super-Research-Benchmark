Traditional mutual-aid networks do continue to supply social support in places that are now dominated by short-term residents, migrants, or other “floating” populations—but only under certain conditions and usually in a weakened form. The evidence from rural Africa, urban Haiti, post-conflict Sudan, and rapidly urbanising parts of Asia points to four recurring patterns:

1. Core functions survive if the network can replace stable membership with “transactional” reciprocity.  
   - In Niger and Sudan the old kin- or caste-based labour-sharing groups have morphed into pay-back or cash-in-kind arrangements that do not assume life-long residence.  
   - Women’s rotating savings groups, church tithing circles, and mosque-based charity pools still operate in migrant enclaves because entry is tied to a small, regular contribution rather than to ancestry or land ownership.

2. Exclusion rises when the original glue was a closed identity.  
   - In Indian villages where farmer producer groups were tied to high-caste households, in-migrants from other castes or landless labourers are simply never invited; the network still functions for the old residents but covers a shrinking share of the total population.  
   - In Haitian urban neighbourhoods, historical “lakou” (courtyard) mutual-aid clusters fragment once renters out-number long-term kin; the remaining members withhold food-sharing or funeral assistance from “strangers.”

3. Scale and speed of in-migration matter.  
   - A gradual influx allows bridging ties to form; sudden surges (climate-displaced Haitians moving into Port-au-Prince peri-urban zones, or Sudanese IDPs crowding into Kadugli) overwhelm the stock of shared resources and trust, so the network either collapses or becomes a gate-keeping device.

4. External actors can either cushion or further erode the network.  
   - Humanitarian cash-transfer programmes that subcontract the existing mutual-aid committee (Sudan) have, in some cases, injected enough liquidity to keep the system running while loosening exclusionary rules.  
   - Conversely, NGOs that bypass the traditional structure and create parallel “beneficiary committees” accelerate the decline of the old network by draining leadership time and legitimising new, competing solidarities.

Bottom line  
Traditional mutual-aid does not automatically disappear in high-floating-population settings, but its coverage narrows and its character shifts from unconditional, long-horizon reciprocity to conditional, short-cycle exchange. Without deliberate bridging measures—multi-ethnic savings circles, landlord-tenant joint committees, or external top-ups that reward inclusive practices—the network tends to become a residual support system for the original residents rather than the community as a whole.
