In digital-media art, “language” is no longer a single channel of signs; it is a braided signal that is seen, heard and felt at the same time.  
The work is not “multimedia” (a collection of parallel tracks) but “multimodal” (one coherent utterance that is articulated by eye, ear and skin together).  
Integration happens at four nested levels: data, time, space and body.  

--------------------------------------------------
1. Data-level fusion – making the three channels speak the same “phoneme”
--------------------------------------------------
• Shared parameter space  
  – A single numerical stream (sensor, generative algorithm, data set) is mapped to color, pitch and vibration frequency simultaneously.  
  – Example: a climate-data piece turns CO₂ values into (a) a red-cyan color gradient, (b) a glissando between 220 Hz and 880 Hz, (c) a 40-200 Hz haptic wave in a floor plate. The audience literally “stands in the data.”

• Cross-modal encoding  
  – Color hue is translated into musical interval (red = minor third, green = perfect fifth).  
  – Amplitude envelope of sound drives the duty-cycle of vibro-tactile motors so that the transient of a drum is felt as a sharp thump on the forearm.

--------------------------------------------------
2. Time-level fusion – micro-synchrony
--------------------------------------------------
The brain detects unity when the three onsets fall within ±30 ms (the “temporal binding window” of multisensory integration).  
Digital art therefore treats audiovisual-tactile events as one timeline:

  Frame 0     light flash  
  Frame 1     10 ms audio click  
  Frame 2     12 ms tactor burst  

All three timestamps are stamped by the same SMPTE or OSC clock so that the spectator experiences one “object” instead of three coincidental stimuli.

--------------------------------------------------
3. Space-level fusion – superimposing modalities in the same place
--------------------------------------------------
• Haptic-optical collocation  
  – A 4 kHz ultrasound phased array creates a focal point in mid-air; the same coordinates are illuminated by a laser volumetric display.  
  – When the visitor puts a finger in the focal point, she both sees a floating pixel and feels a 4 kHz vibration on the skin. The brain automatically attributes the sensation to the visible spot (ventriloquism effect).

• Audio-haptic perspective  
  – Binaural audio is rendered so that the virtual sound source is at the exact x-y-z position of the vibro-tactile actuator sewn in the chair.  
  – When the helicopter “flies overhead,” the spectator hears it above and simultaneously feels the downdraft on the shoulders.

--------------------------------------------------
4. Body-level fusion – turning the spectator into the conductor
--------------------------------------------------
• Bio-feedback loop  
  – EEG (visual attention) and GSR (arousal) are streamed in real time.  
  – High attention → visual resolution increases, musical harmonics brighten, tactile amplitude decreases (the body is “rewarded” with calm).  
  – The participant discovers that her own physiology is the grammar of the piece.

• Wearable “language”  
  – A haptic sleeve with 24 tactors is programmed with spatio-temporal patterns that match phonological features of American Sign Language (location, hand-shape, movement).  
  – Deaf and hearing visitors can literally feel signed poetry while seeing an abstract particle animation driven by the same motion-capture data.

--------------------------------------------------
Design patterns artists use to weave the three strands
--------------------------------------------------
1. Redundant reinforcement (low cognitive load)  
   Same message on all channels—ideal for accessibility or emotional punch.

2. Complementary distribution (higher poetic load)  
   Each modality carries a different semantic slice; meaning emerges only when they are combined (McGurk effect scaled to art).

3. Metamorphic metaphor (trans-sensory pun)  
   A rising melodic line is simultaneously a visual upward scroll and a vibration that travels from ankle to waist—synesthetic pun on “ascension.”

--------------------------------------------------
Concrete artworks that demonstrate the integration
--------------------------------------------------
• “Rain Room” (Random International)  
  Visual: water droplets lit by stroboscopic LEDs.  
  Auditory: the sound of rain is spatialized so that the dry spot the visitor stands in is also the auditory “hole.”  
  Tactile: sub-bass speakers under the floor vibrate with the low-frequency rumble of falling water, making the dry patch feel “protected.”

• “Touch-Music” (Ei Wada + haptic team)  
  Reel-to-reel tape is pulled across the artist’s body; the visual motion is captured by a camera, the magnetic signal is played as sound, and the same tape is attached to tactile transducers so the audience can feel the magnetic “memory” on their forearms.

• “Somatic Cinema” (Brenda Laurel & Interval Research, VR)  
  A short film whose narrative branch points are triggered only when the viewer’s galvanic skin response crosses a threshold; at the same moment the chair delivers a heart-resonant 80 Hz vibration and the color palette shifts, making the body’s unconscious reaction part of the cinematic language.

--------------------------------------------------
Take-away for creators
--------------------------------------------------
1. Build one parametric engine, not three separate tracks.  
2. Calibrate latency to < 30 ms if you want one perceptual object.  
3. Colocate stimuli in space or provide a strong directional cue.  
4. Let the visitor’s body become a writable surface: bio-sense, affect, gesture.  
5. Remember accessibility: a piece that is legible through eye, ear AND skin can be “read” by people with very different sensory profiles, turning multimodality into inclusive language.

When these principles are followed, visual, auditory and tactile languages do not simply accompany one another; they conjugate into a single verb that the spectator inhabits.
