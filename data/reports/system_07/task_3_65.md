Yes – but only indirectly.  
A geochemical anomaly map tells you where the metal is; it does **not** tell you whether that metal can be turned into money inside a city.  What it does do is supply the first, indispensable input to a grade-gradient model that later decides “ore vs. urban waste”.  The steps are:

1.  **Anomaly → local grade profile**  
    The survey gives a point or block value (e.g. 800 ppm Cu in a garden soil, 1.2 kg/t REE in a landfill cell).  These point values are interpolated into a 3-D “grade shell” that shows how concentration changes away from the anomaly centre – the grade gradient.

2.  **Grade gradient → tonnage/grade curve**  
    By slicing the shell at successively lower cut-off grades you generate the classic resource curve: tonnes vs. average grade.  The **steepness** of the gradient now controls the shape of this curve.  
    - A **steep** gradient (metal drops sharply) gives a small, high-grade “nugget”; a **gentle** gradient gives a large, low-grade halo.  
    - The same total metal can sit in either geometry; the gradient tells you which.

3. **Urban economic filter**  
    Urban deposits have fixed, usually **higher** cut-offs than greenfield deposits because:  
    - Land access, relocation, permitting, health & safety, traffic, dust, noise, after-use restoration and social-licence costs are charged **inside** the mine gate.  
    - Throughput is often capped by the city’s waste-handling licence, not by mill capacity, so “economy of scale” is weak.  
    - Revenue is frequently capped by secondary-metal prices (scrap Cu, Al, NdFeB magnets) that sit below LME prices.  

    These extra costs shift the break-even cut-off upward.  The grade-gradient curve is now interrogated at this **urban-specific** cut-off, not at the traditional open-pit cut-off.  If the gradient is too gentle the shell that survives the higher cut-off contains almost no tonnes → project dies before any reserve is declared.  If the gradient is steep, a small but above-cut-off tonnage survives → project can advance to reserve estimation, pilot plant and financial model.

4.  **Sensitivity loop**  
    Because urban cut-offs are driven by local gate fees, real-estate prices and metal discounts, the critical cut-off can move 30–50 % in a few years.  A steep gradient gives you **robustness**: even if the cut-off rises, the high-grade core still survives.  A gentle gradient gives you **fragility**: a small rise in the cut-off erases the remaining ore.

Therefore the geochemical anomaly does not **itself** impose an economic constraint; it merely defines the spatial distribution of metal.  It is the **grade gradient derived from that anomaly** which determines whether enough metal sits above the (higher, urban-specific) cut-off to pay the city’s unique cost base.  In short, anomaly identification is necessary, but the gradient ultimately decides if the urban mineral development is economic.
