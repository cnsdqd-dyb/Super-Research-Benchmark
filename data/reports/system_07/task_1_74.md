The species–area relationship (SAR) is the single most powerful empirical rule in conservation planning: for a given habitat type, the number of species (S) increases with area (A) as  

  S = c Aᶻ                  (1)  

where the exponent z is typically 0.15–0.35.  
Because the curve is decelerating, the *first* hectares added contain far more species than the *last* hectares.  This simple geometry is what sets the “critical fragment size” (CFS): the minimum area below which the SAR predicts that a fragment will lose species faster than it can (re)colonise them, so that the fragment becomes a net sink.  CFS therefore acts as a hard constraint on how small a protected area (PA) can be while still retaining its original biodiversity.  Five design consequences follow.

1.  Lower bound for single reserves  
   If the target is to keep *all* species that currently occur at a site, the reserve must lie on the *upper* flat part of the SAR.  For Amazonian birds, for example, the SAR asymptotes only above ≈10 000 ha; below ≈50 ha the probability of retaining a full forest-dependent assemblage falls below 50 % even under strict protection.  Hence a PA network that is built only from parcels <50 ha will fail to protect area-sensitive taxa no matter how many such parcels are added.

2.  Zoning of management intensity  
   The SAR slope is steepest at small areas, so the *marginal* biodiversity return of the first 100 ha is high, but the *risk* of stochastic extinction is also high.  Many agencies now use CFS as a zoning threshold:  
   ‑ < CFS  community sinks → do not gazette unless they are stepping-stones in a restoration matrix;  
   ‑ CFS – 10 × CFS  “core” zone with full legal protection;  
   ‑ > 10 × CFS  “wilderness” zone where natural disturbance regimes and long-term range shifts can be accommodated.

3.  Choice between “Single Large or Several Small” (SLOSS)  
   The SAR predicts that *one* large fragment of area A always contains more species than *any one* of n small fragments of area A/n.  Meta-analysis shows, however, that when the n small fragments are *pooled* they often exceed the large fragment in species richness because β-diversity among them is high.  The crossover point is the CFS:  
   ‑ if the small fragments are each > CFS, the pooled set (SS) is usually richer (SS > SL);  
   ‑ if the small fragments are each < CFS, they are already depauperate and the large fragment wins (SL > SS).  
   Thus CFS tells planners when the SS option is safe and when it is not.

4.  Buffering edge effects and extinction debt  
   Edge penetration distances (≈50–500 m depending on taxon) scale with fragment diameter, not area.  A fragment must therefore be several times larger than the CFS derived from the *interior* SAR if the goal is to maintain core habitat.  Many CFS estimates are given as “minimum diameter” rather than “minimum area” for this reason (e.g., 400 m diameter ≈ 13 ha for lowland plants, 1 km diameter ≈ 80 ha for Neotropical primates).

5.  Dynamic reserve design under climate change  
   Species ranges will shift.  Because the SAR exponent z is larger for plants than for most vertebrates, small additional areas yield disproportionately many plant species.  Networks that already contain many small fragments above CFS therefore offer more “no-regret” options for future species than one large fragment of equal total area, provided the fragments are functionally connected.  Planners now use CFS to decide how small a fragment can be while still worth including in a climate-smart connectivity corridor.

Operational rule adopted by an increasing number of agencies (Brazilian Atlantic Forest, EU Natura 2000, Australian CAR reserve system):

  “No gazetted PA < CFS unless it is (i) embedded in a matrix of natural or restored habitat < 1 km away from a source > CFS, or (ii) explicitly justified as a complementary β-diversity sample of a multi-patch network.”

In short, the species–area relationship does not merely *suggest* that bigger is better; it quantifies the threshold below which a protected area is biologically non-viable.  Designing without reference to CFS inevitably creates paper parks that lose species—and credibility—within decades.
