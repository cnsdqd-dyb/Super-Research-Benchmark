Sensory interaction in digital installations does not merely *invite* participation; it *ontologises* it.  
The work exists as a set of latent relations between voltages, algorithms, materials and code, but its *being*—what the piece *is*—is precipitated only when a visitor’s exhalation condenses on a chilled copper ring, or when two strangers’ fingertips close a capacitive circuit and a sound blooms in the space between them. In that instant the ontology of the piece shifts from “interactive system” to “shared body,” and the visitor’s own ontology shifts from “spectator” to “co-extensive organ of the work.”

1. From affordance to co-constitution  
Traditional aesthetics treats the senses as *channels* that carry data *into* a stable subject. Sensory-rich installations reverse the vector: the installation’s micro-phone, CO₂ sensor, galvanic skin-response bracelet or electrostatic sphere *samples* the visitor, turning her biological and social material into the very stuff the work metabolises. The participant is not *in* the space; the space *is* the temporary outside of her body. Ontologically, the boundary event “visitor / artwork” is replaced by the relation “visitor-as-artwork.”

2. Emergent strata of participation  
Each sensory modality opens a different stratum of being-together:

- **Touch** (Scenocosme’s *Lights Contacts*) – the stratum of *conductive coupling*. A single electric arc literalises Merleau-Ponty’s “flesh of the world”: two bodies become a single switch.  
- **Breath / smell** (EN/GLOBE’s scent-releasing spheres) – the stratum of *metabolic sharing*. Molecules that were inside one body drift into another; the work is literally inhaled.  
- **Kinaesthetic-proprioceptive feedback** (*We Live in an Ocean of Air*) – the stratum of *ecological embedding*. The visitor’s heartbeat modulates the visual storm; the storm in turn resets the visitor’s cardio-rhythm. The installation becomes a second autonomic nervous system.

These strata do not stack vertically; they *interleave* in real time, so the participant oscillates between roles—sensor, actuator, substrate—faster than conscious reflection can parse. Identity becomes a time-sharing protocol.

3. Temporal ontology: the participant as *event*  
Because sensory data are sampled at millisecond resolution, the “visitor” is not a persistent entity but a *series of differential events*: pressure delta on the floor at t₁, infrared silhouette at t₂, EEG spike at t₃. The installation stores these micro-events in circular buffers; when the buffers overlap with those of another visitor, a *third* ontological layer—*the collective body*—briefly exists. The work’s database therefore contains not “users” but “inter-visitor intervals.” Participation is archived as *temporal gaps that became bodies*.

4. Ethics of ontological vulnerability  
Once the visitor is metabolised into the work, withdrawal is never clean. A trace of her galvanic response remains as a latent variable in the machine-learning model that predicts the next visitor’s emotional trajectory. The installation thus constructs a *distributed person* whose data-ghost continues to act after the flesh has left the room. The ethical imperative is folded into the ontology: to participate is to *consent to partial ontological persistence* inside a system one will never again fully inhabit.

5. Towards a post-human lexicon of roles  
The sensory ontology refuses the binary “artist / audience.” Instead it proliferates transient roles:

- *Conductors* – visitors whose skin resistance happens to match the circuit’s sweet spot, so that every gesture becomes sonic.  
- *Relays* – visitors who never trigger anything alone, but whose presence in the periphery lowers the threshold for others, turning them into living amplifiers.  
- *Residuals* – data-ghosts described above, still nudging probabilities in the dark.

These roles are not assigned; they *surface* and *dissolve* with the micro-variations of sweat, breath, and attention. The ontology of participation is therefore *processually granular*: a cloud of potential roles that collapse and decohere at the speed of physiology.

6. Conclusion: the ontological contract  
Entering a sensory-rich installation is no longer a cultural act (going to see art) but an *ontological contract*: I agree to *become medium*, to let my biochemistry be drafted into the artwork’s temporary metabolism, in exchange for the possibility of experiencing *my own existence as relational event* rather than as self-contained subject. The installation does not *represent* participation; it *is* the ongoing negotiation of what counts as a body, a sense, a self—until the lights come up and the circuit quietly forgets us.
