Oral instruction mechanisms can be *part* of a sustainable heritage-transmission system, but only if they are treated as living infrastructures that are deliberately redesigned every generation.  
In other words, orality is not automatically adaptive; it has to be *made* adaptive. The evidence from four continents shows the same pattern:

1. Pure, unmediated oral transmission collapses when three social changes coincide:  
   - demographic drift (young people move out of the village/territory),  
   - value drift (the activity is no longer tied to livelihood or prestige), and  
   - media drift (a faster, more entertaining symbolic system is available).  
   This is exactly what is happening to northern-Shaanxi Xiaodiao folk songs, Guizhou accordion clubs, and a dozen other genres documented in the attached studies.

2. Where oral tradition *does* survive, it has been re-engineered along four lines:

   - **“Oral-plus” channels**  
     Elders still teach face-to-face, but the corpus is now also seeded in TikTok challenges, WhatsApp voice groups, kindergarten call-and-response games, and VR story circles. The Sichuan ti-tzu flute masters, for example, record 60-second micro-lessons that students must imitate and post back; the public metrics (likes, duets) become the new “audience” that used to be the village square.

   - **Re-anchoring to new status economies**  
     Heritage skills are linked to jobs (tourism, game-design, museum interpretation, eco-ranger posts) or to micro-celebrity (streaming revenue, festival prizes). Once the practice buys smartphones or school fees, intergenerational prestige is re-established and the oral chain is *worth* maintaining.

   - **Rule-tweaking councils**  
     The community sets up a “heritage committee” whose sole mandate is to *authorize* controlled change: new lyrics, new instruments, fusion genres, female performers where only males were allowed, etc. The committee meets annually, votes, and then *orally* promulgates the updated canon—so the *mechanism* stays oral even though the content mutates.

   - **Memory-technique upgrades**  
     Instead of relying on raw individual memory, communities re-inscribe medieval mnemonic devices (chanted number-codes, kinship metaphors, spatial story-walks) but embed them in school drills, graffiti tags, even football chants. The neuro-cognitive load is shared across artefacts, spaces, and digital prompts, so the tradition is no longer fragile to one elder’s stroke or one migrant’s departure.

3. Policy implication:  
   Governments and NGOs should not “record and archive” oral heritage and walk away; they should finance *rotating apprenticeships* (youngsters paid to live with masters for 6–12 months), *micro-grants for innovation*, and *inter-village oral juries* that licence new versions. Sustainability is achieved when every cohort knows it has the *right* to re-compose, and the *obligation* to transmit—two sides of the same oral coin.

Bottom line: oral instruction can absolutely be sustainable, but only when communities stop treating it as a museum piece and start treating it as an open-source operating system that must be patched, forked, and re-released every generation.
