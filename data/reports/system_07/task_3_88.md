The boundary between ritual integrity and tourist interaction comfort is not drawn by a single rule or signpost; it is continuously produced by three mutually reinforcing “regulators” that the host community controls and the visitor must learn to read.

1. Moral-spatial zoning  
   Sacred topography is mapped in concentric layers: inner precincts (off-limits), transitional verandas (silent observation), and outer plazas (interactive).  These zones are not informal; they are encoded in customary law (awig-awig, adat, fono, etc.) and policed by ritual elders.  A tourist who steps over an invisible line is not merely rude—she trespasses on a relationship between the living community and its ancestors/spirits.  The limit is therefore ontological, not decorative.

2. Temporal gates  
   The ritual calendar is non-negotiable.  “Closed days” or “ancestor days” suspend the tourist economy entirely; on open days the ceremony proceeds on village time, not tour-operator time.  This refusal to reschedule is the most effective everyday safeguard against commodification: the ritual keeps its own rhythm and tourists must orbit it, not vice-versa.

3. Performative keys  
   Communities run two parallel registers of the same practice:  
   - the “for-us” register (lontar, kiva, kastom house, kiva ceremony) whose meaning is complete without spectators;  
   - the “for-them” register (shortened, translated, outward-facing version) that satisfies tourist curiosity without exposing sacred core.  
   The switch between registers is explicit and embodied: different costume pieces, different language, different spatial orientation.  When the key changes, the visitor is told—sometimes gently, sometimes firmly—that the inner register has begun and cameras/footsteps must stop.

Comfort for the tourist is therefore not created by diluting the ritual, but by making the boundary legible: clear signage, briefings by local guides, visible markers (cloth bands, closed gates, barefoot zones), and predictable temporal windows.  Integrity is preserved because the community retains the semiotic monopoly: it decides which register is shown, when, and to whom.  The moment visitors accept that the limit is non-negotiable, interaction comfort actually rises—there is no anxiety about “doing the wrong thing” because the rules have been made explicit and are enforced with consistency and respect.
