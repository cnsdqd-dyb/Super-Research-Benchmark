Effective population size (Nₑ) is the single strongest genomic predictor of how much **genetic load** (the fitness loss caused by deleterious mutations) a population will carry after its range contracts under climate change.  
The reason is simple: Nₑ determines how efficiently natural selection purges those mutations. When climate warming forces a species to retreat, three things happen in parallel:

1. Census size and Nₑ drop – often by one to two orders of magnitude.  
2. The retreat usually starts from the range edge, so the survivors are already the most drift-prone, least-connected demes.  
3. The contraction is rapid (decades rather than millennia), so the population has no time to re-equilibrate.

Because selection efficacy scales with Nₑ, every halving of Nₑ roughly doubles the fixation probability of a mildly deleterious allele. Empirical data from *Arabidopsis thaliana* show that 74–94 % of the among-population variance in genetic load is explained by present-day Nₑ alone; populations that have experienced the smallest historical Nₑ carry the highest load today. Simulations and alpine-chipmunk comparisons confirm the same pattern: post-contraction nucleotide diversity falls, relatedness rises, and the frequency of homozygous derived LoF (loss-of-function) alleles increases most where Nₑ has collapsed.

Thus, during a climate-driven range contraction the **lower the effective population size**, the **less purifying selection can act**, so **genetic load accumulates**, accelerating the extinction vortex.
