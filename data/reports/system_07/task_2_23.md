The best-performing porosity-gradient architecture is a **radial (centrifugal) gradient** in which porosity increases from the periphery toward the centre:

- **Peripheral zone** (≈ 50 % porosity, 100–200 µm pores)  
  – Dense strut network gives the construct its mechanical strength (compressive yield and elastic modulus) and shields the edge from stress concentrations.  
- **Central zone** (≈ 70 % porosity, 300–500 µm interconnected pores)  
  – Large, straight channels let cells migrate rapidly, allow vascular ingrowth and provide room for ECM deposition.  

Average porosity is kept at the “sweet-spot” of 60 ± 5 %, so global stiffness is only 10–15 % lower than that of a 60 % uniform scaffold, whereas cell infiltration depth after 7–10 days is 2- to 3-fold higher. In vivo data show that the **forward** version of the gradient (50 % → 70 % from outside to inside) gives the lowest cell death rate and the highest mineralisation after 4 weeks, while still matching the elastic modulus of trabecular bone (0.3–0.8 GPa for Ti- or Zn-based alloys; 5–15 MPa for PCL/β-TCP composites).

If the application is a soft-tissue template (skin, cartilage), the same principle is translated into a **trilayer stack**:

- **Layer 1** (top, 50 % porosity, small fibres) – strength & cell anchoring  
- **Layer 2** (mid, 65 %) – transition  
- **Layer 3** (bottom, 80 %) – large pores for rapid fibroblast/keratinocyte infiltration  

Thus, a **radial (or layer-wise) gradient that moves from ~50 % porosity at the load-bearing surface to ~70–80 % in the cell-colonising core** currently offers the best compromise between fast cell migration and long-term mechanical biocompatibility.
