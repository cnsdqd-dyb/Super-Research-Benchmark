Balancing warfarin’s therapeutic range (INR 2.0–3.0, or 2.5–3.5 for valves) against the day-to-day swings created by food and metabolic clearance is done by (1) standardising the patient’s vitamin-K input and (2) titrating the weekly warfarin dose to whatever clearance rate that particular patient–diet–drug combination produces.  
The practical steps are:

1. Fix the vitamin-K “signal”  
   • Give a single, written list of portion sizes for foods that supply ≥20 µg vitamin K per serving (spinach, kale, broccoli, soy, liver, etc.).  
   • Ask the patient to eat the same number of portions every week (not every day). This converts a “variable inhibitor” into a constant background so the INR change reflects warfarin clearance, not day-to-day diet noise.  
   • If the patient suddenly changes intake (e.g., starts a green-tea binge or leafy-salad diet), re-check INR in 3–5 days and adjust dose early rather than waiting for the next routine visit.

2. Measure the metabolic clearance rate, not the food item  
   • The key enzymes are CYP2C9 (S-warfarin, 7-hydroxy pathway) and, to a lesser extent, CYP1A2/3A4 (R-warfarin). Foods or supplements that induce (cruciferous vegetables, charcoal-grilled meat, St John’s wort) or inhibit (cranberry juice >1 L, pomegranate, mango, grapefruit in large amounts, green tea polyphenols) these isoenzymes change clearance.  
   • Instead of trying to memorise every food, use a simple rule: any new dietary habit that lasts ≥3 consecutive days is treated like a new drug—check an INR 3–5 days after the change and adjust the weekly dose by 5–15 % accordingly.

3. Dose in weekly, not daily, increments  
   • Because the terminal half-life of rac-warfarin is 20–60 h and the food effect is usually delayed 24–48 h, weekly dose titration (e.g., 5 % change divided over the week) smooths out minor fluctuations without over-correcting.  
   • Example: INR 1.7 on Thursday, no bleeding, no missed doses—raise the weekly total by 10 % (e.g., from 28 mg to 31 mg) and redistribute the tablets across the week; recheck INR in 5–7 days.

4. Use “INR velocity” as a safety check  
   • A change of ≥0.5 INR unit within one week (or ≥1 unit within two weeks) is assumed to be a clearance change until proven otherwise.  
   • If the rise is unexplained, hold 1–2 doses and repeat INR in 48 h; if the fall is unexplained, give 1–2 extra 1–2 mg tablets and repeat INR in 48 h—then re-titrate the weekly dose.

5. Document the stable pattern  
   • Once two consecutive INRs are within range on the same weekly dose and the patient’s diet diary shows consistent vitamin-K intake, that dose is recorded as the “maintenance clearance dose” for that specific patient.  
   • Any later deviation triggers the same 3-day/5-day INR rule rather than a wholesale reassessment.

By treating the diet as a controlled variable and the weekly warfarin dose as the dependent variable, clinicians compensate for inter-individual clearance differences (CYP2C9 *2/*3, albumin level, age, amiodarone, etc.) without having to quantify every food–drug interaction in advance.
