Relativistic time dilation does not *undermine* cross-cultural research on time cognition, but it does *complicate* the interpretation of any claim that a culture has discovered a more “accurate” or “universal” model of time.  
The complication is not empirical (no ethnography has ever measured μ-second differences) but conceptual: once Einstein has shown that the rate of flow of time is frame-dependent, any statement that “time is constant” or “time is cyclical” can no longer be treated as a falsifiable description of physical reality; it must be re-described as a *cultural representation* of what time *feels* like inside a particular linguistic and technological niche.  

1. Physical vs. experienced time  
   Cross-cultural questionnaires (Block & colleagues, 1996) find that Japanese undergraduates are more willing than Malawian or U.S. peers to endorse “Einsteinian” statements such as “the rate of passing of time depends on the observer.” Yet the same Japanese respondents also say that a clock does *not* measure “personal” time and that temporal experience is strongly shaped by attention and emotion. In other words, they treat relativistic physics as a *cultural metaphor* rather than as a laboratory datum. The anthropologist must therefore separate three layers:  
   - τ_phys – the coordinate time of physics, which is dilated by velocity or gravity;  
   - τ_clock – the public time of calendars, clocks, and train schedules;  
   - τ_exper – the private time of memory, boredom, narrative, and aging.  
   Relativity directly governs only the first layer; cultural research governs the second and third.

2. Linguistic relativity is not gravitational relativity  
   The fact that Aymara speakers gesture to the *back* to indicate the future, or that Swedish bilinguals shift temporal distance judgments when they switch language mode, is a phenomenon of τ_exper. It is unaffected by the 0.6 μs per year that GPS satellites lose at orbital velocity. The two relativities (Einsteinian and Whorfian) operate on orthogonal scales: 10⁻⁶ seconds vs. 10¹ seconds of discourse time.

3. Reflexive danger  
   The only real threat is rhetorical: if a Western researcher announces that “Einstein proved that time is relative, therefore the Hopi circular metaphor is scientifically superior to the Newtonian linear metaphor,” she commits the same category error as the 19th-century evolutionist who ranked calendars by their closeness to Greenwich mean time. Relativity implies that *no* culturally available metaphor can be physically privileged; it therefore *protects* the autonomy of ethnographic description rather than overruling it.

4. Practical rule for fieldworkers  
   - When participants talk about “fast” or “slow” time, code the utterance as τ_exper.  
   - When they invoke clocks, trains, or smartphones, code it as τ_clock.  
   - When they mention satellites, GPS, or particle accelerators, code it as τ_phys and remember that the associated dilation is orders of magnitude below perceptual threshold.  
   Never use τ_phys to explain away τ_exper; use it only to block the opposite mistake—claiming that one folk model is closer to “what time really is.”

Conclusion  
Relativistic time dilation does not challenge the *data* of cross-cultural time cognition; it challenges the *meta-narrative* that any culture’s ontology of time is closer to the physical truth. Once that ontological claim is abandoned, Einstein and Sapir-Whorf can peacefully coexist: one describes the transformation equations between inertial frames, the other describes the metaphor systems by which humans *symbolize* those frames to themselves.
