Oral history transmission acts as a living hinge between what a culture knows to be true about itself and what a visitor is able (and willing) to grasp.  It does not “solve” the authenticity gap, but it re-positions it: instead of a binary (real vs. staged) it offers a negotiated continuum in which the visitor is invited to listen, question and co-produce meaning.  The bridge is built in five inter-locking moves:

1. Temporal re-anchoring  
   Spoken memory collapses the museum-case distance that MacCannell calls “staged back regions.”  A grandmother explaining how she once dyed indigo in the same courtyard where the tourist stands converts “back then” into “right now,” forcing the guest to recognise that the culture is still being lived, not embalmed.

2. Dialogic authentication  
   Because every performance is potentially interrupted by a question—“Why did you sing that line twice?”—the story’s legitimacy is tested in real time.  The teller’s ability to answer, digress, or admit ignorance is itself the proof of authenticity; no third-party curator is required.  The tourist becomes a witness rather than a consumer.

3. Indexical bilingualism  
   Good oral interpreters instinctually code-switch between emic vocabulary (the local term, the untranslatable joke) and tourist-facing metalanguage (“we call this … which literally means …”).  The moment of untranslatability—left hanging, then partially unpacked—signals that something genuinely foreign exists, but also that the guest is being trusted with it.

4. Embodied synecdoche  
   A single voice, accent, or gesture (the crack in the voice when recounting eviction from ancestral land) stands for the whole group.  The tourist cannot meet “all Balinese farmers,” but can remember one farmer’s cracked voice.  That pars pro toto shortcut preserves complexity without overwhelming the visitor; authenticity is felt in the nervous system, not processed as information.

5. Iterative covenant  
   Most communities now make visitors promise to retell one detail correctly—“When you go home, say we still weave on back-strap looms, not on machines.”  The tourist leaves carrying a narrative obligation; the story’s future accuracy becomes partially their responsibility.  This flips the commodification script: the guest is now a vector of cultural continuity rather than a vector of erosion.

The result is not perfect transparency; it is a manageable misunderstanding.  Oral history does not deliver the culture “intact,” but it keeps the negotiation alive after the souvenir has broken and the Instagram story has expired.
