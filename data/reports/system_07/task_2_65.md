Laser photocoagulation is still the benchmark against which every new therapy for diabetic retinopathy (DR) is measured.  
The trick is to use it only when the danger of letting the disease run its course clearly outweighs the well-documented cost of destroying retinal tissue.  
The following thresholds, taken from the DRS, ETDRS, DRCR.net and the most recent international DR guidelines, are the ones that best preserve the visual field while still preventing blindness.

1. Absolute (“treat now”) indications – risk of severe vision-loss within 12-24 mo is ≥25%  
   - High-risk proliferative DR (HR-PDR)  
     – any neovascularisation of the disc (NVD) ≥ ¼ disc area, or  
     – NVD with vitreous/preretinal haemorrhage, or  
     – neovascularisation elsewhere (NVE) ≥ ½ disc area with haemorrhage.  
   - Active fibrovascular proliferation with traction or recurrent vitreous haemorrhage.  
   - Neovascularisation of the iris (rubeosis) or angle.  
   → Perform prompt pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP); the field loss produced is still smaller than the loss produced by untreated PDR.

2. Strong (“treat within 4-6 wk”) indications – 5-yr risk of progression to HR-PDR ≈ 40-60%  
   - Very severe NPDR (“4-2-1” rule):  
     – ≥4 quadrants of deep haemorrhages, or  
     – ≥2 quadrants of venous beading, or  
     – ≥1 quadrant of IRMA.  
   - Early PDR without HR features in a reliable patient who cannot be reviewed every 6-8 wk.  
   → PRP or deferred PRP plus anti-VEGF if centre-involved DME is present; start with lighter, spaced burns (≤800 burns/session, 250-350 µm, 0.1-0.15 s) to keep field loss <15%.

3. Conditional (“consider or combine”) indications – progression risk ≈15-25%, field loss matters  
   - Severe NPDR in the better-seeing eye of a patient with poor metabolic control or poor follow-up.  
   - PDR in a one-eyed patient or in a patient already with dense cataract or advanced glaucoma.  
   → Either ranibizumab/aflibercept monotherapy with deferred light PRP, or half-dose PRP plus anti-VEGF; aim to stop new vessels rather than ablate the periphery completely.

4. Situations where PRP is withheld or radically modified – risk of field loss outweighs benefit  
   - Mild/moderate NPDR without high-risk systemic markers (HbA1c <7%, good BP, no CKD).  
   - Centre-involved DME without PDR – treat with focal/grid laser or anti-VEGF first; PRP only if PDR develops.  
   - Extensive laser scars or advanced glaucoma already present – use targeted laser to ischaemic retina only, or switch to anti-VEGF.

Key practical points that minimise field loss  
- Use spaced, lighter burns (200-300 µm, 0.1 s, medium-white) rather than the old “confluent grey” technique.  
- Keep total burn count <1500-2000; finish only until neovascular regression is achieved.  
- Prefer short-pulse or sub-threshold micropulse lasers when available (no visible scar).  
- Treat over 2-3 sessions separated by 1-2 weeks to avoid macular oedema and excessive field loss.  
- Always combine with anti-VEGF if centre-involved DME is present; this allows 20-30% fewer PRP spots.  
- Document Goldmann or automated perimetry at baseline and 3-6 mo post-treatment; stop further PRP once new vessels have regressed or field loss approaches 20%.

Bottom line  
Laser is applied when the eye reaches the stage at which the 5-year risk of severe visual loss is ≥10-times higher than the risk of clinically significant field loss from the laser itself (roughly HR-PDR or very severe NPDR). Below that threshold the balance tips the other way, and either observation with tight systemic control or anti-VEGF-based therapy is the safer strategy for the patient’s visual field.
