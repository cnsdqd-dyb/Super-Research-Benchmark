Online reviews and past memories are integrated into destination decision-making through a dual cognitive-affective pathway that blends external social proof with internal autobiographical recall. Their influence is not additive but **interactively filtered**: each source is used to validate, complement, or veto the other, producing a final choice that is both socially credible and emotionally congruent.

1. Online reviews as “external memory proxies”  
   - Narrative transportation: Story-like reviews on TripAdvisor, Google, etc. allow prospective travellers to run a mental simulation of the trip. The richer the sensory and emotional detail, the stronger the vicarious experience, which is then stored in working memory almost as if it were a lived episode.  
   - Perceived diagnosticity: Reviews are mined for attributes that the person has not personally experienced (visa hassle, Wi-Fi speed, safety after dark). These attributes are weighted **more heavily** precisely when autobiographical memory lacks them.  
   - Sentiment as risk filter: A sharp drop in recent sentiment or a cluster of negative health/safety comments acts as a “stop signal,” overriding positive personal memories (“I loved Cairo in 2019, but four recent reviews mention stomach bugs—skip it with the kids”).

2. Past memories as “internal review set”  
   - Memorability hierarchy: Only experiences that were (a) emotionally intense, (b) novel, and (c) retrieved repeatedly become long-term “anchor memories.” These anchors act as a default option set; new destinations must beat the remembered hedonic score of the anchor or offer a compelling offset (e.g., novelty, lower cost).  
   - Nostalgia loop: Positive anchor memories trigger nostalgia, which increases psychological comfort and reduces perceived risk for the **same** destination, but can also **generalise** to similar typologies (e.g., “small Greek fishing village” nostalgia makes one consider a Croatian island never visited).  
   - Memory-protection heuristic (Zauberman): People avoid choices that might corrupt an especially precious memory (“I won’t go back to that untouched reef now that it’s on Instagram; it could ruin my 2008 memory”).

3. Integration rules observed in behaviour  
   - Confirmatory matching: When online reviews describe scenes that overlap with personal anchor memories (“the night market still smells of grilled corn and jasmine”), the destination is fast-tracked; the external narrative **corroborates** the internal one.  
   - Compensatory contrasting: If memories are mediocre but recent reviews report dramatic improvements (new eco-lodge, cleaned-up beach), the traveller may **discount** the past and accept the risk of retrying.  
   - Veto override: Even glowing personal memories can be vetoed when online evidence signals objective change (natural disaster, political unrest, price gouging).  
   - Emotional averaging: When no strong memories exist (first-time long-haul trip), the valence of the top five retrieved reviews is averaged and treated as a surrogate “memory slope”; the destination must cross a positive threshold comparable to the person’s domestic anchor.

4. Temporal layering effect  
   - Recency window: Reviews dated within the traveller’s **autobiographical refresh cycle** (usually last 12 months) are given more weight because they update the memory trace.  
   - Memory decay curve: For destinations visited >3 years ago, the affective imprint remains but factual details fade; hence recent UGC is used to **patch** the decayed cognitive map while the emotional colour is supplied by the lingering memory.

5. Managerial implication  
   Destinations should therefore pursue a **“memory-review resonance”** strategy:  
   - Curate review prompts that elicit sensory-rich, story-style content (“Describe the scent in the early-morning souk”)—it maximises narrative transportation and raises the chance of overlap with future travellers’ anchor memories.  
   - Monitor sentiment dips that coincide with high-nostalgia seasons (e.g., graduation months when 20-somethings revisit gap-year memories); a single negative spike can veto thousands of latent revisits.  
   - Offer “memory-reboot” packages that explicitly showcase what has **improved** since the visitor’s last trip, directly targeting the compensatory contrasting heuristic.

In short, the traveller’s mind treats online reviews as **updatable extensions of personal memory**. The two sources are merged in a continuous loop: memories narrow the consideration set, reviews audit it; reviews supply missing attributes, memories colour them with affect; the final choice emerges only when both cognitive coherence and emotional comfort are simultaneously satisfied.
