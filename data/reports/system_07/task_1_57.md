The tension is managed by turning it into a design problem rather than an either-or choice.  
In practice, creators build a “contract of flexibility” that is written into the text itself:

1. Narrative spine + elastic joints  
   A non-negotiable spine (canon characters, world rules, emotional arc) is surrounded by deliberately loose joints—scenes, side quests, or camera angles that can be bent or re-shot without breaking the whole.  
   Example: In Black Mirror: Bandersnatch the core fate of Stefan is fixed, but the order of deaths and the tone of endings are elastic; every branch returns to a checkpoint on the spine so the plot never forks into unfinishable chaos.

2. Audience agency is given a “budget”  
   Platforms quantify how much change each user action is allowed to spend.  
   – Twitch extension polls can only swap the color palette of the next scene or choose which NPC dies, never rewrite the season finale.  
   – AI-generated heritage tours let visitors pick the order of rooms, but the algorithm refills the narrative buffer every 30 seconds so the guiding theme (e.g., “Silk-Road syncretism”) is never lost.

3. Real-time “coherence janitor”  
   A background layer—sometimes human show-runners, sometimes a rules engine—watches incoming audience input and silently discards or rewrites contributions that would violate the spine.  
   In live RPG shows such as Critical Role, the DM acts as janitor: chat donations can name a tavern, but cannot resurrect a villain whose death was scripted to trigger next week’s arc.

4. Layered canonicity  
   What happens in the participatory layer is declared “myth-adjacent.” Only objects that survive a curatorial gate (next episode, official wiki, or a developer patch) become canon.  
   Fortnite’s live events let 12 million people shoot at Galactus, but the comic released the following week canonizes the exact hit-points and dialogue, overwriting any inconsistency that emerged during the chaos.

5. Emotional continuity > factual continuity  
   Creators privilege emotional beats over micro-details. If the audience votes to spare a character, the writers kill a different character to land the required tragic beat, keeping the dramatic temperature intact even while the factual timeline shifts.

6. Post-hoc smoothing  
   After heavy participation, a “director’s cut” or seasonal patch quietly retcons continuity errors.  
   Destiny 2’s seasonal quests often arrive buggy; the lore team then releases grimoire cards that retroactively explain why player actions that seemed contradictory were actually part of a larger scheme.

7. Transparency as safety valve  
   When the janitor intervenes, the platform signals it (“Story-guide is adjusting the timeline…”). This meta-disclosure prevents the audience from feeling their choices were meaningless while still protecting narrative integrity.

The result is a pulsing system: rigid enough to remain a story, porous enough to feel like a conversation.
