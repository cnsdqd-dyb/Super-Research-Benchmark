In digital animation, “rhythm design” is the invisible score that underlies every motion.  
It is built from two inseparable raw materials:

1. Timing – how many frames an action lasts (the musical “beats”).  
2. Spacing – where the object is drawn in each of those frames (the “notes” inside the beat).

Physical realism and artistic expressiveness are not two dials you turn up or down; they are two ears listening to the same pulse.  
The balance is achieved by letting each ear veto the other at the right moment.

-------------------------------------------------
1.  Start with physics, then ask “what is the feeling?”
-------------------------------------------------
• Weight, mass, momentum and material are translated into frame-counts and spacing charts first (realism ear).  
• The same chart is immediately re-read for emotional colour: does the character hesitate, swagger, snap, float?  
  – A 12-frame heavy sack drop can be left exactly as gravity demands,  
  – but if the sack is “shy” you add 3 frames of micro-hesitation at the top; the drop is still physically correct, yet the spacing is now “stuttering”, not linear.  
  Physics is not violated, but psychology is inserted.

-------------------------------------------------
2.  Use the “70-30 rule” in spline editing
-------------------------------------------------
• In 3D software, block your keys so that 70 % of the curve shape obeys natural acceleration (realism).  
• The remaining 30 % is deliberate distortion: you overshoot one key, compress the next, or create a 1-frame “kick” before a big move.  
  Viewers subconsciously recognise the 70 % as “possible”, but the 30 % is what they remember as style.

-------------------------------------------------
3.  Let the camera negotiate the compromise
-------------------------------------------------
• Fast, stylised spacing that would look “wrong” in full-figure is often acceptable in silhouette or when motion-blur is present.  
• Conversely, close-up facial animation is kept closer to real-time timing because the brain has a built-in tape-measure for lip speed and eye blinks.

-------------------------------------------------
4.  Employ layered rhythm tracks
-------------------------------------------------
Primary action (body), secondary (cloth, hair), tertiary (micro-blinks, UI sparks) are timed on three different but locked tempo grids.  
- Primary: 24 fps real-world cadence.  
- Secondary: 1-frame offset, 10-15 % faster or slower to avoid “marionette” synchrony.  
- Tertiary: often on double-frame or 12 fps to give a hand-drawn flicker that reads as “art” rather than “simulation”.

-------------------------------------------------
5.  Build a two-column timing chart
-------------------------------------------------
On the left: the “truth” column – frames needed for real physics.  
On the right: the “poetry” column – frames added or removed for character.  
Animate the difference, not the motion.  
When the two columns are slid against each other, the gap that appears is where the audience feels the life.

-------------------------------------------------
6.  Test with the eyelid test
-------------------------------------------------
Close your eyes and watch the motion blurred.  
If you can still tell the weight and still sense the intent, the balance is right; if either disappears, one ear is deaf.

-------------------------------------------------
Bottom line
-------------------------------------------------
Realism gives the audience permission to believe;  
expressiveness gives them a reason to care.  
Rhythm design keeps them from noticing the difference.
