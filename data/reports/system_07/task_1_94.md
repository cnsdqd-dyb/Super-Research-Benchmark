Cultural preferences and transport convenience are two of the most powerful filters that decide *where* the peak-season wave actually breaks.  
They do not create the wave – climate, school holidays and marketing do that – but they decide which part of the shore it hits hardest.

1. Cultural preferences: the “invisible itinerary”  
   • National icons first, periphery later.  
     – US and Chinese travellers in Tuscany cluster in Florence (“Florence Visitors” segment); Germans and French disperse to the coast and secondary cities (Explorers/Coast Lovers).  
     – Result: Florence’s museums and Uffizi queues grow 3–4× in July–August, while inland wineries still have tables.  
   • Event-driven culture calendars.  
     – Fall-foliage culture in Korea concentrates 58 % of annual SNP visitors into September–October; hot-spring culture keeps Japanese visiting the same valley in winter.  
     – Catholic countries generate two domestic spikes: Holy Week and Christmas; Buddhist countries add Songkran or Vesak.  
   • Risk and comfort culture.  
     – Anglo-Saxon families treat the “big summer trip” as a once-a-year investment, so they pay for direct flights + hotel packages even when prices surge; Mediterranean families take shorter, more frequent breaks, so they can wait until September and still satisfy the “summer holiday” norm.  
   • Language comfort zones.  
     – Chinese FITs (free-independent travellers) stay inside the Mandarin-speaking service bubble (Florence–Pisa–The Mall outlet); hence the coach parking lots in Tuscany are full of Chinese-plated vehicles in August, while German vans head to Maremma campsites.

2. Transport convenience: the “physical funnel”  
   • Door-to-icon speed matters more than distance.  
     – Barcelona’s cable-car + Bus Turístic loop moves 48 % of peak-period visitors inside a 5 km radius; without that loop the old-town Ramblas would need an extra 120 000 km of walking per day.  
     – Bangkok BTS/MRT lines funnel every second foreign visitor to Siam–Chatuchak–Silom; during Songkran the system records 25 % above-capacity loads between 11:00–14:00, precisely when guidebooks say “take the Skytrain to avoid water-fight traffic”.  
   • Seat capacity sets the ceiling.  
     – One daily 600-seat high-speed train from Florence to Salerno converts 180 000 German “Coast Lovers” from car to rail every August; without that train the A1 motorway would need two extra lanes for the same month.  
     – Cruise ships deliver 30 000 passengers in a six-hour window in Barcelona; the port shuttle metro line (L3) peaks at 8 000 pphpd (passengers per hour per direction) – the equivalent of adding a 50 000-inhabitant suburb for one day.  
   • Micro-accessibility beats macro-accessibility.  
     – In Seoul’s fall-foliage season, the subway reaches the park gate, but the final 2 km trail-head shuttle bus is the bottleneck; GPS data show visitor density drops 60 % beyond the last bus stop.  
     – In European cities, the difference between a 3-min walk from a metro exit and a 12-min walk can shift 20 % of peak-day flows away from lesser-known museums.

3. Interaction effect: when culture meets convenience  
   Convenience can *amplify* cultural preference (easy rail + German camping culture = full Baltic coast in August) or *override* it (new low-cost air route turns a non-iconic city into a stag-party hub overnight).  
   Conversely, when culture is strong enough it will *create* convenience: the Korean national park service runs 40 extra weekend buses in October solely for fall-foliage viewers; Tuscany’s wine roads add English signage every May because US tourists keep getting lost on the way to San Gimignano.

4. Practical mapping for destination managers  
   a. Plot the cultural segments by nationality/month (use mobile-GPS or card-transaction clusters).  
   b. Overlay the transport capacity layer (seat-km per hour to each attraction).  
   c. The cells where both values spike are your “red-zone” micro-destinations; the cells where culture is high but convenience low are your “distribute-or-lose” opportunities (shuttle, timed tickets, pricing).

Bottom line  
Peak-season crowding is not just a calendar problem; it is a *matching* problem between what different cultures insist on seeing and how fast each transport mode can deliver them. Adjust either side of the equation – shift cultural demand with storytelling or add 200 seats an hour – and the heat-map changes within one season.
