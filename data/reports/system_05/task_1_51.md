**Introduction:**
The concept of Nash equilibrium, introduced by John Nash [1], has been widely used in game theory to study the behavior of rational agents in strategic situations. In the context of judicial systems, the Nash equilibrium can be used to analyze the behavior of judges and the stability of their reputation vectors. A reputation vector represents the reputation of a judge or a judicial system, and its stability is crucial for maintaining public trust and confidence in the judiciary.

**Theoretical Framework:**
We model the behavior of judges as a game, where each judge chooses a strategy to maximize their payoff, which is a function of their reputation vector. The Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies, one for each judge, such that no judge can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their strategy, assuming all other judges keep their strategies unchanged. We use the following notation:

* $N$ = number of judges
* $S$ = set of possible strategies for each judge
* $R$ = set of possible reputation vectors
* $u_i(s, r)$ = payoff function for judge $i$, where $s \in S$ is the strategy and $r \in R$ is the reputation vector
* $\pi(s, r)$ = probability of a judge choosing strategy $s$ given reputation vector $r$

The Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies $s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_N^*)$ such that:

$$u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*, r) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*, r) \quad \forall i, s_i \in S$$

where $s_{-i}^*$ is the strategy profile of all judges except judge $i$.
