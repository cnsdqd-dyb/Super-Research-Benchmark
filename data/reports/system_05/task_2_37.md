**In Space Station Life Support, What Air Circulation Filtration Technology Provides the Best Trade-off between Microbial Contamination Control and Energy Consumption?**

**Abstract**

The International Space Station (ISS) hosts a diverse group of microbial organisms, and the changing astronaut crews have provided changes to the existing microorganism communities. The air circulation filtration technology used in the ISS must balance microbial contamination control and energy consumption. This report reviews the current state of air filtration technologies used in the ISS and other space missions, and identifies the best trade-off between microbial contamination control and energy consumption.

**Introduction**

The ISS is a unique environment with a closed-loop life support system, where air, water, and waste are recycled and reused. The air circulation system is critical to maintaining a healthy environment for the astronauts, and filtration technology plays a key role in controlling microbial contamination. However, filtration technology also consumes energy, which is limited in space missions. Therefore, it is essential to find a balance between microbial contamination control and energy consumption.

**Current State of Air Filtration Technologies**

Several air filtration technologies are used in the ISS, including High Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filters, Scroll Media Filters, and Multifiltration Beds. HEPA filters are widely used in the ISS due to their high efficiency (99.97% or higher) in removing particles as small as 0.3 µm [1]. However, they have some disadvantages, such as high pressure drop and large volume requirements [2]. Scroll Media Filters have been proposed as an alternative to HEPA filters, offering lower pressure drop and smaller volume requirements [3]. Multifiltration Beds are also used in the ISS, which consist of multiple layers of filters with different pore sizes to remove a wide range of particles and contaminants [4].

**Trade-off between Microbial Contamination Control and Energy Consumption**

The trade-off between microbial contamination control and energy consumption is critical in selecting air filtration technology for the ISS. A study on the ISS air filtration system found that the HEPA filter is the most effective in removing particles and microorganisms, but it also consumes the most energy [5]. Another study found that the Scroll Media Filter can achieve similar efficiency to HEPA filters with lower energy consumption [6]. The Multifiltration Bed has been shown to be effective in removing a wide range of particles and contaminants, but its energy consumption is higher than the Scroll Media Filter [7].

**Mathematical Modeling**

To evaluate the trade-off between microbial contamination control and energy consumption, a mathematical model can be used. The model can be based on the following equation:

$$\eta = \frac{Q}{P} \times \frac{1}{\Delta p}$$

where $\eta$ is the efficiency of the filter, $Q$ is the flow rate, $P$ is the power consumption, and $\Delta p$ is the pressure drop across the filter [8].

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, the best trade-off between microbial contamination control and energy consumption in air circulation filtration technology for the ISS is achieved by the Scroll Media Filter. This filter offers high efficiency in removing particles and microorganisms, while consuming less energy than the HEPA filter. The Multifiltration Bed is also effective, but its energy consumption is higher than the Scroll Media Filter. Further research is needed to optimize the design of air filtration systems for the ISS and other space missions.
