**Title:** Balancing the Benefits of Timely Treatment with the Harm of False Positives in Early Alzheimer's Diagnosis using Amyloid Biomarkers and Cognitive Tests

**Abstract:**
Early diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (AD) is crucial for timely treatment and slowing disease progression. The introduction of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) has shifted the treatment paradigm from symptomatic management to potential disease modification. However, the diagnosis of AD is challenging, and the use of amyloid biomarkers and cognitive tests raises concerns about false positives. This report reviews the current state of AD diagnosis, the role of amyloid biomarkers and cognitive tests, and the potential benefits and harms of early diagnosis.

**Introduction:**
Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive decline and functional impairment. The disease is estimated to affect over 50 million people worldwide, with numbers expected to triple by 2050 [1]. Early diagnosis is essential for timely treatment and slowing disease progression. The introduction of DMTs, such as lecanemab and donanemab, has shifted the treatment paradigm from symptomatic management to potential disease modification [2].

**Amyloid Biomarkers:**
Amyloid biomarkers, such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau proteins, are increasingly validated as reliable markers for detecting amyloid plaque deposition and tau pathology [3]. The plasma Aβ42/40 ratio is a strong predictor of amyloid plaque burden and can help confirm a diagnosis of AD earlier than clinical assessments alone [4]. However, the use of amyloid biomarkers raises concerns about false positives, which can lead to unnecessary anxiety, invasive testing, and treatment [5].

**Cognitive Tests:**
Cognitive tests, such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), are widely used to assess cognitive function in AD [6]. However, these tests have limitations, including low sensitivity and specificity, and may not capture the subtle differences in disease pathology [7]. Advanced cognitive tests, such as digital cognitive tests, have shown promise in identifying objective cognitive impairment with high accuracy [8].

**Combining Amyloid Biomarkers and Cognitive Tests:**
Combining amyloid biomarkers and cognitive tests may offer a more accurate and cost-effective approach to AD diagnosis [9]. A study published in Nature Medicine found that combining digital cognitive tests and blood biomarkers, such as plasma p-tau217, resulted in 90% accuracy in identifying clinical AD, outperforming the current standard-of-care (70% accuracy) and using blood biomarkers alone (80% accuracy) [10].

**Benefits and Harms of Early Diagnosis:**
Early diagnosis of AD can have several benefits, including timely treatment, slowing disease progression, and improved patient outcomes [11]. However, early diagnosis can also have harms, including false positives, unnecessary anxiety, and invasive testing [12]. A study published in the Journal of Preventive Alzheimer's Disease found that using blood biomarkers in primary health care can effectively reduce false positive and false negative amyloid abnormality and avoid costs associated with disease progression [13].

**Conclusion:**
In conclusion, the use of amyloid biomarkers and cognitive tests in AD diagnosis raises concerns about false positives and unnecessary harm. However, combining these tests may offer a more accurate and cost-effective approach to diagnosis. Early diagnosis can have several benefits, including timely treatment and slowing disease progression. However, it is essential to weigh these benefits against the potential harms of false positives and unnecessary anxiety. Further research is needed to establish reliable and generalizable cut-off points for blood biomarkers and to validate the results in more diverse populations.
