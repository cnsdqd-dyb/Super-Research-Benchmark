**Do Kinetic Control Steps in Extraction Models Affect the Process Economics of Rare Earth Separation from Waste?**

**Abstract**

Rare Earth Elements (REEs) are crucial materials for various technologies, including renewable energy and electronics. However, their extraction from primary sources poses significant environmental and health concerns. This report investigates the impact of kinetic control steps in extraction models on the process economics of REE separation from waste. We review existing literature on REE extraction from various waste sources, including coal ash, electronic waste, and mine tailings. Our analysis reveals that kinetic control steps, such as leaching, adsorption, and solvent extraction, significantly affect the process economics of REE separation. We discuss the advantages and limitations of different extraction methods and highlight the need for sustainable and efficient technologies to recover REEs from waste.

**Introduction**

REEs are a group of 17 elements with unique properties, making them essential for various applications, including renewable energy technologies, electronics, and advanced ceramics [1]. As a result, there is a growing interest in recovering REEs from secondary sources, such as waste materials.

**Kinetic Control Steps in Extraction Models**

Kinetic control steps, such as leaching, adsorption, and solvent extraction, play a crucial role in REE extraction from waste. Leaching is a process that involves the use of a solvent to extract REEs from the waste material. The kinetics of leaching can be described by the following equation:

$$\frac{dC}{dt} = k \cdot C \cdot (1 - \frac{C}{C_{max}})$$

where $C$ is the concentration of REEs, $k$ is the leaching rate constant, and $C_{max}$ is the maximum concentration of REEs that can be extracted [3].

Adsorption is another important kinetic control step in REE extraction. Adsorbents, such as activated carbon, functionalized silica, and zirconium phosphate platelets, can be used to bind REEs from the leachate [4]. The adsorption kinetics can be described by the following equation:

$$\frac{dC}{dt} = k \cdot C \cdot (1 - \frac{C}{C_{max}})$$

where $C$ is the concentration of REEs, $k$ is the adsorption rate constant, and $C_{max}$ is the maximum concentration of REEs that can be adsorbed [5].

Solvent extraction is a widely used method for REE separation and purification. The kinetics of solvent extraction can be described by the following equation:

$$\frac{dC}{dt} = k \cdot C \cdot (1 - \frac{C}{C_{max}})$$

where $C$ is the concentration of REEs, $k$ is the extraction rate constant, and $C_{max}$ is the maximum concentration of REEs that can be extracted [6].

**Process Economics of REE Separation**

The process economics of REE separation from waste are significantly affected by the kinetic control steps. The cost of REE extraction and separation can be estimated using the following equation:

$$Cost = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (C_i \cdot P_i)$$

where $C_i$ is the cost of each step, $P_i$ is the price of each REE, and $n$ is the number of steps involved in the extraction and separation process [7].

**Conclusion**
