## Introduction

The stress-strain relationship in ice flow models is a critical component in predicting sea level rise, as it determines the flow of ice sheets and glaciers. Basal sliding, which occurs when the ice sheet or glacier slides over its bed, plays a significant role in this process. This report examines the relationship between the stress-strain relationship in ice flow models and basal sliding changes, and how it constrains sea level rise predictions.

## Background

Ice flow models, such as the Shallow Ice Approximation (SIA) and the Full Stokes model, are used to simulate the flow of ice sheets and glaciers [1]. These models rely on the stress-strain relationship, which describes the relationship between the stress applied to the ice and its resulting strain rate. The stress-strain relationship is typically represented by a flow law, such as the Glen flow law [2], which relates the strain rate to the stress.

Basal sliding, on the other hand, is a process that occurs when the ice sheet or glacier slides over its bed, resulting in a reduction in friction and an increase in ice flow [3]. The basal sliding law, which describes the relationship between the basal shear stress and the sliding velocity, is a critical component of ice flow models [4].

## The Stress-Strain Relationship and Basal Sliding

The stress-strain relationship in ice flow models is typically represented by a flow law, such as the Glen flow law, which can be expressed as:

$$\dot{\epsilon} = A \sigma^n$$

where $\dot{\epsilon}$ is the strain rate, $A$ is a constant, $\sigma$ is the stress, and $n$ is the stress exponent [2].

Basal sliding, on the other hand, can be represented by a sliding law, such as the Weertman sliding law, which can be expressed as:

$$u_b = C \tau_b^m$$

where $u_b$ is the basal sliding velocity, $C$ is a constant, $\tau_b$ is the basal shear stress, and $m$ is the sliding exponent [4].

The stress-strain relationship and basal sliding are closely linked, as changes in basal sliding can impact the stress-strain relationship, and vice versa. For example, an increase in basal sliding can lead to a reduction in friction, resulting in an increase in ice flow and a change in the stress-strain relationship [5].

The stress-strain relationship and basal sliding have a significant impact on sea level rise predictions.
