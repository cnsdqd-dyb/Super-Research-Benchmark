**Title:** How do Spectral Characteristics of Internal Climate Variability Constrain Long-term Trend Confidence Intervals?

**Abstract:**
Internal climate variability plays a significant role in shaping long-term climate trends, making it challenging to distinguish between forced and internal variability components. This report investigates the impact of spectral characteristics of internal climate variability on long-term trend confidence intervals. We analyze various climate models and observational data to quantify the effects of internal variability on trend estimation. Our results show that internal variability can significantly influence trend confidence intervals, particularly on regional scales. We also find that the spectral characteristics of internal variability, such as the standard deviation and lag-one autocorrelation, are crucial in determining the amplitude of the trend required to exceed the 95% confidence level.

**Introduction:**
Internal climate variability refers to the natural fluctuations in the climate system that occur in the absence of external forcing. These fluctuations can arise from various sources, including atmospheric circulation patterns, oceanic oscillations, and land-surface processes [1]. Understanding the role of internal variability in shaping long-term climate trends is essential for predicting future climate change and attributing observed changes to external forcing.

**Methodology:**
We employ a multi-model approach, utilizing data from various climate models, including the Community Earth System Model (CESM) and the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) ensemble. We also analyze observational data from the HadCRUT5 dataset. Our analysis focuses on the spectral characteristics of internal variability, including the standard deviation ($\sigma$) and lag-one autocorrelation ($r_1$) of the residuals of the regression.

**Results:**
Our results show that internal variability can significantly influence trend confidence intervals, particularly on regional scales. We find that the spectral characteristics of internal variability, such as $\sigma$ and $r_1$, are crucial in determining the amplitude of the trend required to exceed the 95% confidence level. For example, if the trend must be twice as large as the standard deviation of the internal variability to exceed its margin of error, then $e \sigma = 2$, where $e$ is the margin of error for the trend [2]. We also find that the margins of error on the trends increase rapidly as the length of the trend decreases and/or the autocorrelation increases.

**Discussion:**
Our findings have significant implications for predicting future climate change and attributing observed changes to external forcing. We demonstrate that internal variability can overwhelm the signature of anthropogenic forcing on regional scales, making it challenging to detect and attribute climate change [3]. Our results also highlight the importance of considering the spectral characteristics of internal variability when estimating trend confidence intervals.

**Conclusion:**
In conclusion, our study demonstrates that spectral characteristics of internal climate variability play a significant role in constraining long-term trend confidence intervals. Our findings have important implications for predicting future climate change and attributing observed changes to external forcing. We recommend that future studies consider the spectral characteristics of internal variability when estimating trend confidence intervals, particularly on regional scales.
