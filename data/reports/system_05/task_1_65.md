**How do power-law parameters of travel distance evolve with changes in city scale and network topology?**

**Abstract**

The evolution of power-law parameters of travel distance in relation to city scale and network topology is a complex phenomenon that has garnered significant attention in recent years. This report provides an in-depth analysis of the relationship between power-law parameters, city scale, and network topology, with a focus on understanding how changes in these factors impact travel distance. Our research reveals that power-law parameters of travel distance are influenced by city scale, with larger cities exhibiting higher power-law exponents. Additionally, network topology plays a crucial role in shaping travel distance, with more connected and centralized networks leading to shorter travel distances.

**Introduction**

The study of power-law parameters of travel distance is essential for understanding urban mobility and transportation systems. Power laws have been observed in various aspects of urban systems, including city size distributions, transportation networks, and human mobility patterns [1]. The power-law parameter, often denoted as $\alpha$, describes the relationship between the frequency and magnitude of events, such as travel distances. In the context of travel distance, power laws can be used to model the distribution of trip lengths, with $\alpha$ representing the rate at which longer trips become less frequent.

**Methodology**

Our analysis is based on a comprehensive review of existing literature on power-law parameters of travel distance, city scale, and network topology. We examined various studies that investigated the relationship between these factors, including [2], [3], and [4]. **Results**

Our research reveals that power-law parameters of travel distance are influenced by city scale, with larger cities exhibiting higher power-law exponents. This suggests that larger cities have a greater proportion of shorter trips, while smaller cities have a greater proportion of longer trips. We also found that network topology plays a crucial role in shaping travel distance, with more connected and centralized networks leading to shorter travel distances.

The relationship between power-law parameters of travel distance and city scale can be modeled using the following equation:

$$P(d) \propto d^{-\alpha}$$

where $P(d)$ is the probability of a trip of distance $d$, and $\alpha$ is the power-law exponent. Our analysis shows that $\alpha$ increases with city size, indicating that larger cities have a more rapid decline in trip frequency with increasing distance.
