**Title:** Balancing Positive Symptom Control and Minimizing Extrapyramidal Side Effects in Antipsychotic Treatment: A Review of the Current Evidence

**Abstract:**
Antipsychotic medications are a crucial component of treatment for schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders. However, these medications can have significant side effects, including extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) such as parkinsonism, dystonia, and tardive dyskinesia. The goal of antipsychotic treatment is to balance the control of positive symptoms, such as hallucinations and delusions, with the minimization of EPS. This review aims to summarize the current evidence on the relationship between antipsychotic dose and efficacy, as well as the risk of EPS, and to discuss strategies for optimizing antipsychotic treatment.

**Introduction:**
Antipsychotic medications work by blocking dopamine receptors in the brain, which can help to reduce positive symptoms of psychosis. However, this blockade can also lead to EPS, particularly at high doses [1]. The relationship between antipsychotic dose and efficacy is complex, with most antipsychotics having a hyperbolic relationship between dose and efficacy, with a plateau at high doses [2]. In contrast, the dose-response curve for EPS is often linear, with higher doses associated with a greater risk of EPS [3].

**Dose-Response Curves:**
The dose-response curves for antipsychotics can be used to optimize treatment and minimize EPS. For example, aripiprazole has a dose-response curve that shows increasing efficacy up to a dose of 12 mg/d, with little additional benefit at higher doses [4]. Similarly, risperidone has a dose-response curve that shows increasing efficacy up to a dose of 6 mg/d, with higher doses associated with a greater risk of EPS [5]. The dose-response curve for olanzapine is more complex, with higher doses associated with increased efficacy, but also a greater risk of EPS [6].

**Partial Agonists:**
Partial agonists, such as aripiprazole and brexpiprazole, have a lower risk of EPS compared to full antagonists [7]. These medications work by partially activating dopamine receptors, rather than fully blocking them, which can help to reduce the risk of EPS [8]. Cariprazine, a partial agonist with high affinity for D3 receptors, has been shown to have a lower risk of EPS compared to other antipsychotics [9].

**Strategies for Optimizing Treatment:**
Several strategies can be used to optimize antipsychotic treatment and minimize EPS. These include:

1. **Using the minimum effective dose**: Using the lowest effective dose of an antipsychotic can help to minimize the risk of EPS [10].
2. **Choosing an antipsychotic with a lower risk of EPS**: Selecting an antipsychotic with a lower risk of EPS, such as a partial agonist, can help to reduce the risk of these side effects [11].
3. **Monitoring for EPS**: Regular monitoring for EPS can help to identify patients who are at risk of these side effects and allow for prompt intervention [12].
4. **Switching to a different antipsychotic**: If a patient is experiencing EPS, switching to a different antipsychotic with a lower risk of these side effects may be necessary [13].

**Conclusion:**
Antipsychotic treatment is a complex process that requires careful consideration of the balance between positive symptom control and the minimization of EPS. By understanding the dose-response curves for antipsychotics and using strategies such as choosing an antipsychotic with a lower risk of EPS, monitoring for EPS, and switching to a different antipsychotic if necessary, clinicians can optimize treatment and improve patient outcomes.
