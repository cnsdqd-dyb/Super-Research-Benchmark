**In MRI Screening for High-Risk Breast Cancer Populations, What Strategy Balances Detection Sensitivity with the Burden of False Positives and Overdiagnosis?**

**Introduction**

Breast cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide. Early detection is crucial for improving survival rates, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a valuable tool for screening high-risk populations. However, the high sensitivity of MRI can lead to false positives and overdiagnosis, resulting in unnecessary biopsies, patient anxiety, and increased healthcare costs. This report aims to investigate the optimal strategy for balancing detection sensitivity with the burden of false positives and overdiagnosis in MRI screening for high-risk breast cancer populations.

**Background**

Recent studies have shown that abbreviated breast MRI (AB-MRI) protocols can maintain high sensitivity while reducing operational costs and improving patient throughput [1]. AB-MRI has been proposed as a potential solution to increase access to high-sensitivity screening breast MRI, particularly for women with dense breast tissue [2]. However, the optimal strategy for implementing AB-MRI in high-risk populations remains unclear.

**Methods**

A comprehensive review of the literature was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of AB-MRI in detecting breast cancer in high-risk populations. Studies comparing AB-MRI to full-protocol MRI (FP-MRI) and other imaging modalities, such as mammography and digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT), were included. The review focused on the sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy of AB-MRI, as well as its potential to reduce false positives and overdiagnosis.

**Results**

The review revealed that AB-MRI maintains high sensitivity (95.7%) compared to FP-MRI (95.2%) [3], while reducing the examination time and costs. However, AB-MRI has lower specificity (86.7%) compared to FP-MRI (92.0%) [4]. The use of AB-MRI as a supplemental screening modality to mammography or DBT has been shown to improve detection rates and reduce false positives [5]. A cost-effectiveness analysis demonstrated that AB-MRI is a cost-effective strategy for high-risk populations, with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of $23,394 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained [6].

**Discussion**

The results of this review suggest that AB-MRI is a viable strategy for balancing detection sensitivity with the burden of false positives and overdiagnosis in high-risk breast cancer populations. The use of AB-MRI as a supplemental screening modality can improve detection rates while reducing the number of false positives and unnecessary biopsies. However, the optimal implementation of AB-MRI protocols requires careful consideration of patient selection, imaging protocols, and performance metrics to ensure high-quality patient care.

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, AB-MRI is a promising strategy for balancing detection sensitivity with the burden of false positives and overdiagnosis in high-risk breast cancer populations. Further research is needed to standardize AB-MRI protocols and evaluate their effectiveness in diverse populations. The use of AB-MRI as a supplemental screening modality has the potential to improve breast cancer detection rates while reducing healthcare costs and improving patient outcomes.

**Recommendations**

1. AB-MRI should be considered as a supplemental screening modality for high-risk breast cancer populations, particularly those with dense breast tissue.
2. Imaging protocols and patient selection criteria should be standardized to ensure high-quality patient care.
3. Performance metrics, such as sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy, should be monitored to evaluate the effectiveness of AB-MRI protocols.
4. Further research is needed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of AB-MRI in diverse populations and to standardize AB-MRI protocols.

**Mathematical Model**

Let $S$ be the sensitivity of AB-MRI, $P$ be the prevalence of breast cancer, and $C$ be the cost of AB-MRI. The expected number of true positives (TP) can be calculated as:

$$TP = S \times P \times N$$

where $N$ is the number of patients screened. The expected number of false positives (FP) can be calculated as:

$$FP = (1-S) \times (1-P) \times N$$

The cost-effectiveness of AB-MRI can be evaluated using the ICER, which is calculated as:

$$ICER = \frac{C}{QALY}$$

where $QALY$ is the quality-adjusted life year gained.
