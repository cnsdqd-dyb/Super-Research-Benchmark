Network‐flow assignment with **elastic** demand is fundamentally more complex than the standard fixed‐demand case.  Under elastic demand, travel demand (OD trips) is not fixed but itself depends on travel costs, so flows and demands must be solved **simultaneously**.  In practice this means the equilibrium conditions form a joint fixed‐point or variational‐inequality problem in a higher‐dimensional space (flows *and* demands), rather than a simple convex program in flows alone.  For example, one can cast the elastic‐demand user‐equilibrium as a variational inequality in an “extended” space that includes both link/path flows and OD demand variables ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261515001551#:~:text=DUE%20with%20elastic%20demand%20is,in%20an%20extended%20Hilbert%20space)).  Equivalently, the static problem can be formulated as an optimization of (sum of link congestion integrals + consumers’ disutility), which is more complex than the pure flow‐cost minimization of fixed demand ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v42y2008i2p249-260.html#:~:text=The%20traffic%20assignment%20problem%20,large%20instances%20with%20high%20accuracy)).  In short, accommodating elastic demand **adds new variables and nonlinear supply–demand constraints**, effectively making the mathematical model much larger and more intricate.

This added complexity has two major consequences for convergence and computation.  First, **algorithmic complexity** increases: one typically must embed a flow‐assignment solver inside a demand‐update loop (or solve a large complementarity/VI system all at once).  For example, Babonneau and Vial (2008) solve the static elastic‐demand assignment by a specialized cutting‐plane method because standard algorithms (like Frank–Wolfe) no longer apply directly ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v42y2008i2p249-260.html#:~:text=The%20traffic%20assignment%20problem%20,large%20instances%20with%20high%20accuracy)).  In a dynamic setting, Friesz et al. (2015) show that E–DUE is an infinite‐dimensional VI and they propose projection‐ and proximal‐point algorithms with nontrivial convergence proofs ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261515001551#:~:text=This%20paper%20is%20concerned%20with,on%20increasingly%20relaxed%20notions%20of)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261515001551#:~:text=projection%20method%2C%20and%20a%20proximal,convergence%2C%20and%20computational%20efficiency%20are)).  In other words, ensuring that the flow assignment “converges” in the presence of elastic demand requires **more sophisticated solution methods** (fixed‐point iterations, VI solvers, etc.) and often many more iterations, since each update changes both flows and demands.  

Second, the **convergence conditions** become stricter.  Standard user‐equilibrium algorithms for fixed demand often rely on nice properties like strongly monotone link‐costs.  With elastic demand, one must also impose structure on the demand functions.  Recent analysis shows that convergence of projection or fixed‐point schemes for **elastic‐demand** equilibrium is guaranteed only under combined monotonicity conditions: path delays need only be “weakly increasing” (Lipschitz), but **inverse demand functions must be strongly decreasing** with flow to stabilize the inner demand‐flow loop ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261521000163#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%20we%20present,nonmonotone%20path%20delays%20via%20a)).  In effect, the dual supply‐demand system must satisfy global Lipschitz and monotonicity bounds for the algorithms to converge ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261521000163#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%20we%20present,nonmonotone%20path%20delays%20via%20a)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261521000163#:~:text=fixed,weak)).  If those conditions fail, flows and demands could oscillate or diverge. 

In summary, requiring convergence of the flow‐assignment under elastic demand **dramatically increases model complexity**.  The problem transforms from a single‐level convex/travel‐time fixed‐point problem into a higher‐dimensional (often non‐convex) equilibrium system.  One must introduce additional variables for OD demand, add demand‐cost balancing equations, and typically solve a variational inequality or complementarity model ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261515001551#:~:text=DUE%20with%20elastic%20demand%20is,in%20an%20extended%20Hilbert%20space)) ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v42y2008i2p249-260.html#:~:text=The%20traffic%20assignment%20problem%20,large%20instances%20with%20high%20accuracy)).  All of this makes both the *formulation* and *solution process* more complex: models become larger, computational demands grow, and convergence proofs require stronger assumptions on cost and demand functions ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261521000163#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%20we%20present,nonmonotone%20path%20delays%20via%20a)) ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v42y2008i2p249-260.html#:~:text=The%20traffic%20assignment%20problem%20,large%20instances%20with%20high%20accuracy)).  Nonetheless, with suitable algorithms and assumptions, accurate solutions can still be obtained for large networks (as demonstrated by Babonneau and Vial ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v42y2008i2p249-260.html#:~:text=The%20traffic%20assignment%20problem%20,large%20instances%20with%20high%20accuracy)) and Friesz et al.
