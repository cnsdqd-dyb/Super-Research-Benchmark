# Sensory Interaction and Audience Participation in Digital Installations

Interactive digital installations use sensors (motion detectors, cameras, microphones, touchscreens, etc.) to detect visitor actions and generate immediate multimedia responses. In such works, the art environment remains passive until people engage it: movements, gestures or sounds made by viewers become “primary input to a responsive computer system” ([www.isea-symposium-archives.org](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/presentation/audience-participation-and-response-in-movement-sensing-installations-presented-by-winkler/#:~:text=Movement,through%20a%20participant%E2%80%99s%20actions%2C%20interpreted)).  As Todd Winkler observes, these environments are typically “realized through physical action” – the installation only exists via participant input ([www.isea-symposium-archives.org](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/presentation/audience-participation-and-response-in-movement-sensing-installations-presented-by-winkler/#:~:text=determined%20by%20individual%20action%2C%20curiosity%2C,those%20actions%20do%20not%20require)).  Those within the sensor field thus become co-authors of the experience (Winkler calls them “players”), while onlookers outside the sensing zone remain mere “spectators” ([www.isea-symposium-archives.org](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/presentation/audience-participation-and-response-in-movement-sensing-installations-presented-by-winkler/#:~:text=The%20term%20%E2%80%9Caudience%E2%80%9D%20may%20ambiguously,role%20in%20a%20live%20event)). In short, the installation’s sensory hardware and code literally define who is **acting** and who is **observing**. In practice, this means the audience’s very presence is parsed by the system: stepping into the interaction area changes a visitor’s role from viewer to active agent. Because the work responds only when triggered, the piece is effectively co-constructed by its participants.  

## Audience Roles and Interaction Levels  
Critical literature has formalized how participatory roles emerge in interactive art.  For example, Schraffenberger and van der Heide (2015) reference a taxonomy that delineates distinct modes of engagement: **Observational** (passive viewing), **Navigational** (moving through or around the installation), **Participatory** (directly triggering outputs), **Co-authoring** (shaping content or outcomes), and **Intercommunicative** (two-way dialogue with the system) ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277967958_Audience-artwork_interaction#:~:text=taxonomy%20%28details%20in%20Sec,authoring%2C%20and%20intercommunication.)). These categories show how a sensor-driven system creates an ontology of participation by limiting and defining possible actions. For instance, an installation might allow participants to walk around a space (navigational), press buttons or gestures to change visuals (participatory), or even collaborate on generative sounds (co-authoring). The design thus pre-specifies the participation “levels” that the audience can inhabit. In concrete terms, interactive artists often distinguish in-building the viewer’s roles: those detected by sensors as “activators” (players) versus passive observers ([www.isea-symposium-archives.org](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/presentation/audience-participation-and-response-in-movement-sensing-installations-presented-by-winkler/#:~:text=The%20term%20%E2%80%9Caudience%E2%80%9D%20may%20ambiguously,role%20in%20a%20live%20event)).  

- **Players vs Spectators:**  Those whose bodies trigger the sensors become “players” (active participants), whereas those only watching remain “spectators” ([www.isea-symposium-archives.org](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/presentation/audience-participation-and-response-in-movement-sensing-installations-presented-by-winkler/#:~:text=The%20term%20%E2%80%9Caudience%E2%80%9D%20may%20ambiguously,role%20in%20a%20live%20event)). The system’s logic literally enforces this binary role ontology.  
- **Sensory Modes:**  Each mode of interaction corresponds to different senses. One model even includes a *sensory mode* parameter – indicating which human senses are engaged (visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory) ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277967958_Audience-artwork_interaction#:~:text=intercommunication,mode%2C%20indicates%20which%20senses%20of)).  For example, many installations couple sight and sound: a movement might flash lights and emit tones simultaneously.  Others may use touch or proximity sensors to elicit haptic feedback or scent. In all cases, the choice of sensor channels defines the type of participation (e.g. a looming shadow might trigger a visual cascade, whereas a clap might change the soundscape).  

In effect, the installation’s programming maps every possible sensory input to some predefined category of interaction. The audience is thus incorporated into a structured schema: **Which interaction mode** (observing, walking, stepping, touching) **in what sensory channel** leads to what outcome. This built-in mapping is the core of the participation ontology.

## Dialogic Feedback and Embodied Presence  
Digital installations often behave like conversational partners: they “listen” to the audience and “respond” via changing effects.  Wanjiku (2024) describes interactive art devices as those that “respond to a user’s touch and/or voice with an appropriate perceptual event” ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382878501_Interactive_Installations_Engaging_Audiences_through_Participatory_ART#:~:text=be%20engaged%2C%20and%20manipulative%20skills,and%2For%20voice%20with%20an%20appropriate)).  In other words, the system provides immediate positive feedback to the audience’s actions, making the interaction feel like a dialogue. As one theorist puts it, the computer **“listens to the audience (the first half of reactivity), thinks about what it heard (autonomy), and then speaks its thoughts back to the audience (the second half)”** ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277967958_Audience-artwork_interaction#:~:text=and%20reactivity%20that%20they%20achieve,By%20making%20the)). This cyclic exchange of input and output embeds each participant’s action as a “utterance” in the artwork.  

Because of this two-way coupling, the audience’s bodily experience becomes a fundamental part of the art. Giomi (2020) notes that the algorithmically-mediated sensory feedback allows visitors to become aware of themselves as a *“lived body”* through proprioception and movement ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=en#:~:text=that%20is%2C%20the%20specific%20way,body%20through%20proprioception%20and%20movement)).  For example, a motion-capture installation might modulate its virtual environment in step with a participant’s breathing and balance, so the user feels their own body merge with the digital space. In doing so, the participant’s physical presence is not just detected but amplified: the installation reconfigures space and adds depth cues or sounds that highlight one’s own gestures. This heightened embodied awareness means the visitor is no longer a detached spectator but a bodily participant.  

Thus, the system’s design – the choice of sensors and the rules for feedback – shapes how the audience *experiences* the work. Each tactile touch, gesture or step is classified and interpreted by the software, which in turn modifies the setting. This loop of perception and action confers a sense of agency: users learn that **their actions have meaning** in the built environment (lights brighten when they approach, sounds change when they clap, etc.). As Wanjiku puts it, by engaging the audience’s curiosity and skills, interactive installations create a “dialogue of sorts” between visitor and artwork ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382878501_Interactive_Installations_Engaging_Audiences_through_Participatory_ART#:~:text=be%20engaged%2C%20and%20manipulative%20skills,and%2For%20voice%20with%20an%20appropriate)) ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=en#:~:text=that%20is%2C%20the%20specific%20way,body%20through%20proprioception%20and%20movement)). The audience’s sensory actions become tokens in this dialogue — effectively entries in the ontology the installation has defined.

## Constructing a Participation Ontology  
All these factors combine to form an **ontology of audience participation** — a structured set of roles and modes that the installation enforces via its sensory logic. In practical terms, the installation’s hardware and code predefine *which* kinds of audience-being can exist. Whitelaw et al. describe this as generating “concomitant ontologies”: each interactive artwork contains the potential for specific realities that only appear through user-system interaction ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=en#:~:text=an%20interdependence%20of%20the%20recipient%E2%80%99s,interactor%E2%80%99s%20simulation%20and%20direct%20lived)). Only when participants’ sensor-driven actions interact with the installation’s material interface and algorithmic rules do particular experiences occur.  

For example, in Stelarc’s “Fractal Flesh” performance, spectators’ signals (collected via digital electrodes) literally controlled the artist’s bodily movements, creating a bizarre hybrid of performer and audience ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=en#:~:text=and%C2%A0voluntary%20choreography,the%20marks%20of%20physical%20presence)). Here the ontology of participation included remote control agents and a split performer-body.  In more mundane installations, the ontology might include categories like *“active heatmap”* vs *“idle presence,”* or *“solo performer”* vs *“collective contributor.”* The programming defines these classes. Giomi notes that the conditions for a digital artwork’s existence are inseparable from how it mediates presence: it is an **interdependent relationship** of body, sensory feedback and algorithm ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=en#:~:text=an%20interdependence%20of%20the%20recipient%E2%80%99s,interactor%E2%80%99s%20simulation%20and%20direct%20lived)) ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=en#:~:text=that%20is%2C%20the%20specific%20way,body%20through%20proprioception%20and%20movement)). 

In short, sensory interaction in digital installations does not just create an engaging effect; it **builds the very framework of participation**. The sensors and software effectively say “if you move/touch/speak in this way, you are a [player/co-author/etc.]; if you stand aside, you are a spectator.”  Audience members find themselves falling into those predefined categories. This system of categories and interactions *is* the ontology of participation: a formalized map of how viewers can engage. By design, every possible participatory act is recognized and given a place in the artwork’s structure, so the installation’s sensory logic *constructs* the audience’s modes of being within it ([www.isea-symposium-archives.org](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/presentation/audience-participation-and-response-in-movement-sensing-installations-presented-by-winkler/#:~:text=determined%20by%20individual%20action%2C%20curiosity%2C,those%20actions%20do%20not%20require)) ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/estetica/6715?lang=en#:~:text=an%20interdependence%20of%20the%20recipient%E2%80%99s,interactor%E2%80%99s%20simulation%20and%20direct%20lived)).
