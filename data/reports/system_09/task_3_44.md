Optimizing replenishment under a decomposed demand‐covariance structure generally **reduces** the variability of logistics costs.  In effect, the decomposition (for example by eigen‐ or principal‐component analysis) transforms correlated demands into independent factors.  Replenishment policies can then be set on these orthogonal components so that safety stocks and order quantities hedge the dominant sources of demand variability.  This is mathematically equivalent to “pooling” the uncertain demands: correlated demand streams are effectively aggregated, which lowers the total safety stock needed.  In classical risk‐pooling terms, centralizing (or coupling) inventory for correlated products **cuts down on excess stock and risk** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037722170701017X#:~:text=Conventional%20wisdom%20for%20%E2%80%9Cinventory%20pooling%E2%80%9D,a%20way%20to%20dramatically%20cut)). 

In contrast, high positive correlation tends to **increase** cost risk if ignored.  For instance, Zhou et al. found that higher demand variance and higher correlation coefficients drive up total cost in a replenishment model ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716017300532#:~:text=postponement%20and%20logistics%20can%20improve,demand%20can%20increase%20total%20cost)).  By using the decomposed covariance, one can **decouple** the multi‐item inventory equations (e.g. by normalizing Gaussian demands into independent ones) and solve for each item separately ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716017300532#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20presented,normalized%20random%20variables%20are%20uncorrelated)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716017300532#:~:text=To%20demonstrate%20the%20usefulness%20of,solved%20numerically%20without%20any%20approximation)).  The result is a more balanced ordering plan: inventory buffers are aligned along the principal directions of demand fluctuation, rather than over–buffering every item.  

In practical terms, this means the optimized policy absorbs joint demand swings more efficiently.  Safety stocks are not simply the sum of individual variances but reflect the reduced aggregate uncertainty after pooling.  The net effect is a **lower variance in logistics costs**.  Empirical and theoretical studies of risk pooling confirm this intuition: aggregating correlated demand streams reduces the spread in required inventories (and thus cost) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037722170701017X#:~:text=Conventional%20wisdom%20for%20%E2%80%9Cinventory%20pooling%E2%80%9D,a%20way%20to%20dramatically%20cut)).  In short, replenishment optimization that exploits the decomposed demand correlations yields steadier, less volatile total cost than naïve policies that ignore those correlations.
