One key insight from checkpoint‐inhibitor studies is that the PD-1 pathway acts like an “immune rheostat,” tuning T‐cell activation in proportion to self‐antigen affinity.  In practice this means high-affinity (potentially autoreactive) T cells express more PD-1 and are kept in check, whereas lower-affinity tumor‐specific T cells can escape inhibition.  Thus, blocking PD-1 preferentially unleashes anti–tumor T cells while preserving much of peripheral tolerance.  In effect, PD-1’s graded, affinity‐dependent signaling balances potent tumor immunity against autoimmunity ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284868/#:~:text=reactive%20T%20cells%20calibrate%20their,1%20inhibits%20T%20cells)).  (For example, Jiang et al. showed high‐affinity self‐reactive clones upregulate PD-1 to enforce tolerance, so anti–PD-1 therapy restores their effector function with only modest autoimmune activation ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284868/#:~:text=reactive%20T%20cells%20calibrate%20their,1%20inhibits%20T%20cells)).) 

**Answer:** The PD-1 checkpoint functions as a tunable “rheostat” on T cells – its signaling strength scales with antigen affinity – so that blocking PD-1 unleashes tumor‐specific responses while high‐affinity self-reactive clones remain suppressed, balancing anti-tumor activation against autoimmune risk ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284868/#:~:text=reactive%20T%20cells%20calibrate%20their,1%20inhibits%20T%20cells)).
