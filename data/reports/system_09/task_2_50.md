# ESG Factor Models vs Multi-Criteria ESG Portfolios

ESG (“Environmental, Social, Governance”) criteria can be incorporated into portfolio models either by treating ESG as a **risk factor** or by adding ESG metrics as extra objectives/constraints.  A factor-model approach extends traditional risk models (e.g. Fama–French) by including an ESG factor or “sustainability” factor.  For example, recent studies construct zero-investment “ESG-tilt” portfolios or factors and find that high-ESG *minus* low-ESG long–short factors capture systematic return variations ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-024-00382-z#:~:text=be%20financially%20material%2C%20the%20integration,in%20common%20asset%20pricing%20models)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7755284/#:~:text=Pure%20factor%20portfolios%20have%20the,92)).  In other words, an ESG factor can be treated like other style factors (value, momentum, etc.) to quantify the incremental risk of ESG exposure.  This approach quantifies **ESG risk** objectively from market data and can be integrated into standard risk models (e.g. adding an ESG factor to a Fama–French model ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-024-00382-z#:~:text=be%20financially%20material%2C%20the%20integration,in%20common%20asset%20pricing%20models))).  In principle, factor construction can “standardize” responsibility by summarizing disparate ESG signals into one factor loading, providing a uniform ESG exposure measure across stocks.  For instance, Pástor, Stambaugh and Taylor (2021) argue that sustainable companies should earn lower equilibrium returns (reflecting lower ESG risk), so an ESG factor would capture that effect.  In practice, factor-model methods (e.g. pure factor portfolios) explicitly control for conventional risks (market, size, value, etc.) to isolate the *ESG signal* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7755284/#:~:text=Pure%20factor%20portfolios%20have%20the,92)).  This mitigates confounding influences and yields an ESG “risk premium” that can be back-tested or used for portfolio construction.  

However, factor models rely on how the ESG factor is defined (e.g. which ratings or proxies used) and do not eliminate all discretion.  ESG data themselves are often composite scores, and factor definitions may vary.  Moreover, factor models focus on systematic returns and risk; they quantify ESG as a **financial risk attribute** rather than directly enforcing any normative “responsibility” goal.  In other words, even with an ESG factor, one must still decide from which rating or proxy the factor is built.  Indeed, empirical work notes that ESG ratings from different providers diverge substantially, which complicates any attempt at a single objective measure ([papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4667120#:~:text=measures%20for%20Socially%20Responsible%20Investments,the%20ESG%20evaluation%20of%20the)).  For example, Cesarone *et al.* (2023) find that lack of alignment between raters “undermines the robustness” of any ESG measure, since one agency’s high‐ESG firm may be another’s low-ESG―leading to under- or over-estimation of sustainability ([papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4667120#:~:text=measures%20for%20Socially%20Responsible%20Investments,the%20ESG%20evaluation%20of%20the)).  A factor model can be estimated on one set of ratings, but cross-provider disagreement implies that such a “standardized” factor is only as reliable as the input data. 

# Multi-Criteria ESG Portfolios and Weighting Bias

Alternatively, many ESG portfolios are generated via **multi-criteria optimization**: treating ESG as an additional objective (or constraint) alongside return and risk.  This leads to a Pareto (efficient) frontier in risk/return/ESG space ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716024000095#:~:text=We%20present%20a%20framework%20for,scheme%20in%20a%20multicriteria%20ranking)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716024000095#:~:text=establish%20an%20initial%20set%20of,matrix%2C%20wherein%20investors%20perform%20pairwise)).  To select a single portfolio, these approaches usually **aggregate objectives using weights**.  For example, one might maximize a utility that is a weighted sum of return, negative risk, and ESG score (or minimize risk subject to a required ESG level).  In practice, this means choosing relative weights for “risk vs. return vs. ESG.”  Those weights invariably reflect investor preferences or mandates.  

The need to set weights introduces **subjectivity**.  Multiple studies have pointed out that simple weighted sums of ESG metrics (e.g. totaling E, S, G sub-scores) or risk/return/ESG tradeoffs depend heavily on the chosen weights. Zhang *et al.* note that many ESG optimizers “simply sum the individual scores of E, S, and G as the total ESG score, which is a **subjective approach**,” and that using fixed agency-provided weights assumes unrealistic independence among criteria ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S209623202100007X#:~:text=an%20important%20role%20in%20formulating,difficult%20to%20guarantee%20in%20reality)).  In other words, a linear aggregation assumes that poor performance in one ESG dimension can be fully offset by strength in another, which may not align with any particular notion of “responsibility.”  Moreover, the statistical structure of ESG data (nonlinearities, interactions) is lost in a linear sum ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S209623202100007X#:~:text=an%20important%20role%20in%20formulating,difficult%20to%20guarantee%20in%20reality)). 

Empirically, multi-objective ESG models are very sensitive to weighting choices.  For instance, one recent multi-criteria CVaR optimization found that **shifting weights drastically changes the portfolio**.  Their results show the highest utility when the return criterion got the most weight, and they explicitly remarked that “this highlights the model’s sensitivity to the weighting scheme… and reinforces the importance of performance as a critical driver” ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-24242-x#:~:text=As%20demonstrated%20in%20Tables%207,By%20adjusting%20the%20weights)).  In other words, if *performance* vs *ESG* are weighted differently, the optimal portfolio can change substantially, potentially introducing bias favoring whichever criterion is over-weighted.  

Even when trying to mitigate subjectivity, weighted approaches require normative input.  Methods like Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) or Stochastic Multicriteria Acceptability Analysis (SMAA) can be used to derive or sample weights based on investor rankings ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716024000095#:~:text=establish%20an%20initial%20set%20of,matrix%2C%20wherein%20investors%20perform%20pairwise)).  For example, one ESG-optimization paper uses AHP pairwise comparisons to generate criteria weights reflecting investor priorities, then Monte Carlo sampling (SMAA) to explore robust portfolios across many weight scenarios ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716024000095#:~:text=establish%20an%20initial%20set%20of,matrix%2C%20wherein%20investors%20perform%20pairwise)).  This “preference-based” approach creates a distribution of weights rather than a single vector, and then computes a probabilistic ranking of portfolios.  While clever, it still assumes that the initial comparisons and resulting weight distribution capture the investor’s subjective trade-offs ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716024000095#:~:text=establish%20an%20initial%20set%20of,matrix%2C%20wherein%20investors%20perform%20pairwise)).  In short, one must always start with some subjective judgment (even if made robust via randomization), because there is no form of ESG integration that is entirely free of preference.  

# Balancing Risk and Responsibility

In practice, factor models and multi-criteria methods are not mutually exclusive.  An ESG-aware factor model can be used to **quantify risk** in a way that is agnostic to explicit weights: for example, by adding an ESG factor to a risk model, one can measure how much ESG exposure contributes to volatility or expected return ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-024-00382-z#:~:text=be%20financially%20material%2C%20the%20integration,in%20common%20asset%20pricing%20models)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7755284/#:~:text=Pure%20factor%20portfolios%20have%20the,92)).  This quantifies the *financial risk* of ESG, but it does not by itself decide *how much* ESG should matter in the portfolio.  To impose a responsibility standard, one still needs a constraint or objective (e.g. “only stocks above X ESG score”).  Conversely, multi-objective optimization can enforce a responsibility goal (e.g. minimum ESG threshold) while a separate factor model handles diversification and risk.  

In summary, **pure factor-based construction does help objectify the ESG risk component** – it can reveal to what extent ESG is already priced into returns, and it frames sustainability as another systemic factor ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41260-024-00382-z#:~:text=be%20financially%20material%2C%20the%20integration,in%20common%20asset%20pricing%20models)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7755284/#:~:text=Pure%20factor%20portfolios%20have%20the,92)).  But it doesn’t automatically “weight” the ESG pillars (E vs S vs G) in a normative way.  Meanwhile, **multi-criteria weighting explicitly imposes investor or policy priorities**, but at the cost of embedding subjective bias into the model ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S209623202100007X#:~:text=an%20important%20role%20in%20formulating,difficult%20to%20guarantee%20in%20reality)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-24242-x#:~:text=This%20highlights%20the%20model%E2%80%99s%20sensitivity,By%20adjusting%20the%20weights)).  Even advanced weighting methods (AHP, SMAA, robust optimization) reduce but do not eliminate this subjectivity ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716024000095#:~:text=establish%20an%20initial%20set%20of,matrix%2C%20wherein%20investors%20perform%20pairwise)).  The choice of approach depends on what the portfolio manager values more: risk-based objectivity or explicit control of “responsibility.”  In either case, the literature makes clear that weighting ESG factors is not purely mechanical – one must carefully justify any weights used.
