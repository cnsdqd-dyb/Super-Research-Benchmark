# Lagged Soil–Vegetation Feedback and Carbon Budget Uncertainty

Soil and vegetation carbon pools respond on different timescales. Under rising CO₂, plant growth pulls carbon into biomass (and eventually litter), but soil organic pools turn over much more slowly, often taking years to decades to adjust. As a result, if forcing is reversed (for example, atmospheric CO₂ concentration declines), vegetation biomass and soil carbon can continue to rise for a time due to this legacy effect. This *disequilibrium* or hysteresis – the lag between CO₂ fertilization, vegetation growth, litterfall, and soil carbon uptake – means the land sink does **not** simply mirror itself when forcings reverse.  Asaadi et al. (2024) note that “transient changes in [CO₂] lead to a long-term disequilibrium between the CO₂ fertilization effect, vegetation biomass, litterfall, and soil carbon” ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=be%20delayed%20and%20thus%20show,induced%20hysteresis)). In their experiments, vegetation carbon and even soil carbon continued to increase for years after CO₂ had peaked, because the system was still “catching up” to earlier elevated CO₂ (see Fig. 3 in ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/20/2283/2023/#:~:text=3,BGC%20mode)) ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=be%20delayed%20and%20thus%20show,induced%20hysteresis))).

This lagged feedback has been shown to broaden prediction uncertainty. In a multi-model CMIP6 study with CO₂ ramped up then down, Asaadi et al. found that when emissions switched from positive to negative, the disagreement among models in key feedback metrics (the carbon–concentration feedback β and carbon–climate feedback γ) surged above what would be expected from linear accumulation of error.  In fact, the spread in model estimates of β increased by about a factor of four in the decline phase ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=in%20the%20previous%20section%2C%20but,while%20the%20increase%20in%20model)).  The authors conclude that the “model response to a change from increasing to decreasing forcing introduces an additional layer of uncertainty” ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=the%20emissions%20become%20negative%2C%20we,change%20from%20increasing%20to%20decreasing)).  Put simply, different models handle the post-peak lag in vegetation and soil carbon differently, so the multi-model ensemble fan‐out grows.  Asaadi et al. explicitly attribute this jump in uncertainty to “differences in the lagged model responses to the change from increasing to decreasing [CO₂] forcing” ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=the%20model%20uncertainty%20in%20feedback,This)) ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=in%20the%20previous%20section%2C%20but,while%20the%20increase%20in%20model)). 

This effect is compounded in more complex models. Models that explicitly simulate soil depth or microbial processes tend to have even larger spreads in projected carbon storage. For example, one study showed that a conventional soil C model predicted a moderate carbon loss (± dozens of Pg C), whereas a vertically‐resolved or microbial‐explicit version predicted **huge** uncertainty (± hundreds of Pg C) under the same climate scenario ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04526-9#:~:text=global%20total%20soil%20C%20over,lead%20to%20a%20larger%20prediction)). In other words, adding realistic soil–vegetation dynamics *tends to* increase, not decrease, forecast uncertainty.  It reflects the many interacting processes (and lags) that can play out differently in each model’s parameterization. 

**In summary:** Yes, lagged soil–vegetation feedbacks amplify uncertainty in global carbon-budget predictions. The time‐delay between vegetation forcing and soil response creates hysteresis in the land carbon sink, so that sinks inferred during one trajectory (rising CO₂) do not simply reverse on a CO₂ decline. Quantitative studies show this lag produces a strong jump in model spread when forcings switch sign ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=the%20emissions%20become%20negative%2C%20we,change%20from%20increasing%20to%20decreasing)) ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=in%20the%20previous%20section%2C%20but,while%20the%20increase%20in%20model)).  As a result, carbon-budget or Earth‐system model projections must contend with an extra source of uncertainty – the legacy of past forcings stored in vegetation and soils – which makes precise forecasting more difficult.  

**Key Points:** 
- Vegetation–soil carbon feedbacks have inertia: plants can increase carbon input (litter) faster than soil carbon can equilibrate. Even as CO₂ falls, biomass and soil C may continue rising for years ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=be%20delayed%20and%20thus%20show,induced%20hysteresis)).  
- In model experiments, this causes hysteresis: the carbon sink under declining CO₂ lags its behavior under rising CO₂. Models disagree strongly on this behaviour, causing a jump in prognostic uncertainty ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=the%20emissions%20become%20negative%2C%20we,change%20from%20increasing%20to%20decreasing)) ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=in%20the%20previous%20section%2C%20but,while%20the%20increase%20in%20model)).  
- Complex soil/vegetation models (with microbial pools or multiple layers) tend to show much larger uncertainty ranges than simple models ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04526-9#:~:text=global%20total%20soil%20C%20over,lead%20to%20a%20larger%20prediction)).  
- Together, these effects mean that soil–vegetation lagged feedback **does** amplify uncertainty in global carbon-budget projections ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=be%20delayed%20and%20thus%20show,induced%20hysteresis)) ([bg.copernicus.org](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/21/411/2024/#:~:text=the%20emissions%20become%20negative%2C%20we,change%20from%20increasing%20to%20decreasing)).
