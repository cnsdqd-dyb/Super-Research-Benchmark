# Signal Coordination Stability and Congestion Propagation

Urban traffic signal networks have a finite “stability” range of timing parameters (cycle, offsets, green splits) within which queues clear and flow remains manageable.  When coordination is well within this range, each intersection can process its arrivals each cycle and queues stay limited.  But once that stability boundary is crossed (e.g. demand approaches or exceeds capacity, or offsets are badly misaligned), the system enters a critical regime where local jams can cascade through the network.  In practice this means that under stable coordination, congestion stays localized; at or beyond the stability limit, it spreads rapidly.

- **Subcritical (stable) regime:** Properly coordinated signals clear local queues each cycle.  Congestion remains fragmented into small pockets and isolated clusters ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-023-01144-w#:~:text=in%20Fig.%C2%A01%20,is%20called%20the%20critical%20point)).  In this regime the network behaves subcritically – any local slowdown tends to dissolve rather than propagate, and throughput stays near its maximum.
- **At the stability boundary:** The system operates at a tipping point.  City-scale studies show that as flow reaches its peak, the number of separate congested clusters actually *peaks* ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-023-01144-w#:~:text=maximum%20number%20of%20congested%20clusters,and%20the%20resulting%20traffic%20collapse)).  Beyond this critical density, those clusters abruptly merge into one giant, city-spanning jam.  In effect the network undergoes a percolation-like phase transition: many small jams coalesce into a single congested cluster, and capacity falls off sharply ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-023-01144-w#:~:text=maximum%20number%20of%20congested%20clusters,and%20the%20resulting%20traffic%20collapse)).
- **Supercritical (unstable) regime:** Once the network is oversaturated, congestion spreads aggressively.  Queue spillbacks from one intersection quickly trigger backups at others.  Empirical models treat this like an epidemic: the jam-propagation rate (β) far exceeds the recovery rate (μ), so congestion multiplies across links faster than it can clear ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15353-2#:~:text=can%20be%20characterized%20by%20the,9%2C22)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15353-2#:~:text=Despite%20the%20complex%20human%20behavior,The%20proposed%20model)).  In network terms R₀>1, meaning small perturbations snowball into system-wide gridlock rather than fading out.

In summary, the stability boundary of signal coordination marks a network-wide tipping point.  As long as signals are coordinated (and demand is) **below** this threshold, congestion remains local and the network can recover.  **At or above** the boundary, however, the network becomes fragile: small delays and queue spillbacks propagate rapidly, merging individual jams into a widespread gridlock ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-023-01144-w#:~:text=in%20Fig.%C2%A01%20,is%20called%20the%20critical%20point)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15353-2#:~:text=can%20be%20characterized%20by%20the,9%2C22)).  This behavior – small, isolated jams versus a system-wide jam – has been likened to a phase transition or epidemic threshold in network theory, highlighting the critical role of signal stability in controlling congestion spread.  

**Sources:** Recent studies of urban traffic models show that (1) as flow approaches capacity, many small congested clusters suddenly coalesce into one large jam ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42005-023-01144-w#:~:text=maximum%20number%20of%20congested%20clusters,and%20the%20resulting%20traffic%20collapse)), and (2) once overwhelmed, congestion propagates much faster than it dissipates ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15353-2#:~:text=can%20be%20characterized%20by%20the,9%2C22)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-15353-2#:~:text=Despite%20the%20complex%20human%20behavior,The%20proposed%20model)). These effects directly tie signal coordination stability to network-wide congestion dynamics.
