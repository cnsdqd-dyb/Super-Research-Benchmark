# Conceptual “distance” vs. normative conflict

A **geodesic distance** is a precise mathematical concept – the shortest path between two points on a curved space (a manifold with a metric).  In principle we *can* embed concepts in a **conceptual space** with a distance metric, as in Gärdenfors’s theory of conceptual spaces: there, concepts are regions in a multi-dimensional similarity space, and the Euclidean distance between two points reflects how similar the corresponding items are ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13164-020-00495-5#:~:text=,Indow%20%2026%3B%20Shepard)).  For example, shades of blue are plotted in a 3-D color space so that more similar blues lie closer together ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13164-020-00495-5#:~:text=,Indow%20%2026%3B%20Shepard)).  In moral psychology, researchers have even used multi-dimensional scaling to show that several **moral principles** (e.g. fairness, autonomy, etc.) can be mapped as regions in a shared “moral space,” such that cases judged to be morally similar fall near each other and attract the same principle ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13164-020-00495-5#:~:text=subjective,survey%20in%20which%20they%20were)).  

However, **“universality”** and **“relativity”** as used in justice or ethics are very broad, abstract positions – not specific, finely-grained values.  *Moral universalism* holds that certain principles or values (e.g. human rights, fairness) apply objectively and equally to all people, everywhere ([grokipedia.com](https://grokipedia.com/page/Moral_universalism#:~:text=Moral%20universalism%20is%20the%20meta,specific%20frameworks.%5E%7B%5B2%5D%7D%5E%7B%5B3%5D%7D%20Historically)).  *Moral relativism* holds instead that truth, rightness and justice depend on cultural or contextual frameworks ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/archIves/win2024/entries/relativism/#:~:text=Relativism%2C%20roughly%20put%2C%20is%20the,assessment%20is%20specified%20or%20supplied)).  These are meta-ethical stances, not points determined by a few quantitative attributes.  In other words, universality and relativism are ontological views about how justice works, not easily parameterized coordinates in a pre-existing metric space.  Without a clear specification of what “coordinate values” capture the degree of universalism vs relativism, one cannot even locate these positions as points in a fixed feature space.  

A **“justice-principle space”**, if it exists, would have to be defined by choosing axes or dimensions (for example, weight given to human rights, social welfare, tradition, etc.).  Only after that could one attempt to put theories or positions into that space and measure distances.  Even then, the shortest path (geodesic) between two points would simply be a measure of how closely aligned the clusters of principles are, not a special boundary of conflict.  By contrast, normative conflicts usually happen *when* some groups insist on universal norms while others emphasize local norms; such clashes are better explained by substantive disagreements than by numeric “distance.”  For instance, one commentator notes that imposing “universal” values can provoke a backlash – a “clash of civilizations” – when people feel outsiders are forcing their norms on them ([books.openedition.org](https://books.openedition.org/pupo/413?lang=en#:~:text=5If%20Samuel%20Huntington%20has%20drawn,certain%20cultural%20values%20and%20social)).  This illustrates that the fault-line of conflict lies in incompatible values and power, not a smooth metric threshold.  

Similarly, the phrase **“critical surface of theoretical conflicts”** is usually a metaphor from physics (like a phase transition boundary), not a standard concept in ethics or law.  One might try to interpret it as “the boundary in idea-space where debates turn bitter.”  But in practice conflicts in justice principles arise from competing worldviews and values (universal vs. particular, individual rights vs. community duties, etc.), and there is no known “distance” measure that suddenly flips from peace to conflict when it hits a certain value.  Actual disputes are resolved (or not) by argument, negotiation, and examination of principles, not by calculating a shortest-path distance.  

In summary, embedding **universality** and **relativism** as points in a geometric space of justice principles would first require a well-defined metric on that space – something not provided by the abstract question.  While cognitive scientists have shown that moral principles *can* be modeled in a conceptual similarity space ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13164-020-00495-5#:~:text=subjective,survey%20in%20which%20they%20were)), universality and relativity are so high-level that they resist this kind of quantification.  Without a precise metric, the notion of a geodesic distance between “universalism” and “relativism” has no clear meaning.  Therefore it does **not** automatically define a “critical surface” of conflict.  In effect, moral or theoretical conflicts are not determined by geometric distance in an abstract manifold, but by the actual content and context of the disagreeing principles – which must be analyzed on their own terms. 

**Answer:** No. The idea conflates a formal geometric notion (geodesic distance) with loosely defined philosophical positions.  In an abstract “principle space,” one cannot compute a geodesic between universality and relativity unless a concrete metric space has been specified – which it hasn’t.  In practice, conflicts between universalist and relativist justice views arise from substantive value clashes, not from crossing a mathematically defined threshold.
