# Successional State-Transition Models in Restoration

Ecological succession is often conceptualized as a sequence of community states (e.g. bare ground → grasses → shrubs → forest).  In mathematical or graphical form this can be represented by a **state-transition model** or **state-transition matrix**, which records which community state can shift to which other state (and at what rates or probabilities). Such models (for example Markov-chain matrices of cover‐type transitions ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304380010006125#:~:text=for%20vegetation%20restoration%20assessment%20and,been%20disturbed%20by%20natural%20disasters)) or more general state-and-transition models) are widely used to describe vegetation dynamics.  In the state-and-transition framework, managers identify discrete “states” (community types or phases) and possible **transitions** between them, including directions that are difficult or irreversible ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9203009/#:~:text=of%20STGs%20designed%20for%20particular,example%20the%20%2039%20%E2%80%9CEDIT%E2%80%9D)) ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)).  The goal is to capture how ecosystems *normally* move through successional phases, and to use that knowledge to guide management (e.g. by promoting desirable transitions and avoiding risky ones) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9203009/#:~:text=of%20STGs%20designed%20for%20particular,example%20the%20%2039%20%E2%80%9CEDIT%E2%80%9D)) ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)).

However, such state-transition matrices are **descriptive models**, not physical laws.  They summarize how an ecosystem *tends* to change under given conditions.  In practice, real successional trajectories can vary widely (due to species interactions, disturbances, climate, etc.), and restoration projects often introduce new actions that are not encapsulated in a passive successional matrix.  For example, managers may **plant late-successional species directly**, control invasives, or modify soils – interventions that effectively create transitions not present in a natural succession matrix.  In this sense, the matrix itself does not *ictate* what is impossible: rather, it highlights *when* natural recovery might stall or require help.

## Limitations: Thresholds and Alternative States

State-transition models often reveal **thresholds** and alternative stable states that can impede restoration.  For instance, many systems exhibit *hysteresis*: once a community crosses a threshold into a degraded or invaded state (e.g. grassland dominated by exotic annuals, or a thicket without deep-rooted perennials), the usual successional matrix no longer leads back to the original state without strong intervention.  As one rangeland example explains, “once the transition crosses the threshold, none of the alternative states can move back into a desired phase… without extensive and expensive inputs” ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)).  In other words, beyond certain points (like heavy woody invasion or loss of key species) **passive succession stalls** and natural regeneration alone fails. 

Classic research on restoration ecology similarly finds that many degraded ecosystems are *resilient to passive restoration*, owing to factors such as biotic feedbacks, loss of seedbanks, connectivity changes, or invasion by exotics ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints)).  These feedbacks create self-reinforcing states (for example, invasive plants altering soil chemistry) that are not easily undone by simply removing the original disturbance.  Successional models that incorporate such feedbacks (alternative-state models) show that simply re‐establishing historical conditions may not be enough to trigger return to the former community ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints)).  In that sense, the state-transition structure *does* highlight constraints: if an ecosystem is in an undesirable state according to the matrix (e.g. a persistent weedy or degraded phase), the matrix suggests it will stay there unless external forces are applied. 

Put differently, a fixed transition matrix can reveal “impassable” links.  For example, a Markov transition matrix fitted from observation might show that a grassland state never directly transitions to a mature forest state (probability = 0).  If a restoration goal is to achieve forest cover, the matrix indicates that passive grass-to-forest succession is impossible without crossing intermediate stages.  This might appear to constrain restoration: one would have to first establish shrub or young tree phases (or in practice, physically plant trees) to create a path.  In many systems, some successional jumps simply do not occur naturally – so if the matrix is interpreted rigidly, it would limit the predicted effectiveness of passive restoration.

## Restoration Interventions and Altered Trajectories

Crucially, restoration is **not limited to the transitions encoded in a passive successional matrix**.  Restoration managers actively modify conditions, effectively *rewiring* the state-transition diagram. For example, planting a mix of late-successional species can skip multiple early stages, adding “shortcut” transitions that the natural matrix lacked.  Likewise, clearing invasives or amending soil can remove barriers that kept the ecosystem in a degraded state, again altering the trajectory.  In essence, a restoration practitioner supplies new transition edges between states. 

This means that while the state-transition matrix sets out the “default” ecological pathways, restoration can bypass or expand them. For instance, Luxton et al. (2024) showed that by treating degraded temperate shrublands and actively planting tree species, novel mixes of vegetation states could establish that would not arise via the usual successional path ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4907/13/2/199#:~:text=Restoration%20Trajectories%20and%20Ecological%20Thresholds,Planted%20Urban%20Forest%20Successional%20Development)).  In practical terms, this implies that the static transition probabilities in a model do not fix the ultimate outcome: they can be changed by human action.

Field examples confirm this flexibility. In a landslide restoration study, scientists used a Markov-chain transition matrix (built from satellite data) to **simulate vegetation recovery** over time ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304380010006125#:~:text=for%20vegetation%20restoration%20assessment%20and,been%20disturbed%20by%20natural%20disasters)).  The model predicted ongoing progress toward forest cover.  However, in reality further storms and landslides “disturbed” the recovery.  This illustrates two points: (1) the model’s fixed transition matrix captured one hypothetical trajectory, but (2) unexpected events can deviate real restoration from that path.  Similarly, many restoration projects achieve recovery rates faster or slower than predicted by naive succession models, simply because additional actions (like fertilization or biological control) alter the dynamics.

## Implications for Restoration Effectiveness

In summary, successional state-transition matrices identify *potential* pathways and highlight where natural recovery may stall (especially when thresholds or feedbacks create alternative stable states) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints)) ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)).  They are useful **management tools** for diagnosing when a site might need intervention (e.g. “the model shows no transition out of this degraded state without help”) ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9203009/#:~:text=of%20STGs%20designed%20for%20particular,example%20the%20%2039%20%E2%80%9CEDIT%E2%80%9D)).  But these matrices do **not of themselves impose an unbreakable limit** on restoration.  Restoration is an active process precisely because it often must break or revise the natural trajectory.  By adding or accelerating transitions, managers can drive the ecosystem toward the target state even if the undisturbed succession would not.

Thus, state-transition models do *suggest* where restoration may be difficult or require extra effort (e.g. when a threshold has been crossed ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints))).  However, they are **guides**, not “restoration law”.  Successful restoration can alter the assumed transitions.  In effect, the matrix constrains restoration **only insofar as managers allow it to**.  Smart restoration planning uses these models to pinpoint where to intervene, but then uses interventions to go beyond the default succession  ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9203009/#:~:text=of%20STGs%20designed%20for%20particular,example%20the%20%2039%20%E2%80%9CEDIT%E2%80%9D)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints)). 

**Key points:**  
- Ecological state-transition matrices (or STMs) **describe** how succession tends to proceed, including irreversible thresholds ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints)).  
- They can **flag constraints** (e.g. alternate states or absent transitions) that make passive recovery ineffective ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints)).  
- Restoration actions can **bypass or modify** these trajectories (for example, planting species to force a state change), adding transitions not in the natural matrix ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304380010006125#:~:text=for%20vegetation%20restoration%20assessment%20and,been%20disturbed%20by%20natural%20disasters)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9203009/#:~:text=of%20STGs%20designed%20for%20particular,example%20the%20%2039%20%E2%80%9CEDIT%E2%80%9D)).  
- Therefore, while successional models inform restoration strategy, they do *not rigidly limit* what restoration can achieve. They highlight where **extra effort** is needed, but effective restoration can overcome those constraints by altering the trajectory. 

**Conclusion:**  Successional state-transition matrices reveal potential bottlenecks and guide where restoration must intervene (such as overcoming thresholds) ([rangelandsgateway.org](https://rangelandsgateway.org/topics/rangeland-ecology/overview-state-transition-models#:~:text=represent%20the%20reversible%20portion%20of,without%20extensive%20and%20expensive%20inputs)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169534703003197#:~:text=successional,prioritize%20and%20address%20these%20constraints)). In that sense they illustrate **constraints** on the natural recovery pathway. However, they do not inherently *stop* restoration: managers can actively change the system’s path.  In practice, restoration effectiveness is determined by combining knowledge of successional constraints with targeted actions, not by the inevitability of the modeled transitions ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9203009/#:~:text=of%20STGs%20designed%20for%20particular,example%20the%20%2039%20%E2%80%9CEDIT%E2%80%9D)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304380010006125#:~:text=for%20vegetation%20restoration%20assessment%20and,been%20disturbed%20by%20natural%20disasters)).
