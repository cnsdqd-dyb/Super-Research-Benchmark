# Cloud microphysics parameterizations and model resolution  

Climate models (even “convection‐permitting” regional models at 1–4 km grid spacing) cannot explicitly resolve individual cloud or raindrops.  Processes like droplet activation, collision-coalescence, freezing and ice aggregation occur at scales far smaller than any practical grid.  As a result, **cloud microphysics is always parameterized** in weather and climate models ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20this%20is%20not%20the,precipitation%20particles%2C%20many%20microphysical%20processes)).  These parameterizations are inherently approximate – they rely on simplified bulk rate equations or limited spectral (“bin”) representations – and many details (especially for ice processes) remain ill-constrained by observations ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20this%20is%20not%20the,precipitation%20particles%2C%20many%20microphysical%20processes)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Moreover%2C%20there%20is%20no%20well%E2%80%90defined,respect%2C%20microphysics%20is%20arguably%20more)).  In short, microphysics schemes are a major source of uncertainty in climate simulations.  One recent review even calls cloud microphysics “a problem that refuses to die,” noting that microphysical uncertainties will likely *dominate* overall model uncertainty even as resolution increases ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Microphysics%20represents%20one%20part%20of,together%20parameterizations%20for%20subgrid%E2%80%90scale%20cloud)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Given%20limited%20direct%20observational%20guidance%2C,Overall%2C%20we%20argue%20that%20to)).  

Parameterizations in general (clouds, convection, turbulence, etc.) are **widely recognized as the key limitation on model resolution** ([climate.sustainability-directory.com](https://climate.sustainability-directory.com/question/what-factors-limit-model-resolution/#:~:text=Parameterization%20is%20arguably%20the%20most,simplified%20mathematical%20formulas%20called%20parameterizations)).  In principle, running a model on finer grids will let it “solve” more cloud dynamics explicitly (resolving updrafts, downdrafts and mesoscale organization).  Indeed, storm-resolving (∼1 km) models are now routine for weather and regional climate ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=A%20conceptually%20straightforward%20way%20to,modeling%20at%20similar%20resolutions%20is)).  However, *microphysics remains parameterized at every grid size*.  There is **no resolution at which microphysical processes are fully fluid-dynamically resolved** – phenomena like droplet collision or ice nucleation still occur below grid scale ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20this%20is%20not%20the,precipitation%20particles%2C%20many%20microphysical%20processes)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Moreover%2C%20there%20is%20no%20well%E2%80%90defined,respect%2C%20microphysics%20is%20arguably%20more)).  (In fact, microphysics even lacks a clear “largest unresolved scale”: growth processes can be sensitive to molecular‐scale diffusion and turbulence.)  Thus, increasing model resolution *reduces* some parameterization errors (e.g. for convection or cloud fraction) but does *not eliminate* microphysics uncertainty ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=A%20conceptually%20straightforward%20way%20to,modeling%20at%20similar%20resolutions%20is)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20this%20is%20not%20the,precipitation%20particles%2C%20many%20microphysical%20processes)).  As one authoritative source notes, raising resolution can effectively remove the “macrophysics” error (making clouds explicit), **but cannot remove the microphysics error – it remains to be parameterized no matter what** ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20this%20is%20not%20the,precipitation%20particles%2C%20many%20microphysical%20processes)).  

- **Parameterization errors propagate as bias.**  Any systematic bias in a microphysics scheme (for example, overestimating rain conversion or freezing) will feed back on the simulated climate (through latent heating, cloud albedo, etc.).  These biases tend to persist and “propagate” through the model time‐integration (affecting temperature, humidity, circulation).  This propagation is not an unbounded random walk – conservation laws keep errors bounded – but it does mean that errors in microphysics lead to persistent errors in predicted rainfall, cloud cover and radiative forcing.  For example, one study found that bulk vs. bin microphysics schemes can produce very different cloud mass profiles and precipitation while using identical large‐scale conditions ([acp.copernicus.org](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/18/13/2018/acp-18-13-2018.html#:~:text=parameterizations,resolution%20in%20the%20NWP%20models)).  In general, “different cloud parameterizations can produce vastly different results” for rainfall and radiation ([climate.sustainability-directory.com](https://climate.sustainability-directory.com/question/what-factors-limit-model-resolution/#:~:text=Parameterization%20is%20arguably%20the%20most,simplified%20mathematical%20formulas%20called%20parameterizations)), which reflects how microphysical assumptions feed into the simulated climate.  

- **Relative impact versus grid spacing.**  Some experiments have quantified the interplay between resolution and microphysics complexity.  In one cloud-system study, simply coarsening the grid (from 0.5 km to 15–35 km) *dramatically* reduced cloud mass and sensitivities to aerosols, whereas switching from an explicit “bin” microphysics to a two-moment bulk scheme had a smaller (though still significant) effect ([acp.copernicus.org](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/18/13/2018/acp-18-13-2018.html#:~:text=parameterizations,resolution%20in%20the%20NWP%20models)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Given%20limited%20direct%20observational%20guidance%2C,Overall%2C%20we%20argue%20that%20to)).  In that case, resolution changes caused 2–4 times larger changes in cloud water than did changing the microphysics scheme ([acp.copernicus.org](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/18/13/2018/acp-18-13-2018.html#:~:text=parameterizations,resolution%20in%20the%20NWP%20models)).  The takeaway is that at very coarse resolution, unresolved convective dynamics are the first-order error.  But as the model approaches fine (cloud-permitting) scales, **the remaining uncertainty increasingly comes from microphysics**.  A recent review emphasizes that even if one removes coarse-grid convection errors by high resolution, the uncertainty in cloud forecasts is then “arguably” dominated by microphysics parameterization ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Given%20limited%20direct%20observational%20guidance%2C,Overall%2C%20we%20argue%20that%20to)).  

In summary, **cloud microphysical parameterizations do impose a practical limit on how much regional climate models improve by finer resolution**.  Because microphysics must always be parameterized, its errors and uncertainties remain even in high‐resolution runs.  Those errors propagate into the simulation of clouds, precipitation and climate.  In effect, even an ultra‐fine‐meshing model would not escape microphysical uncertainty, so cloud microphysics sets an intrinsic floor on prediction accuracy ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Given%20limited%20direct%20observational%20guidance%2C,Overall%2C%20we%20argue%20that%20to)) ([climate.sustainability-directory.com](https://climate.sustainability-directory.com/question/what-factors-limit-model-resolution/#:~:text=Parameterization%20is%20arguably%20the%20most,simplified%20mathematical%20formulas%20called%20parameterizations)).  That said, microphysics is *one* of several limits (along with available data, dynamics, etc.); it should be addressed in concert with higher resolution.  Indeed, improving microphysics schemes and making them “scale-aware” is an active research focus, precisely because parameterized microphysical error remains a key constraint on model skill ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Given%20limited%20direct%20observational%20guidance%2C,Overall%2C%20we%20argue%20that%20to)) ([climate.sustainability-directory.com](https://climate.sustainability-directory.com/question/what-factors-limit-model-resolution/#:~:text=Parameterization%20is%20arguably%20the%20most,simplified%20mathematical%20formulas%20called%20parameterizations)).  

**Answer:** Yes.  Because cloud microphysics are always parameterized, the errors in those parameterizations persist and feed through the model, effectively limiting how far gains in resolution can improve forecasts.  In practice, this means that simply refining the grid won’t eliminate microphysical uncertainty – it remains a dominant error source unless the schemes themselves are improved ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7507216/#:~:text=Given%20limited%20direct%20observational%20guidance%2C,Overall%2C%20we%20argue%20that%20to)) ([climate.sustainability-directory.com](https://climate.sustainability-directory.com/question/what-factors-limit-model-resolution/#:~:text=Parameterization%20is%20arguably%20the%20most,simplified%20mathematical%20formulas%20called%20parameterizations)).
