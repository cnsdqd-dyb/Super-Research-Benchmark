# Rheology and Basal Sliding in Ice-Flow Models

Ice-sheet models combine a **flow-law** (stress–strain relationship) for internal deformation with a **sliding law** at the bed.  In practice, the ice’s rheology is usually implemented by a power-law (Glen’s law) of the form $\dot\varepsilon = A\,\tau^n$, where $\dot\varepsilon$ is strain rate, $\tau$ the deviatoric stress, $A$ a softness/temperature factor, and $n$ the stress exponent ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-022-00385-x#:~:text=%24%24%7B%5Cdot%7B%5Cepsilon%20%7D%7D_%7Be%7D%3DA%7B%5Ctau%20%7D_%7Be%7D)).  (Commonly, $n\simeq3$ is assumed so that ice is strongly shear-thinning ([tc.copernicus.org](https://tc.copernicus.org/articles/15/1731/2021/#:~:text=Above%2C%20n%3Dn,thinning%20fluid%C2%A0%28Glen%2C%C2%A01955)).)  This flow law dictates how easily ice deforms under a given driving stress: higher $n$ or lower $A$ means **stiffer** ice (slower creep for a given stress), while lower $n$ or higher $A$ means more compliant ice.  In models, any mismatch between observed surface velocities and those predicted by internal deformation is attributed to sliding. Thus **basal sliding laws are calibrated relative to the flow-law**: sliding is effectively “the excess” motion beyond what ice creep would produce ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-025-01661-z#:~:text=models%20represent%20internal%20deformation%20through,mass%20loss)).

Because of this coupling, the stress–strain law strongly **constrains the impact of changes in basal sliding on mass loss and sea-level rise**.  For example, if basal friction is suddenly reduced (more slippery bed), the driving stress redistributes: part is relieved at the bed and part goes into deforming the ice column.  How much the glacier or ice sheet speed-up translates into increased ice discharge depends on the flow law.  A highly non-linear (high-$n$) rheology amplifies velocity response to stress changes, while a “softer” rheology (low $n$ or high $A$) spreads the stress and damps the response.  In effect, the flow law **limits the acceleration** that basal sliding can produce.  As one review notes, ice-sheet models “represent internal deformation through flow laws” and thereafter attribute any remaining motion to sliding ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-025-01661-z#:~:text=models%20represent%20internal%20deformation%20through,mass%20loss)). Crucially, **forecasts of sliding-driven mass loss hinge on these flow laws**. In the words of De La Peña et al. (2025), forecasts of basal-sliding contribution “fundamentally depend on flow laws for internal deformation” and even small changes in the flow-law form or parameters have a “substantial impact on model outcomes” ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-025-01661-z#:~:text=models%20represent%20internal%20deformation%20through,mass%20loss)).

Recent studies highlight that uncertainty in the rheology rations the predicted sea-level response to sliding.  In idealized modeling experiments, uncertainties in Glen’s-law parameters alone can rival the effect of climate forcing.  For instance, Zeitz et al. (2020) showed that plausible variations in $n$ and $A$ could **double the flow-driven mass loss** over a few centuries compared to standard values ([tc.copernicus.org](https://tc.copernicus.org/articles/14/3537/2020/index.html#:~:text=in%20flow,that%20this%20uncertainty%20carries%20over)).  When basal sliding is allowed (using the shallow-shelf approximation), the spread becomes even larger: with sliding, the ensemble of flow-law parameter choices produced relative changes in ice loss from –30% to +470% after 100 years ([tc.copernicus.org](https://tc.copernicus.org/articles/14/3537/2020/index.html#:~:text=When%20sliding%20is%20taken%20into,still%20range%20from%20%E2%88%9216%20to)), and up to a **5-fold difference** compared to the median when sliding is included ([tc.copernicus.org](https://tc.copernicus.org/articles/14/3537/2020/index.html#:~:text=Allowing%20for%20not%20only%20flow,100%C2%A0years%20compared%20with%20standard%20parameters)).  In other words, allowing sliding amplified the effect of rheology uncertainty dramatically.  These findings imply that the stress–strain law does not merely passively limit flow – it actively modulates how much a change in basal friction changes the ice flux to the ocean.

Another insight comes from observational constraints on $n$.  If the true ice rheology is more sensitive to stress (e.g. $n\approx4$ instead of 3, as some satellite-based analyses suggest), then current models (using $n=3$) may **underestimate** the sensitivity of ice flow to sliding and melt forcing.  As one study notes, a higher exponent makes flow rates more sensitive to stress, and hence “future sea-level rise is likely more sensitive to climate forcings than predicted by present models using common assumptions of the flow law” ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-022-00385-x#:~:text=is%20a%20step%20towards%20reassessing,assumptions%20of%20the%20flow%20law)). This means that the assumed stress–strain law effectively **encodes a ceiling** on how aggressive the ice response to basal changes can be. In practice, if a model assumes a softer rheology (lower stress response), then even large increases in sliding will not translate into arbitrarily larger ice discharge or sea-level rise; conversely, if the ice is actually stiffer (higher $n$), increased sliding could accelerate flow more than a simpler model would predict.

In summary, the constitutive flow law in ice-sheet models acts as a key constraint on sea-level projections when basal sliding changes.  Because basal sliding is diagnosed relative to internal deformation, any change in bed slipperiness propagates through the stress–strain relation. Robust, well-constrained flow laws are therefore essential: inaccuracies in the stress–strain relation would mis-calibrate the sliding response and hence bias sea-level rise forecasts ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-025-01661-z#:~:text=models%20represent%20internal%20deformation%20through,mass%20loss)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-022-00385-x#:~:text=is%20a%20step%20towards%20reassessing,assumptions%20of%20the%20flow%20law)). The coupling means that **even a dramatic change at the bed cannot produce unlimited mass loss** – it is the ice’s rheology that ultimately caps how much extra stress can turn into flow.
