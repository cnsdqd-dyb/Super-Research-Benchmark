In practice there is no built‐in “magic” threshold that defines substitutes – it’s a design choice or tuning parameter.  Shopping‐basket methods may compute a similarity or association score between every pair of items (using Jaccard, cosine, log‐likelihood, etc.), and then simply *filter* that list to the top N or to scores above some cutoff.  In other words, **the threshold is set externally** (by the data scientist or product team) to yield a reasonable number of recommendations.  Common approaches include: 

- **Top‐N neighbors**: e.g. for each product you pick the top 5 or 10 most similar items as “substitutes.”  This effectively imposes a dynamic cutoff (you ignore all but the highest‐scoring pairs) ([stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/2390312/collaborative-filtering-non-personalized-item-to-item-similarity#:~:text=w%2Fratings%2C%20then%20of%20course%20you,commerce%20site)).  
- **Significance or confidence rules**: e.g. if using a statistical association measure (like Dunning’s log‐likelihood), you may require the co‐occurrence to be statistically significant (p < 0.05 or 0.01) before calling it a real relationship ([opensourceconnections.com](https://opensourceconnections.com/blog/2016/06/06/recommender-systems-101-basket-analysis/#:~:text=The%20bell%20curve%20above%20is,01)).  This is equivalent to a similarity‐score threshold on the null hypothesis test.  
- **Support/coverage floors**: in many retail systems one also insists that each substitute pair has enough support (or combined sales) so as not to be a fluke.  Again the minimum support (count) or confidence acts as a threshold selected by business need.

In short, **nothing intrinsic in market‐basket analysis automatically “decides” the threshold** – it’s chosen by the system designer.  One typically examines the distribution of item‐pair similarities or runs cross‐validation/A–B tests on historical data to balance recall vs. precision, then fixes a cutoff.  Practically this means tuning by domain strategy – for example, ensure each product has only a few high‐similarity substitutes, or only show items whose association exceeds a given significance level ([stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/2390312/collaborative-filtering-non-personalized-item-to-item-similarity#:~:text=w%2Fratings%2C%20then%20of%20course%20you,commerce%20site)) ([opensourceconnections.com](https://opensourceconnections.com/blog/2016/06/06/recommender-systems-101-basket-analysis/#:~:text=The%20bell%20curve%20above%20is,01)).  

**Sources:** Industry writeups and CF literature explain that item–item recs always need a cutoff (e.g. only “top‐N” similar items or those above a chosen significance) to avoid an explosion of trivial recommendations ([stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/2390312/collaborative-filtering-non-personalized-item-to-item-similarity#:~:text=w%2Fratings%2C%20then%20of%20course%20you,commerce%20site)) ([opensourceconnections.com](https://opensourceconnections.com/blog/2016/06/06/recommender-systems-101-basket-analysis/#:~:text=The%20bell%20curve%20above%20is,01)). These cutoffs are set by experiment or business policy, not by the basket‐analysis formula itself.
