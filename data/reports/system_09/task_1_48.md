The key point is that the cultural‐covariance matrix encodes *how much* cultural features vary together in the population, and its eigenvectors/eigenvalues pick out the “principal” cultural dimensions and their variances.  In fact, if $\Sigma$ is the covariance of the cultural foundation vectors, then each eigenpair $(v,\lambda)$ of $\Sigma$ satisfies 
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(v^T X)^2\big] \;=\; v^T\Sigma v \;=\; \lambda,$$ 
meaning the eigenvalue $\lambda$ is exactly the variance of the data projected onto that eigenvector (cultural direction) ([math.stackexchange.com](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2147211/why-are-the-eigenvalues-of-a-covariance-matrix-equal-to-the-variance-of-its-eige#:~:text=with%20%24v%24%29,what%20we%20wanted%20to%20show)).  Thus a large eigenvalue in $\Sigma$ means one cultural dimension dominates the diversity (one strong common factor), whereas many moderate eigenvalues means the cultural variance is spread over multiple independent dimensions.

When we build a “social cohesion” matrix – for example a graph or similarity matrix whose edge weights are determined by cultural similarity – its eigenvalue spectrum reflects those same modes of variation.  In network terms, the spectrum of the adjacency or Laplacian matrix encodes connectivity and cohesion.  For instance, the eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian “indicate and summarize many network properties including … an approximate connectivity measure” ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0106052#:~:text=two%20node%20graph%20of%20an,many%20such%20separate%20network%20properties)).  Concretely, if one defines the social‐cohesion matrix as something like $M = X X^T$ (where $X$ is the matrix of cultural vectors), then $M$ shares the same nonzero eigenvalues (up to scaling) as the covariance $X^T X$.  In other words, large variances in certain cultural dimensions (large eigenvalues of $\Sigma$) create large eigenvalues in the social cohesion matrix, indicating a strong cohesive mode along that dimension.  Conversely, if the covariance is “flatter” (more isotropic or high‐dimensional), the network spectrum is more spread out: no single eigenvalue dominates, and the largest eigenvalue (and thus overall connectivity) is smaller.

In practical terms, *greater cultural heterogeneity* (more nonzero covariance modes) tends to *broaden* the eigenvalue spectrum of the cohesion matrix – producing multiple moderate eigenvalues which correspond to subgroups or fragmented clusters – while *greater cultural homogeneity* (one big covariance eigenvalue) yields a spectrum with one or few very large eigenvalues.  The largest eigenvalue (or spectral gap) of the social network is thus driven by the dominant cultural variance: a big $\lambda_{\max}$ in $\Sigma$ produces a big $\lambda_{\max}$ (strong cohesion) in the network, while a more uniform $\Sigma$ produces a more even, less “peaked” network spectrum ([math.stackexchange.com](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2147211/why-are-the-eigenvalues-of-a-covariance-matrix-equal-to-the-variance-of-its-eige#:~:text=with%20%24v%24%29,what%20we%20wanted%20to%20show)) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0106052#:~:text=two%20node%20graph%20of%20an,many%20such%20separate%20network%20properties)).

**Answer:** In such models the covariance of the cultural vectors tells us which cultural dimension has the most spread.  Its eigen‐decomposition gives principal cultural axes: each eigenvalue equals the variance along one axis ([math.stackexchange.com](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2147211/why-are-the-eigenvalues-of-a-covariance-matrix-equal-to-the-variance-of-its-eige#:~:text=with%20%24v%24%29,what%20we%20wanted%20to%20show)).  A social‐cohesion matrix built from these vectors (e.g. a similarity or Gram matrix) then has an eigen‐spectrum that mirrors this variance structure.  Large cultural‐covariance eigenvalues create large eigenvalues in the cohesion spectrum (signalling a tight cohesive group along that cultural dimension), while a more uniform covariance (diverse cultural axes) yields a broader, flatter spectrum.  In network terms the Laplacian or adjacency eigenvalues summarize cohesion (connectivity) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0106052#:~:text=two%20node%20graph%20of%20an,many%20such%20separate%20network%20properties)), and these eigenvalues grow or shrink in direct proportion to the corresponding variances in the cultural covariance.  Thus the shape of the covariance eigenvalue distribution (few large values vs. many moderate values) directly shapes the shape of the cohesion‐matrix eigenvalue spectrum ([math.stackexchange.com](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2147211/why-are-the-eigenvalues-of-a-covariance-matrix-equal-to-the-variance-of-its-eige#:~:text=with%20%24v%24%29,what%20we%20wanted%20to%20show)) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0106052#:~:text=two%20node%20graph%20of%20an,many%20such%20separate%20network%20properties)).
