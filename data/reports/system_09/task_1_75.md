# Interfacial charge-transfer kinetics set the rate limits.  The solid–liquid interface (electrode–electrolyte boundary) governs how fast Li⁺ ions (or other charge carriers) can be inserted/extracted.  In P2D (Newman) models this enters via the Butler–Volmer exchange current; in practice “Li⁺ transfer resistance” at the interface is often the *largest* internal resistance in a battery ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.1c21683#:~:text=The%20development%20of%20high,this%20basis%2C%20the%20mechanism%20and)).  When kinetics are sluggish (low exchange current or high activation energy), a given high current causes large overpotentials and concentration gradients.  This means that a larger fraction of the applied voltage is “lost” to polarization, and the electrode cannot fully intercalate quickly. As a result, fast cycling drives the electrode potential into ranges that trigger extra side reactions (thicker SEI formation, electrolyte decomposition or Li plating), consuming active lithium and damaging the electrode ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/11/2/65?type=check_update&version=3#:~:text=gradual%20loss%20of%20the%20B2,charging%2C%20into%20the%20positive%20electrode)).  

- **Polarization and side-reactions accelerate at high C.**  At low C-rates the interface reactions can keep up and polarization is small, but at high C even modest kinetic barriers (e.g. finite exchange current density) cause significant voltage drop.  This in turn raises the anode potential (relative to Li/Li⁺) and forces faster electrolyte reduction or Li metal deposition.  Indeed, experiments show that higher current leads directly to accelerated degradation: for example, increasing the charge rate from 1C to 1.5C (with 2C discharge) roughly halved cycle life, and 2C charging shortened life by ~70% ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/11/2/65?type=check_update&version=3#:~:text=electrode%E2%80%99s%20elasticity,fast%20charging%E2%80%93fast%20discharging%20cycling%20operations)).  The same study attributes this to side reactions at the electrodes (loss of active Li, electrode structural damage, and thicker SEI) that become much more pronounced under high-rate conditions ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/11/2/65?type=check_update&version=3#:~:text=gradual%20loss%20of%20the%20B2,charging%2C%20into%20the%20positive%20electrode)) ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.1c21683#:~:text=The%20development%20of%20high,this%20basis%2C%20the%20mechanism%20and)).  

- **Dominant aging pathways shift with rate.**  Detailed models and experiments show that as C-rate rises, Li plating eventually dominates capacity fade.  Under “serious plating” conditions (high current or low temperature), cycle life crashes and almost all capacity loss comes from irreversible Li deposition ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/8/11/234#:~:text=serious%20lithium%20plating%2C%20the%20cycle,under%20high)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772671124002924#:~:text=phenomena%2C%20such%20as%20the%20Solid,some%20negative%20effects%20of%20fast)).  For instance, one mechanics-based study found that at very low temperature and 3C charging, plating could consume ≈100% of the lost capacity, driving the cell to end-of-life in only a few dozen cycles ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/8/11/234#:~:text=serious%20lithium%20plating%2C%20the%20cycle,under%20high)).  By contrast, at moderate rates or higher temperatures, SEI growth is more important.  In short, slow interface kinetics during fast cycling shift the dominant degradation from gradual SEI growth to rapid plating and “dead Li,” dramatically cutting Latin-ion cell longevity.

- **Implications for cycle-life modeling.**  Accurate life-prediction models **must** include interface kinetics.  In practice this means using Butler–Volmer or related expressions to compute interfacial currents and overpotentials, together with rate-dependent aging terms (SEI growth, plating rates, etc.).  Models that include these show that fast charge‐discharge imposes much higher degradation rates.  By contrast, models ignoring kinetic limits would erroneously predict similar lifetimes at all C.  In fact, parametric analyses (e.g. varying exchange current densities) show life and end-of-life voltage depend sensitively on kinetics and C-rate.  For example, one electrochemical aging model predicted that **higher charging rates cause “increased degradation, with lithium plating becoming more severe at these elevated rates”** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772671124002924#:~:text=phenomena%2C%20such%20as%20the%20Solid,some%20negative%20effects%20of%20fast)).  In summary, interface-reaction rates set how battery stress scales with C-rate: slow kinetics amplify voltage drop and side reactions at high current, so cycle life falls off rapidly with faster cycling.  Thus any reliable cycle-life prediction must account for these solid–liquid interface kinetics, especially under aggressive (high-C) conditions ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/11/2/65?type=check_update&version=3#:~:text=electrode%E2%80%99s%20elasticity,fast%20charging%E2%80%93fast%20discharging%20cycling%20operations)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/8/11/234#:~:text=serious%20lithium%20plating%2C%20the%20cycle,under%20high)).

**Key points:** Slow solid/liquid reaction kinetics act like a bottleneck at high current, forcing large overpotentials.  This accelerates parasitic reactions (SEI film growth, Li plating) that consume cyclable lithium and damage electrodes.  Empirical and model studies show that raising C-rate drastically shortens life (e.g. ∼50–70% life loss going from 1C to 2C ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/11/2/65?type=check_update&version=3#:~:text=electrode%E2%80%99s%20elasticity,fast%20charging%E2%80%93fast%20discharging%20cycling%20operations)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-0105/8/11/234#:~:text=serious%20lithium%20plating%2C%20the%20cycle,under%20high))).  Therefore, cycle-life models must include interfacial exchange-current parameters and rate-dependent aging in order to capture the observed C-rate dependence of battery degradation.
