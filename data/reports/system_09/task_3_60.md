Membrane CO₂‐capture systems rely on diffusion (solution–diffusion or pore flow) rather than phase change, so in principle they can be very energy‐efficient.  For example, Baker et al. showed that as the CO₂ content in the feed rises (10→30 %), the electric energy for a membrane capture system drops dramatically (~52% decrease) compared to only ~7.5% drop for a traditional amine scrubbing system ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1383586622014186#:~:text=to%20the%20commonly%20used%20amine,plants%20that%20were%20sized%20to)).  In other words, membranes *can* use much less power at high CO₂ partial pressures.  However, **diffusion resistances** (both inside the membrane and in the gas boundary layers) eat into this advantage.  Any mass‐transfer resistance means the membrane does *not* see the full driving force, so more compression or deeper vacuum is needed to achieve the same CO₂ flux.  In practice this raises the parasitic power. 

Favre et al. specifically point out that high‐permeance membranes create strong **concentration polarization** in the gas channels – essentially an additional mass‐transfer (diffusive) resistance at the membrane surface ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0375/12/9/884/htm#:~:text=materials%20will%20for%20sure%20generate,membrane%20processes%20for%20carbon%20capture)).  This unmodeled resistance reduces effective permeance and forces higher pressure drops (and thus more pumping work) for a given capture target.  In effect the “energy vs. capture” curves for CCS become worse (shift upward) when diffusion losses are included.  Xu and co‐workers note that membrane separations are *rate-governed* (diffusional) processes that inherently produce more entropy than equilibrium separations, making them less energy‐efficient ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376738817315831#:~:text=separating%20agent%20or%20membranes%20processes,to%20be%20less%20energy%20efficient)).  Thus, when diffusion limits are taken into account, the energy‐consumption curves (e.g. kWh per ton CO₂ vs. capture fraction or CO₂ purity) are higher than the ideal case.  In short, diffusion resistances in a membrane module reduce the effective driving force and raise the required compressor/vacuum work – degrading the membrane’s energy efficiency and shifting the CCS energy‐efficiency curve toward higher energy penalties ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0375/12/9/884/htm#:~:text=materials%20will%20for%20sure%20generate,membrane%20processes%20for%20carbon%20capture)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376738817315831#:~:text=separating%20agent%20or%20membranes%20processes,to%20be%20less%20energy%20efficient)). 

**Answer:**  Diffusion (mass‐transfer) resistances – both within the membrane polymer/pores and in the gas boundary layer – reduce the effective CO₂ flux for a given pressure difference.  In effect one must raise pressure or add stages to compensate, which increases energy use.  As Favre et al. note, high‐performance membranes will inevitably suffer boundary‐layer concentration polarization (an extra diffusive resistance) that is often neglected in process models ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0375/12/9/884/htm#:~:text=materials%20will%20for%20sure%20generate,membrane%20processes%20for%20carbon%20capture)).  Including these diffusion losses forces more pumping power to achieve the same capture, so the membrane CCS “efficiency curve” (e.g. kWh/tonne vs. capture rate) shifts upward: membranes look less ideal and save less energy than predicted by models that ignore diffusion.  This is consistent with the general result that “rate‐limited” (diffusional) separations have intrinsically lower second‐law efficiency ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376738817315831#:~:text=separating%20agent%20or%20membranes%20processes,to%20be%20less%20energy%20efficient)).
