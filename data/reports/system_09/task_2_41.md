# Federated Credit Scoring: Accuracy vs. Privacy Trade-off

Modern P2P lending platforms build very accurate default‐prediction models by mining rich, high‐dimensional borrower data.  For example, Mu et al. (2019) show that an ensemble of gradient‐boosted trees (GBDT, XGBoost, LightGBM) can *highly* accurately predict loan defaults on P2P data ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378437119313652#:~:text=accurately%20predict%20the%20default%20probability,based)).  However, such centralized ML relies on aggregating sensitive user records, creating a privacy risk.  Federated learning (FL) is often proposed to avoid pooling raw consumer data.  In FL, each institution keeps customer data locally and only shares model updates.  A DataFleets blog illustrates this idea: with FL the credit bureaus could train a “single, holistic credit scoring model” without ever explicitly accessing users’ raw data ([medium.com](https://medium.com/datafleets-blog/federated-learning-for-credit-scoring-1db292de9fbb#:~:text=1,ever%20explicitly%20accessing%20consumer%20data)).  In other words, no single central database holds all the personal records, which on paper improves privacy.

In practice, achieving the *ideal* balance — maximum predictive power *and* maximum privacy — is extremely difficult.  Any measure that hides or perturbs data (encryption, differential-privacy noise, etc.) tends to **reduce** the model’s accuracy, so there is an inherent privacy‐utility tradeoff.  For example, Wang et al. (2023) develop a vertical‐FL logistic regression for credit scoring that uses homomorphic encryption and a penalty term to filter redundant information ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167923622001816#:~:text=method%20based%20on%20logistic%20regression,multiple%20parties%20securely%20and%20effectively)).  Their scheme can “leverage information from multiple parties securely and effectively” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167923622001816#:~:text=method%20based%20on%20logistic%20regression,multiple%20parties%20securely%20and%20effectively)), improving default‐prediction by using more data.  But the homomorphic encryption adds computation and strict privacy guarantees that would not be present in an ordinary ML model.  Likewise, DataFleets points out that to get full privacy one might need *secure protocols and differential privacy*, at the cost of some accuracy ([medium.com](https://medium.com/datafleets-blog/federated-learning-for-credit-scoring-1db292de9fbb#:~:text=machines,level%20scalability%20for%20big%20datasets)). In short, **no known credit‐scoring model is free**: a fully private design will usually score *less accurately* than one with unfettered access to all data.

Crucially, even “simple” FL (replacing centralized data pooling with gradient‐sharing) is **not inherently leak-proof**.  Recent research highlights that gradient updates can reveal sensitive information.  For instance, Du *et al.* (2025) survey gradient inversion attacks and note that adversaries *can indeed “reconstruct private training data from shared gradients”* in many FL settings ([www.usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du#:~:text=Federated%20learning%20,three%20fundamental%20aspects%20influencing%20GIA%27s)).  Similarly, Wainakh *et al.* (2021) propose a label‐leakage attack (LLG) demonstrating that, by inspecting the magnitude and direction of a user’s gradient, one can determine which labels (e.g. default vs. non-default) appear in that user’s data.  Even more strikingly, recent work shows that **aggregate gradients** – not just individual ones – may leak labels.  In “Breaking Secure Aggregation…,” researchers craft a special “fishing” model such that the server can solve a linear system and recover each client’s labels from the summed gradient.  Their experiments achieved *100%* recovery of private labels from aggregated updates ([openreview.net](https://openreview.net/forum?id=oj3cNVye5k#:~:text=and%20gradients%20of%20FCL%27s%20bias,various%20datasets%20and%20model%20architectures)).  In other words, a malicious server could manipulate the FL process and extract borrowers’ repayment histories or default flags, even though only “safe” sums of gradients were shared.  

Of course, these attacks depend on details (model architecture, batch size, etc.) and can be mitigated.  The USENIX SoK by Du *et al.* emphasizes that gradient attacks tend to be fragile: simple defenses (gradient clipping, noise, regularization) can break many inversion methods, so leakage is **not** truly “inevitable” if good practices are applied ([www.usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du#:~:text=SOTA%20GIAs%20across%20diverse%20settings,realistic%20investigations%20on%20this%20topic)).  In federated credit systems one can layer on secure aggregation protocols, additive noise (differential privacy), or cryptographic techniques.  For example, the multi-party logistic scheme above uses encrypted gradients to prevent any party from seeing raw updates ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167923622001816#:~:text=method%20based%20on%20logistic%20regression,multiple%20parties%20securely%20and%20effectively)).  However, each protection makes the model harder to train or slightly less precise.  In fact, “optimal” balance is subjective: regulators or platforms must *choose* how much prediction power to sacrifice for privacy.  

In summary, a P2P credit model that only uses FL‐style aggregation **does not magically achieve perfect privacy**.  It can certainly improve security compared to a central database, but it still embodies a tradeoff.  Without extra safeguards, gradients can leak behavioral patterns ([www.usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du#:~:text=Federated%20learning%20,three%20fundamental%20aspects%20influencing%20GIA%27s)) ([openreview.net](https://openreview.net/forum?id=oj3cNVye5k#:~:text=and%20gradients%20of%20FCL%27s%20bias,various%20datasets%20and%20model%20architectures)).  With safeguards (DP noise, encryption, gradient clipping, etc.) one can substantially reduce leaks, but then the default‐prediction accuracy will typically drop.  Thus the algorithm must be carefully designed – there is no free lunch.  In practice, credit-scoring platforms aim for a *reasonable* tradeoff (perhaps by regulatory mandate), not a theoretically “optimal” point.  In short: federated aggregation helps protect privacy, but unless rigorous privacy measures are added, it is **not** impervious to inference attacks ([www.usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du#:~:text=Federated%20learning%20,three%20fundamental%20aspects%20influencing%20GIA%27s)) ([openreview.net](https://openreview.net/forum?id=oj3cNVye5k#:~:text=and%20gradients%20of%20FCL%27s%20bias,various%20datasets%20and%20model%20architectures)).

**Key ideas and trade-offs:**  

- **High accuracy needs data:** State-of-the-art P2P default models exploit high-dimensional consumer behavior for best performance ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378437119313652#:~:text=accurately%20predict%20the%20default%20probability,based)). Centralizing data gives top accuracy but breaches user privacy.  
- **Federated premise:** FL can train a joint model without sharing raw records; e.g. bureaus could combine data “without ever explicitly accessing consumer data” ([medium.com](https://medium.com/datafleets-blog/federated-learning-for-credit-scoring-1db292de9fbb#:~:text=1,ever%20explicitly%20accessing%20consumer%20data)). This in principle reduces certain privacy risks.  
- **Privacy measures hurt accuracy:** Techniques like secure aggregation, encryption, or differential privacy introduce noise or computational overhead. They protect users but typically reduce model performance ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167923622001816#:~:text=method%20based%20on%20logistic%20regression,multiple%20parties%20securely%20and%20effectively)) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/datafleets-blog/federated-learning-for-credit-scoring-1db292de9fbb#:~:text=machines,level%20scalability%20for%20big%20datasets)).  
- **Gradient inversion risks:** Sharing gradients (even aggregated) can leak info. Empirical attacks have reconstructed user data or labels from gradients ([www.usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du#:~:text=Federated%20learning%20,three%20fundamental%20aspects%20influencing%20GIA%27s)) ([openreview.net](https://openreview.net/forum?id=oj3cNVye5k#:~:text=and%20gradients%20of%20FCL%27s%20bias,various%20datasets%20and%20model%20architectures)). Thus “secure” FL must add defenses or accept some risk.  
- **No silver bullet:** Proper defenses (DP noise, HE, clipping, etc.) can largely block known attacks ([www.usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du#:~:text=SOTA%20GIAs%20across%20diverse%20settings,realistic%20investigations%20on%20this%20topic)), but there is always a trade-off. Any “optimal” solution must choose how much privacy to trade for predictive power; a perfect Pareto‐optimal in both dimensions is generally impossible in real systems.  

**Sources:** Recent studies and reviews of FL and credit scoring ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378437119313652#:~:text=accurately%20predict%20the%20default%20probability,based)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167923622001816#:~:text=method%20based%20on%20logistic%20regression,multiple%20parties%20securely%20and%20effectively)) ([www.usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/du#:~:text=Federated%20learning%20,three%20fundamental%20aspects%20influencing%20GIA%27s)) ([openreview.net](https://openreview.net/forum?id=oj3cNVye5k#:~:text=and%20gradients%20of%20FCL%27s%20bias,various%20datasets%20and%20model%20architectures)) show the methods and their vulnerabilities. These works confirm that FL can help combine multi-party data for credit models, but also document the privacy attacks that must be guarded against.
