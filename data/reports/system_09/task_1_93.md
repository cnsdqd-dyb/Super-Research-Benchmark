# Integration of Online Reviews and Travel Memories in Destination Choice

Travelers draw on **personal memories** when choosing destinations. Tourists’ recollections of past trips become a key information source: as Braun-LaTour et al. note, *“the memory of tourism experiences is the single most important source of information”* for deciding on a future destination ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/tourisme/5053?lang=#:~:text=Braun,same%20experience%20in%20the%20future)).  Positively remembered experiences (fun excursions, great service, etc.) significantly boost the intention to return ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211973623001277#:~:text=Memories%20of%20tourism%20experiences%20are,However%2C%20such)).  In psychological terms, people mentally “time travel” – vividly replaying past vacations and imagining future ones – when making choices ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0732118X21000180#:~:text=Mentally%20traveling%20to%20one%27s%20personal,close%20decisions%20tend%20to%20include)).  Thus, nostalgia and affect-laden memories can strongly bias destinations toward which travelers feel attached, effectively anchoring their preferences on familiar, favorably remembered places.

In parallel, travelers heavily rely on **online reviews** as social information.  Nowadays, prospective visitors routinely consult user-generated reviews and travel blogs for up-to-date, first-person accounts of a place.  Indeed, researchers report that online travel reviews (OTRs) have *“significantly transformed”* trip planning, with many tourists searching social media and review platforms to form expectations ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12658100/#:~:text=Online%20travel%20reviews%20,credible%20than%20other%20forms%20of)).  Because most reviews are written by fellow travelers (not by advertisers), they are viewed as highly credible ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12658100/#:~:text=on%20OTRs%20for%20information%20to,tourists%20has%20become%20an%20issue)).  These narratives – detailing sights, food, accommodations and service – let potential visitors gather cues and “pre-experiences” of the destination ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12658100/#:~:text=form%20expectations%20and%20even%20trigger,OTRs%20on%20potential%20tourists%20and)).  Studies show that reading reviews helps reduce uncertainty and perceived risk: travelers use them to obtain practical information and essentially “reduce risk or uncertainty” in their decision-making ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12658100/#:~:text=on%20OTRs%20to%20obtain%20valuable,information%20to%20better%20understand%20and)).  In short, online reviews act as a rich, trusted information source that complements or compensates for a traveler’s lack of direct experience.

In practice, **memory and reviews combine** in a user’s decision process. If a place is well-known to someone, their personal memories provide a baseline image; if it’s novel, reviews fill the gap. Consumers typically integrate both sources: they may start with what they already recall or expect, then adjust those impressions based on others’ current experiences.  For example, theory predicts individuals will reuse information from important past trips (via episodic memory) while simulating future visits ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0732118X21000180#:~:text=Mentally%20traveling%20to%20one%27s%20personal,close%20decisions%20tend%20to%20include)).  Empirical evidence confirms this interaction: one study found that *positive external information* (analogous to strong positive reviews) actually **amplified** the influence of prior travel memories on revisit intent ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211973623001277#:~:text=were%20stronger%20when%20memories%20of,memory%20to%20improve%20destinations%27%20competitiveness)).  Conversely, marketing research shows that external cues can *“alter how memory affects decisions”* ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211973623001277#:~:text=Within%20the%20marketing%20discipline%2C%20practitioners,Madore%2C%20Jing%2C)) – for instance, a stream of very negative reviews might dampen enthusiasm even for a previously beloved destination. In sum, destination decision-making is a dynamic synthesis: personal recollections anchor a traveler’s expectations, while online reviews serve as social proof that confirms, updates or revises those expectations. By weighing these inputs together, travelers form a holistic image of a destination’s likely appeal before booking their trip ([journals.openedition.org](https://journals.openedition.org/tourisme/5053?lang=#:~:text=Braun,same%20experience%20in%20the%20future)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211973623001277#:~:text=were%20stronger%20when%20memories%20of,memory%20to%20improve%20destinations%27%20competitiveness)).
