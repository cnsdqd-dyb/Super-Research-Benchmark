In practice, fuel‐cell MEAs use a compromise: thick catalyst layers (more Pt) improve longevity, but any extra thickness adds transport losses.  Thick cathodes retain more water and longer oxygen pathways, so their local gas diffusion resistance (and even proton/path resistance) rises sharply ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=previously%20presented%20modelling%20and%20experimental,41)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013468617303778#:~:text=potential%20in%20the%20electrode,degradation%20and%20for%20which%20deeper)).  For example, doubling the cathode CL thickness (from ~4.4 to 11.7 μm) has been measured to increase diffusion‐resistance by ~264% ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=that%20a%20significant%20increase%20in,or%20the%20lower%20I%2FC%20ratio)).  (Thin layers, by contrast, flow reactants easily but tend to corrode carbon supports and lose platinum quickly.)  Notably, tests show that with a fixed total Pt loading the polarization curves barely change with thickness ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013468617303778#:~:text=under%20which%20conditions%20the%20cell,that%20thickness%20does%20matter%20in)) – implying that designers thicken the CL mainly for durability rather than peak power.  Thus the engineering goal is to *gain the durability advantage of extra catalyst volume while preserving gas access*. 

To achieve that balance, MEA designers carefully tailor the CL microstructure and composition.  Key approaches include: 

- **Optimizing ionomer and porosity.**  The Nafion (ionomer) binder fraction and pore volume are tuned so that protons can reach the catalyst without choking gas flow.  Too much ionomer swells into narrow channels and slows O₂ diffusion, while too little leaves dead zones for protons ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=solidification%20and%20ultimately%20the%20physical,Too%20little)).  In practice, studies find optimal ionomer/carbon ratios of roughly 0.7–1 (about 25–40 wt% Nafion) under typical humidities ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=retaining%20larger%20amounts%20of%20product,27%2C28%5D.%20In%20addition)).  Likewise, high meso-porosity (and a direct high-water‐contact fraction) is maintained so that O₂ can percolate through the layer.  These design rules hold especially for thicker CLs: any imbalance (excess ionomer or collapsed pores) will exacerbate mass‐transfer losses ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=solidification%20and%20ultimately%20the%20physical,Too%20little)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=porosity%20and%20thickness%20of%20the,content%20of%20two%20GDLs%20with)).  In short, the CL is made as porous as practical for gas paths while still forming continuous protonic networks. 

- **Layered/graded catalyst structures.**  Rather than one homogeneous slab, the catalyst layer is often built in sub-layers to separately optimize reactions and transport.  A common scheme is a **double-layer CL**: a thin, ionomer-rich sublayer (adjacent to the membrane) ensures good proton conduction and utilization of Pt, while a thicker, more porous or PTFE‐laden sublayer (facing the GDL) enhances oxygen diffusion and water drainage ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=In%20Ref.%C2%A0%5B17%5D%2C%20Su%20and%20co,type%20of%20electrode%20provides%20a)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=layers.%20In%20Ref.%C2%A0,distribution%20ratio%20of%20Pt%20and)).  For example, Zhang et al. fabricated a **“dual-bonded”** CL with both ionomer and hydrophobic PTFE binders and saw a ~25% power improvement ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=layers.%20In%20Ref.%C2%A0,distribution%20ratio%20of%20Pt%20and)).  Similarly, graded Pt loading (more Pt in one sub-layer) can balance utilization: Su et al. showed that varying the Pt‐to‐thickness ratio between two layers changed overall Pt utilization and allowed a thicker total CL without local flooding ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=In%20Ref.%C2%A0%5B17%5D%2C%20Su%20and%20co,type%20of%20electrode%20provides%20a)).  In practice these multilayer designs create continuous gas channels (in the PTFE‐rich portion) and strong proton networks (in the ionomer‐rich portion), so even a relatively thick total layer maintains good mass transfer. 

- **Hydrophobic additives and microporous backing.**  The CL is usually underlain by a microporous layer (MPL) and GDL that act as gas reservoirs.  By tailoring these layers (e.g. adding hydrophobic carbon or graded porosity), engineers ensure that liquid water exits quickly and that gases distribute evenly into the CL.  A well‐engineered MPL/GDL can offset some losses of a thicker CL by providing many entry paths to the catalyst.  In addition, incorporating hydrophobic PTFE or sulfonated polymers in the outer CL drives water out of pores.  In fact, thicker CLs intrinsically hold more product water, which becomes a diffusion barrier ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=previously%20presented%20modelling%20and%20experimental,41)), so every strategy to wick liquid away broadens gas pathways.  Graded pore distributions through the GDL/MPL/CL stack have also been studied as a systematic fix: a recent review emphasizes that a “graded design” (varying porosity, hydrophobicity, catalyst loading, etc. through the MEA) improves both uniformity and mass‐transport ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544219307170#:~:text=fuel%20cells,plane)).  In short, engineers use MPLs, flow‐field hydrophobic treatments, and pressure/humidity control to keep even thick catalyst layers well‐ventilated. 

- **Advanced catalysts and thin‐film designs.**  Finally, novel catalyst/support materials help decouple thickness from performance.  For instance, 3M’s nanostructured thin‐film (NSTF) electrodes use crystalline supports (whiskers) with no carbon and minimal ionomer – effectively an “infinitely thin” catalyst layer.  Such NSTF CLs eliminate carbon corrosion and ionomer degradation entirely ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=forefront%20of%20research%20and%20has,with%20such%20a%20catalyst%20structure)), greatly boosting durability even at very low total Pt loadings.  Of course, this approach demands excellent gas access in a very thin film, but state-of-the-art cell designs can deliver it.  Similarly, new catalyst‐support architectures (e.g. large-sphere carbon with nitrogen doping) yield very open pore networks.  One study reports nearly humidity‐independent ionomer films and an *“unprecedented low”* local O₂ transport resistance for a sub-0.04 mgPt/cm² layer ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-33892-8#:~:text=hydrophilic%20nature%20of%20the%20carbon,%E2%88%922%7D%29%20catalyst%20layer)).  These materials allow thin, high‐surface‐area CLs that inherently give both high power density and long life. 

In summary, a thicker catalyst layer will indeed boost durability but also raises O₂ diffusion resistance.  Fuel‐cell engineers manage this trade-off by **designing the CL structure itself** – through optimized binder content, graded/multi-layer fabrication, hydrophobic doping, and advanced supports – so that a “thicker” CL still has efficient gas pathways.  The result is an MEA that retains the high power density of a thinner layer while gaining the extra catalyst buffering needed for long life ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=previously%20presented%20modelling%20and%20experimental,41)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=forefront%20of%20research%20and%20has,with%20such%20a%20catalyst%20structure)). 

**Key references:** EIS and polarization studies show mass‐transfer spikes with CL thickness ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=previously%20presented%20modelling%20and%20experimental,41)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/21/7299#:~:text=that%20a%20significant%20increase%20in,or%20the%20lower%20I%2FC%20ratio)). Graded‐layer designs (ionomer vs. PTFE sublayers) demonstrably improve high‐current power ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=layers.%20In%20Ref.%C2%A0,distribution%20ratio%20of%20Pt%20and)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=In%20Ref.%C2%A0%5B17%5D%2C%20Su%20and%20co,type%20of%20electrode%20provides%20a)).  Reviews of MEA engineering stress that spatially grading porosity, ionomer content, and catalyst loading through the MEA is crucial for balancing performance and durability ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544219307170#:~:text=fuel%20cells,plane)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S037877531630091X#:~:text=solidification%20and%20ultimately%20the%20physical,Too%20little)).
