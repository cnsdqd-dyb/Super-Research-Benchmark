Heparin is given by continuous IV infusion (usually after a small bolus at cannulation) and **frequently titrated** to lab targets. For example, ELSO guidelines suggest an infusion of ~7.5–20 U/kg/hr ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6753426/#:~:text=Current%20ELSO%20guidelines%20for%20anticoagulation,6)), but in practice centers individualize within a wide range.  Labs like ACT, aPTT or anti-Xa are checked often (e.g. every 4–6 h) and the heparin rate is adjusted up or down (often in 100 U/hr steps) to stay in the chosen anticoagulation window. Typical targets might be ACT ~180–220 s, aPTT ≈50–90 s or anti-Xa ≈0.3–0.7 U/mL ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11935455/#:~:text=ECMO%20is%20typically%20monitored%20by,mortality%20without%20an%20increase%20in)). 

In balancing clot vs. bleed risk, many centers use **lower–intensity anticoagulation** when safe.  For instance, targeting a **lower ACT** (around 140–160 s) or the low end of the anti-Xa range has been associated with **fewer bleeding complications** without increasing thrombotic events ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11935455/#:~:text=ECMO%20is%20typically%20monitored%20by,mortality%20without%20an%20increase%20in)).  In fact, observational studies in ECMO have shown that bleeding (not clotting) is more strongly linked to mortality ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9782867/#:~:text=analyzed%20bleeding%20and%20thrombotic%20complications,5%20%2C%2014)), so protocols often “err on the side” of milder anticoagulation.  If any significant bleeding occurs, the heparin is reduced or temporarily held (often after checking for obvious bleed sources), whereas evidence of oxygenator clot or systemic thrombus prompts an increase in heparin dose.  Many teams will supplement antithrombin (ATIII) if heparin resistance is suspected, since low ATIII can blunt heparin’s effect.

In summary, heparin dosing on ECMO is **dynamic and individualized**: a continuous infusion is started (typically with an initial 50–100 U/kg bolus), then carefully titrated to maintain coagulation test targets.  Adjustments are guided by frequent lab checks and by clinical status, always aiming for the lowest effective anticoagulation.  Anti-Xa monitoring is increasingly used because it correlates best with heparin dose and has been linked to fewer bleeding episodes ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11935455/#:~:text=ECMO%20is%20typically%20monitored%20by,mortality%20without%20an%20increase%20in)). Guidelines (ELSO) recommend aggressive monitoring and leave the exact targets up to institutional protocols ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6753426/#:~:text=Current%20ELSO%20guidelines%20for%20anticoagulation,6)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11935455/#:~:text=ECMO%20is%20typically%20monitored%20by,mortality%20without%20an%20increase%20in)), reflecting the need to balance thromboembolism risk against bleeding on a case-by-case basis.
