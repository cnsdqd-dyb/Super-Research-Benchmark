# Balancing Model Accuracy and Interpretability  

Risk‐type models (e.g. credit or insurance scoring) sit squarely in regulated domains where **both** accurate predictions and transparent reasoning are needed.  Simple models (linear regressions, scoring rules, shallow decision trees) are inherently interpretable, but as regulators note, such high‐simplification often *sacrifices accuracy* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=model%20explainability%20of%20course%20is,not%20seen%20by%20the%20model)).  Complex methods (random forests, boosting, deep nets) can capture nuanced patterns and improve performance, but they act as “black boxes” ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391150327_Reimagining_Compliance_Explainable_AI_Models_for_Financial_Regulatory_Audits#:~:text=models%2C%20particularly%20complex%20ones%20like,often%20function%20as%20%E2%80%9Cblack%20boxes%2C%E2%80%9D)).  The tension is clear: in regulated finance the model output must often be justified to stakeholders and overseers, but straightforward, high‐accuracy models and fully interpretable models can point in opposite directions.  As one source observes, “deep neural networks… often function as ‘black boxes,’ making it difficult to understand or justify their decisions” – a serious problem for compliance ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391150327_Reimagining_Compliance_Explainable_AI_Models_for_Financial_Regulatory_Audits#:~:text=models%2C%20particularly%20complex%20ones%20like,often%20function%20as%20%E2%80%9Cblack%20boxes%2C%E2%80%9D)).

One strategy is to use post‐hoc explainability tools with the high‐accuracy model so that we don’t *lose* predictive power.  Techniques like SHAP and LIME do exactly that: they explain a trained (opaque) model’s outputs by attributing contributions to input features, without changing the model itself ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=There%20is%20also%20a%20variety,%282020)).  In practice, this means the bank or insurer can use a powerful ML model to score loans or policies, then run SHAP to report, say, “this feature gave +0.15 to the risk score, that feature gave –0.10,” etc.  In effect, this *attempts* to give users a view into the black box.  For example, an industry working group notes that SHAP (game‐theoretic feature attributions) and LIME (local linear surrogates) are widely used to make complex models more transparent ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=There%20is%20also%20a%20variety,%282020)).  In principle, this approach lets us “have our cake and eat it”: retain the sophisticated model for accuracy and use SHAP to achieve some transparency.  In fact, several recent frameworks explicitly **optimize** model accuracy subject to a minimum explainability constraint (often measured via Shapley values) ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391150327_Reimagining_Compliance_Explainable_AI_Models_for_Financial_Regulatory_Audits#:~:text=and%20Internal%20Audits,theoretic%20measures%20and)).  In these formulations, SHAP is built into the model‐risk objective so that predictive power and compliance requirements are balanced.

# SHAP and Other XAI Tools: A Partial Fix  

Post-hoc explainers certainly *improve* visibility compared to a naked black box.  By decomposing each prediction into feature contributions, SHAP produces an intuitive result (feature impact values) that stakeholders can inspect.  In global mode it approximates overall feature importance; in local mode it explains individual decisions.  This helps address calls for “meaningful information about the logic” of a model (e.g. GDPR/AI Act Article 13) or for risk committees seeking to understand what drives predictions.  For instance, a Bundesbank review observes that using XAI **provides** more reliable risk metrics for control processes ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Deutsche%20Bundesbank,case%20and%20to%20the%20stakeholders)).  Many practitioners report that SHAP results align with domain intuition, helping to catch obvious errors.  

However, regulators and experts caution that SHAP/LIME provide only a *partial* view, not a guarantee.  The Bundesbank warns that XAI explanations “only show a limited picture of model behavior” and that “ML combined with an XAI approach cannot make the black box fully transparent, merely less opaque” ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Deutsche%20Bundesbank,case%20and%20to%20the%20stakeholders)).  In other words, we should treat SHAP outputs as a **compensation for missing transparency**, not as a literal truth of the model’s logic ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Deutsche%20Bundesbank,case%20and%20to%20the%20stakeholders)) ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=,and%20susceptibility%20of%20misleading%20explanations)).  Indeed, adding SHAP to a workflow is effectively adding another (meta‐)model; it introduces its own uncertainties and costs.  In practice one must design diligent controls around these methods.  Standard advice is to adopt a *balanced approach*: tailor XAI use to the specific use case and stakeholder needs, and *don’t forget* fundamental risk controls ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Deutsche%20Bundesbank,case%20and%20to%20the%20stakeholders)) ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=a%20need%20to%20recognise%20trade,manage%20risks%20and%20enhance%20client)).  If a bank relies on SHAP for loan‐decision explanations, regulators expect that the bank can validate that the explanations are stable, consistent with underlying policy, and integrated into a broader model‐risk framework.

# Known Limitations of SHAP Explanations  

In both theory and practice, SHAP-based explanations can be **imperfect or misleading**.  For one thing, true Shapley values (from cooperative game theory) are hard to compute exactly for large models, so implementations use approximations (e.g. KernelSHAP sampling or TreeSHAP heuristics).  Studies have found that these empirical SHAP estimates often **deviate from the exact values** and even mis-rank features.  For example, one theoretical analysis proves that **any** definition of SHAP scores necessarily yields *misleading information* about feature importance in certain models ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0888613X23002438#:~:text=the%20most%20widely%20used%2C%20including,routinely%20observed%20in%20ML%20classifiers)).  Empirically, its authors show there exist even very simple classifiers for which SHAP’s attributions are wrong, and they re-iterate that “such theoretical limitations of SHAP scores are routinely observed in ML classifiers” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0888613X23002438#:~:text=the%20existing%20definitions%20of%20SHAP,routinely%20observed%20in%20ML%20classifiers)).  Another study finds that the SHAP tool’s (Python library) outputs can fail to capture the true feature order imposed by exact Shapley values ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0888613X23002438#:~:text=by%20the%20SHAP%20tool%20accurately,The)).  In sum, there are provable counter-examples where SHAP **over‐ or under‐estimates** the importance of some features.  

In real data one faces additional issues: SHAP assumes feature independence and linear additive contributions, so **strong correlations** can destabilize the attributions.  In practice, highly correlated predictors (common in financial data) can cause SHAP values to swing unpredictably between them.  The literature and practitioners frequently note that correlated inputs can make SHAP *lose consistency* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20game,at%20an%20additional%20effort%2C%20be)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=model%20added%20to%20the%20workflow%2C,does%20not%20explain%20causal%20relationships)).  In the Fraunhofer fintech guide, SHAP’s authors explicitly warn that “strongly correlated features… cause instabilities in SHAP evaluations,” and they stress that SHAP only reflects *associations*, not causal relationships ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=model%20added%20to%20the%20workflow%2C,does%20not%20explain%20causal%20relationships)).  (In other words, SHAP explains *what the model uses*, not necessarily why that feature truly matters for the real-world outcome.)  This matters for compliance: an unstable explanation (if different random seeds or minor data changes give different SHAP scores) can make regulators question the reliability of any single explanation snapshot.  

Further, SHAP and LIME explanations can be **attacked**.  Research (Slack et al. 2020) demonstrates that a malicious model provider can craft inputs to “fool” SHAP or LIME into producing chosen explanations ([www.edps.europa.eu](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2023-11-16-techdispatch-22023-explainable-artificial-intelligence_en#:~:text=There%20is%2C%20however%2C%20research%20that,apart%20and%20a%20malicious%20developer)).  In practical terms, nothing prevents an unscrupulous vendor from salt­ing the training data or tweaking the computation so that certain variables show overly high or low contributions in the SHAP report.  The European Data Protection Supervisor’s tech report flags exactly this: *“research…shows potential weaknesses in…SHAP…perturbation-based methods…a malicious developer”* can subvert them ([www.edps.europa.eu](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2023-11-16-techdispatch-22023-explainable-artificial-intelligence_en#:~:text=There%20is%2C%20however%2C%20research%20that,apart%20and%20a%20malicious%20developer)).  This security gap is a direct compliance threat: if an external audit or regulator uses SHAP output as evidence of fairness or transparency, those numbers could in principle be polluted.  Finally, even putting aside adversaries, SHAP’s sampling or tree‐traversal approximations come with **error**.  If one does not use enough samples or ignores certain interactions, the attributions will contain noise. A recent blog notes that SHAP may incur “approximation errors in large datasets” without careful sampling ([johal.in](https://johal.in/explainable-ai-techniques-building-interpretable-models-using-shap-for-financial-risk-assessment/#:~:text=,sampling%20techniques%20without%20sacrificing%20accuracy)).  At the extreme, computing exact Shapley values is combinatorial, so any shortcut necessarily induces inaccuracy. Regulators know this: a Basel BIS paper points out that XAI methods suffer from “inaccuracy, instability and susceptibility of misleading explanations” ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=,and%20susceptibility%20of%20misleading%20explanations)).

In summary, SHAP values should be treated with caution.  Some key pitfalls are:  
- **Approximation Error:** KernelSHAP sampling may require many permutations for stability. Insufficient sampling produces noisy or biased SHAP scores. TreeSHAP reduces cost for trees, but any approximation can still drift from the true Shapley values ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0888613X23002438#:~:text=by%20the%20SHAP%20tool%20accurately,The)) ([johal.in](https://johal.in/explainable-ai-techniques-building-interpretable-models-using-shap-for-financial-risk-assessment/#:~:text=,sampling%20techniques%20without%20sacrificing%20accuracy)).  
- **Feature Correlations:** SHAP assumes features are independent. Correlated inputs can lead to unintuitive attributions or swap contributions between features ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20game,at%20an%20additional%20effort%2C%20be)).  
- **Theoretical Misranking:** Even exact Shapley values can mistakenly downplay relevant features and rank irrelevant ones higher; studies demonstrate classifiers for which SHAP is systematically misleading ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0888613X23002438#:~:text=the%20most%20widely%20used%2C%20including,routinely%20observed%20in%20ML%20classifiers)).  
- **Adversarial Manipulation:** As noted, the algorithms can be *gamed* by crafted inputs or models ([www.edps.europa.eu](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2023-11-16-techdispatch-22023-explainable-artificial-intelligence_en#:~:text=There%20is%2C%20however%2C%20research%20that,apart%20and%20a%20malicious%20developer)).  
- **Causality vs Correlation:** SHAP captures model correlations, not causal structure ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=model%20added%20to%20the%20workflow%2C,does%20not%20explain%20causal%20relationships)) – regulators are sometimes wary of “correlation-based” explanations if they are claimed as causal.  

# Regulatory and Compliance Considerations  

Given these strengths and limits, where do SHAP explanations fit into regulatory compliance?  On one hand, regulators **expect** high-risk models to be as transparent as feasible, and SHAP (among other tools) has become a de-facto standard way to provide that transparency.  For example, SHAP has been integrated into model-risk management workshops across Europe, and it is explicitly mentioned by central banks (e.g. Bank of England uses SHAP) as a way to “understand and communicate feature importance at a global and local level” ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20game,at%20an%20additional%20effort%2C%20be)).  Many guidance documents (e.g. from EAR or FDIC) essentially treat SHAP as a legitimate explanation technique for credit/anti-fraud models, provided it is used responsibly.  

On the other hand, regulators clearly recognize that *“explainability is essential for … regulatory compliance”* but that typical XAI methods cannot give a *perfect* explanation ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=The%20increasing%20adoption%20of%20artificial,While%20there%20are%20existing%20explainability)).  The BIS note specifically acknowledges that while advanced AI is being adopted, its “limited explainability… poses significant challenges” for compliance ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=The%20increasing%20adoption%20of%20artificial,While%20there%20are%20existing%20explainability)).  Crucially, recent regulatory papers emphasize that explainability tools have “notable limitations, including inaccuracy, instability and susceptibility of misleading explanations” ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=,and%20susceptibility%20of%20misleading%20explanations)).  

What does this mean in practice?  Regulators (and auditors) will not simply accept SHAP outputs as foolproof evidence.  They will look for robust model governance: independent validation, benchmarking, documentation of data and fairness tests, etc.  If a bank’s SHAP dashboard says “age 50 increased risk by +0.2,” an examiner might ask: how stable is that value?  What if age is correlated with income; could the SHAP attributions be shifting arbitrarily?  They would also be aware of attacks: as EDPS notes, adversarial perturbations can fool SHAP ([www.edps.europa.eu](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2023-11-16-techdispatch-22023-explainable-artificial-intelligence_en#:~:text=There%20is%2C%20however%2C%20research%20that,apart%20and%20a%20malicious%20developer)).  In short, **regulators expect that any explanation tool is vetted, tested, and supplemented by controls**.  

In fact, recent guidance suggests regulators accept a trade‐off: an opaque model with superior accuracy can be used *if* it is tightly governed ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=a%20need%20to%20recognise%20trade,manage%20risks%20and%20enhance%20client)).  The BIS paper explicitly says that jurisdictions may **recognize the trade-off** between explainability and performance, *“so long as risks are properly assessed and effectively managed”* ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=a%20need%20to%20recognise%20trade,manage%20risks%20and%20enhance%20client)).  That implies banks can get away with “limited explainability” provided they bolster model risk management (stress testing, fallback rules, human oversight, etc.).  Put differently, SHAP’s approximations must be embedded in a risk framework.  If the SHAP explanation is likely to be biased or noisy, the bank should either reduce model complexity or add compensating processes.  

It’s worth noting that some regulatory texts (e.g. EU AI Act) do not explicitly say “you must use SHAP” or any specific metric.  They talk about *transparency obligations* and giving users meaningful information on logic.  Whether a SHAP chart by itself satisfies, say, a GDPR “right to explanation” is debated – many lawyers argue it falls short of a truly *legal* explanation.  But clearly, organizations are using SHAP-type results to meet those standards in practice, and regulators have not banned them.  Instead, authorities urge caution: for example, the European Data Protection Supervisor and others highlight that post-hoc XAI outputs are only *one part* of an overall compliance strategy ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=,and%20susceptibility%20of%20misleading%20explanations)) ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391150327_Reimagining_Compliance_Explainable_AI_Models_for_Financial_Regulatory_Audits#:~:text=context%20of%20regulatory%20compliance%20,opacity%20of%20these%20systems%20poses)).

# Conclusion  

In summary, interpretability algorithms (SHAP, LIME, surrogate models, etc.) **do** provide a way to preserve high predictive accuracy while gaining some transparency into risk models.  They let models remain technically unconstrained (so long as you use SHAP after training) and still yield feature‐attribution explanations to stakeholders.  This mixed approach can in many cases satisfy business and some regulatory demands without sacrificing performance ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=There%20is%20also%20a%20variety,%282020)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Deutsche%20Bundesbank,case%20and%20to%20the%20stakeholders)).  

However, it is equally clear that SHAP’s own approximation **errors and assumptions** mean it cannot wholly replace the need for sound controls.  As one study starkly puts it, SHAP can “yield misleading information about the relative importance of features” in even simple cases ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0888613X23002438#:~:text=the%20most%20widely%20used%2C%20including,routinely%20observed%20in%20ML%20classifiers)).  Regulators have taken note: they acknowledge XAI is helpful but call out its inaccuracy and potential instability ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=,and%20susceptibility%20of%20misleading%20explanations)).  Thus, relying *solely* on SHAP for regulatory compliance is risky.  A prudent approach is to use SHAP as one tool (with sufficient sampling and audits) while also employing model validation, monitoring, and simpler baseline models as backstops.  

**In short:** SHAP and similar tools *aim* to balance accuracy with transparency, and they **can** help models meet interpretability demands.  But any approximation error, instability or “gaming” in the SHAP output may indeed undermine that effort if unchecked.  To remain compliant, firms must recognize SHAP’s limitations, validate its explanations, and integrate it into a broader model-risk governance regime ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919207/#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Deutsche%20Bundesbank,case%20and%20to%20the%20stakeholders)) ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm#:~:text=,and%20susceptibility%20of%20misleading%20explanations)).  Simply put, SHAP is helpful but not a silver bullet – its approximation errors must be managed so they do not jeopardize trust or regulatory acceptance.
