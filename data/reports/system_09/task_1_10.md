# Nanocarrier Size and Targeted Delivery

Nanoparticle drug carriers must be engineered so they circulate stably in blood yet still permeate target tissues and enter cells.  Particle **size** (and its distribution) is a key control knob.  A narrow, well‐controlled size range (low polydispersity) ensures that most carriers lie in an optimal window: large enough to avoid rapid renal clearance, yet small enough to penetrate “leaky” target vasculature and cell membranes.  In practice this means keeping most nanocarriers roughly in the tens-to-hundreds of nanometers range; carriers much smaller or larger than this behave very differently and perform poorly.  As one review notes, *“average particle diameter and the polydispersity index (PDI) … determine [nanocarrier] stability, efficacy, and biodistribution”* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=attributes%20of%20lipid,PDI%20and%20size%20stability%2C%20among)).  A controlled, narrow size distribution thus improves colloidal stability (preventing aggregation or sudden changes) and makes delivery more predictable.  

## Serum Protein Corona vs. Cell Uptake

Upon injection, nanoparticles are instantly coated by serum proteins (the *protein corona*), which gives them a new “biological identity” that governs their fate ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9812987/#:~:text=challenge%20is%20that%20the%20particles,issues%20of%20protein%20corona%20and)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714949/#:~:text=only%20favor%20small%20particles%20,into%20consideration%2C%20especially%20when%20designing)).  Size strongly affects this corona: for example, smaller nanoparticles (with higher curvature) often bind proteins more avidly, whereas larger particles develop **thicker** coronas.  Deng *et al.* found that modest increases in AuNP diameter (e.g. from 20 to 50–100 nm) significantly increased the binding constant and thickness of the adsorbed protein layer ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714949/#:~:text=only%20favor%20small%20particles%20,into%20consideration%2C%20especially%20when%20designing)).  In general, then, large nanocarriers accumulate a heavier protein coating.  This has two key consequences: (1) corona formation **masks targeting ligands** and reduces direct NP–cell interactions ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9812987/#:~:text=challenge%20is%20that%20the%20particles,issues%20of%20protein%20corona%20and)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9812987/#:~:text=PC%20affects%20the%20cellular%20uptake,In%20LNPs%20with%20two)), and (2) a heavy corona (especially opsonins) flags the particle for rapid clearance by phagocytic cells (liver/spleen).  Indeed, one study showed that a serum corona *“lowers the surface energy and decreases the NP–cell membrane interaction, thereby reducing NP uptake,”* and that this inhibitory effect grew worse with larger particle size ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9812987/#:~:text=PC%20affects%20the%20cellular%20uptake,In%20LNPs%20with%20two)).  For example, 50 nm gold nanoparticles saw a much bigger drop in cellular uptake when corona-coated, compared to 5 nm or 20 nm ones ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9812987/#:~:text=uptake%20,addition%2C%20the%20surface%20chemistry%20and)).  Thus, large carriers tend to be more strongly “opsonized” and blocked from cell entry, while very small carriers (with high surface area) may bind copious protein but still penetrate membranes relatively easily.  

## Size-Dependent Clearance and Permeability

Size also determines biodistribution via physiological filters. *Very* small particles (<~10 nm) are rapidly **cleared by the kidneys** (glomerular pores are ~10–15 nm) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=endothelial%20lining%20of%20kidney%20capillaries,spleen%20and%20to%20some%20extent)).  At the same time, they have huge surface area for protein adsorption and can pick up extensive coronas (and risk aggregation or unexpected immune recognition).  At the other extreme, *very* large particles (hundreds of nanometers) cannot exit normal capillaries and get trapped or taken up by immune cells.  In fact, one guideline is that only nanocarriers **≤100–150 nm** can readily extravasate through the discontinuous (“leaky”) capillaries of tumors or certain organs ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=circulating%20nanocarriers%20smaller%20than%20around,nm%20for%20passive%20targeting%20tumour)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=Relative%20sizes%20of%20particles%20and,size%20of%20the%20lipidic%20carriers)). Carriers much above 100–150 nm largely **remain in circulation or are sequestered** in organs (liver, spleen) by phagocytes.  As one review summarizes, *“vesicle size is one of the main parameters that determines clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES). The rate of uptake by immune system cells increases with increasing carrier size.”* ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=Relative%20sizes%20of%20particles%20and,size%20of%20the%20lipidic%20carriers)).  Conversely, particles in the ~20–100 nm range strike a balance: small enough to diffuse through leaky vasculature (supporting enhanced permeability and retention, EPR) but large enough to avoid immediate renal loss.  For example, shrinking liposomes below ~50 nm in diameter has been shown to *“greatly reduce mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS)–mediated clearance”* and prolong circulation, without sacrificing EPR delivery ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=It%20has%20been%20reported%20that,empty)).  

In summary, **size governs stability vs. permeability**:  
- Particles **<10 nm**: Very high permeability out of blood, but *very short circulation time* due to renal excretion ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=endothelial%20lining%20of%20kidney%20capillaries,spleen%20and%20to%20some%20extent)) and massive corona effects.  
- Particles **~20–50 nm**: Excellent tissue penetration and still relatively long-lived in blood; moderate protein adsorption. Often a sweet spot for transporter-like carriers.  
- Particles **~50–150 nm**: Optimal for tumor targeting via EPR ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=circulating%20nanocarriers%20smaller%20than%20around,nm%20for%20passive%20targeting%20tumour)); less renal loss. But above ~50 nm, additional corona layers and phagocytic uptake begin to rise.  
- Particles **>150–200 nm**: Poor tissue extravasation (trapped in capillaries) and strong uptake by immune cells ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=Relative%20sizes%20of%20particles%20and,size%20of%20the%20lipidic%20carriers)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=circulating%20nanocarriers%20smaller%20than%20around,nm%20for%20passive%20targeting%20tumour)). Large corona layers can further slow or prevent cellular uptake.  

## Balancing the “Corona vs. Uptake” Trade-off

Because serum proteins tend to coat larger particles, while cell uptake tends to favor smaller ones, the two processes compete.  By **controlling the size distribution** (biasing toward an optimal window), one can tilt this balance in favor of delivery.  A narrow distribution centered around ~20–80 nm, for instance, ensures that most carriers can both extravasate into target tissues and engage cells, without being swallowed by the liver or the kidneys.  It also makes the corona more uniform: a monodisperse population accumulates a predictable corona **thickness** (and composition) rather than a mix of thick/coated and thin/lightly-coated particles.  This uniformity helps maintain targeting ligand accessibility.  In practice, formulation methods (homogenization, extrusion, microfluidics, etc.) are used to achieve low polydispersity.  As one review notes, *“constant and narrow size distribution are necessary to achieve optimum clinical outcomes,”* and in particular *size stability* is crucial to keep nanosystems from aggregating ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=for%20evaluating%20the%20stability%20of,of%20available%20techniques%20for%20the)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=attributes%20of%20lipid,PDI%20and%20size%20stability%2C%20among)).  

Together, these effects mean that well-controlled size distribution **optimizes** both stability *and* permeability.  The carriers are small enough to navigate pores and membranes (maximizing delivery to target cells), yet large/uniform enough to survive long in blood and avoid uncontrolled clearance.  The result is improved targeted delivery: fewer particles are lost to nonspecific protein binding or phagocytic uptake, while more reach and enter the intended cells.  

**Key points:**  Carefully engineering nanocarriers with a narrow size range (often on the order of tens of nm) keeps them in the “Goldilocks zone” for drug delivery.  In this zone, carriers form only moderate protein coronas that do not completely shield targeting ligands ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9812987/#:~:text=challenge%20is%20that%20the%20particles,issues%20of%20protein%20corona%20and)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714949/#:~:text=only%20favor%20small%20particles%20,into%20consideration%2C%20especially%20when%20designing)), and they are sized to exploit leaky vasculature (EPR) without being filtered out ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=endothelial%20lining%20of%20kidney%20capillaries,spleen%20and%20to%20some%20extent)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=circulating%20nanocarriers%20smaller%20than%20around,nm%20for%20passive%20targeting%20tumour)).  This size control thereby balances the competing demands of *stability* (long circulation, low aggregation) and *permeability* (effective extravasation and cell uptake), maximizing the therapeutic index of the nanomedicine ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=attributes%20of%20lipid,PDI%20and%20size%20stability%2C%20among)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=Relative%20sizes%20of%20particles%20and,size%20of%20the%20lipidic%20carriers)).  

**References:** Experimental and review studies of nanoparticle delivery consistently highlight size and PDI as critical factors for biodistribution ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=attributes%20of%20lipid,PDI%20and%20size%20stability%2C%20among)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=circulating%20nanocarriers%20smaller%20than%20around,nm%20for%20passive%20targeting%20tumour)).  Small shifts in diameter dramatically alter protein adsorption and uptake ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7664725/#:~:text=Particle%20size%2C%20total%20surface%20area,as%20a%20result%20of%20the)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714949/#:~:text=only%20favor%20small%20particles%20,into%20consideration%2C%20especially%20when%20designing)).  Thus tuning the size distribution is a proven strategy to optimize targeted nanomedicine performance in balance with serum protein competition ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9812987/#:~:text=challenge%20is%20that%20the%20particles,issues%20of%20protein%20corona%20and)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6027495/#:~:text=Relative%20sizes%20of%20particles%20and,size%20of%20the%20lipidic%20carriers)).
