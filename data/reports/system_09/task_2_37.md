In practice, designers have found that the most energy‐efficient way to keep cabin air biologically clean is to *chemically* inactivate organisms – rather than simply stuffing the flow through ultra-dense filters.  For example, NASA engineers pioneered UV/photocatalytic systems (TiO₂ + UV light) in the ISS plant chambers; these reactors destroy bacteria, viruses and spores in the air with only a few‐watt UV lamps.  In one NASA report, the TiO₂/UV “photocatalytic oxidation” process (originally developed to scrub ethylene from plant chambers) proved effective against “bacteria, viruses, and molds” ([spinoff.nasa.gov](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/Air_Treatment_Systems_Break_Down_Pollutants_Germs#:~:text=Anderson%20wanted%20to%20break%20down,also%20bacteria%2C%20viruses%2C%20and%20molds)).  Because the microbes are killed by the UV-driven catalyst, the ventilation can then rely on a much gentler particulate filter (with far lower pressure drop) downstream – yielding very high overall kill rates but far lower fan power than driving HEPA alone. 

By contrast, the ISS’s standard CHIPS filters (activated‐charcoal + HEPA) do remove >99.99% of particles ([ntrs.nasa.gov](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230017092#:~:text=International%20Space%20Station%20%28ISS%29,filters%20was%20ascertained%20at%20NASA)), but at the cost of a very high pressure drop and thus heavy blower power.  In a hybrid approach, a light UV-catalyst module in the airstream handles most microbes and VOCs, and a moderate-efficiency particulate pre-filter catches debris.  NASA notes that *“photocatalytic oxidation and ionizing technologies are not standalone … and must be part of a well-designed … HVAC system that includes highly effective filters”* ([spinoff.nasa.gov](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/clean-air-tech#:~:text=Marshall%20scientist%20Jay%20Perry%2C%20who,indoor%20air%20quality%2C%E2%80%9D%20Perry%20said)) – in other words, use UV/PCO *with* (not as a substitute for) filtration.  In sum, UV‐activated photocatalytic oxidation (often coupled with a modest HEPA/MERV filter) delivers near-HEPA level disinfection with far lower fan power, offering the best microbe‐vs‐energy trade-off seen in space‐flight life support ([spinoff.nasa.gov](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/Air_Treatment_Systems_Break_Down_Pollutants_Germs#:~:text=Anderson%20wanted%20to%20break%20down,also%20bacteria%2C%20viruses%2C%20and%20molds)) ([spinoff.nasa.gov](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/clean-air-tech#:~:text=Marshall%20scientist%20Jay%20Perry%2C%20who,indoor%20air%20quality%2C%E2%80%9D%20Perry%20said)). 

**Answer:**  An in-duct UV+TiO₂ photocatalytic oxidation module used alongside a modest particulate filter.  This NASA-derived hybrid system inactivates essentially all airborne microbes in the airstream but only needs a small UV lamp and lighter filter (much less fan energy) compared with “ultra‐high” HEPA filtration alone ([spinoff.nasa.gov](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/Air_Treatment_Systems_Break_Down_Pollutants_Germs#:~:text=Anderson%20wanted%20to%20break%20down,also%20bacteria%2C%20viruses%2C%20and%20molds)) ([spinoff.nasa.gov](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/clean-air-tech#:~:text=Marshall%20scientist%20Jay%20Perry%2C%20who,indoor%20air%20quality%2C%E2%80%9D%20Perry%20said)).
