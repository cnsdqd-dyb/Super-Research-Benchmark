In most interactive live‐stream systems, the “content‐switch” threshold (i.e. the minimum time a viewer must stay on one stream/content before switching) is not derived from a fundamental law but set by the system designers based on human and technical factors.  In practice it is a fixed or configurable “dwell time” on the order of a few seconds – long enough to avoid rapid flicker but short enough to feel responsive.  For example, some designs let the system or streamer set this threshold (sometimes even varying it by viewer profile).  One documented implementation explicitly uses thresholds of 3–8 seconds, chosen according to viewer attributes (e.g. 3 s for young adults, up to 8 s for older viewers) or browsing speed ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN114257824A/en#:~:text=The%20first%20preset%20time%20threshold,target%20user%20may%20also%20set)) ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN114257824A/en#:~:text=the%20first%20preset%20time%20threshold,of%20the%20target%20user%20is)).  In general, the value is chosen empirically or via user studies, and may align with video segment/key‐frame boundaries so that switches only occur cleanly.  In short, the timing threshold is a design parameter – often fine‐tuned by testing – that balances responsiveness against stability (with typical values of a few seconds, as illustrated above).
