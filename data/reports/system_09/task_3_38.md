# Regenerative Braking and Power Matching in Multi‐Train Operation  
Electric trains equipped with regenerative braking convert kinetic energy to electrical power during deceleration. In a multi‐train catenary, that regenerated energy can be **matched** to other trains’ needs (e.g. feeding accelerating trains or stored in wayside devices) or else fed back to the AC grid.  In practice, “power matching” schemes (via train timetables, onboard control or substation controllers) try to synchronize braking and acceleration so that regen energy is **absorbed locally** rather than dumped.  When train density is high, many braking and accelerating events tend to overlap, so a large fraction of regenerated energy can be reused by nearby trains. Conversely, at low density (few trains running), surplus regen energy often must go back into the supply or resistors. 

- **High train density:**  Multiple trains run simultaneously.  Braking trains can directly supply others, reducing net power drawn from or fed into the grid.  The converters on each train operate with larger fundamental currents, which tends to *reduce the relative distortion* of each converter’s output.  In effect, some distortion currents cancel or are absorbed, so less net harmonic current is injected at the substation.  
- **Low train density:**  Fewer trains means less on‐line load to accept regen energy.  Braking power is forced back into the grid or dissipated, so more trains operate in “regeneration mode” relative to accelerating modes. This results in more power electronics actively **injecting current into the grid** without a matching load.  Multiple inverters feeding back simultaneously can cause larger harmonics on the supply.

## Harmonics Under Traction vs. Braking  
Traction converters and regenerative inverters are nonlinear power electronic loads that produce harmonics.  Measured and simulated studies show regenerative‐braking operation generally produces **more harmonic distortion** than pure traction.  For example, Zhang *et al.* (2023) found that under regenerative braking the *current harmonic distortion* was “much higher” than under traction  ([www.eurekaselect.com](https://www.eurekaselect.com/article/132486#:~:text=Results%3A%20Under%20the%20regenerative%20braking,the%20traction%20operation%20of%20the)), and the train’s *power factor* fell (i.e. more reactive and distorted current).  Similarly, Xu (2017) reports that AC–DC–AC locomotives in braking mode draw very low fundamental current, so the *percentage* of low‐order harmonics is high; in fact “under regenerative braking…the feeder current THD is large” ## Impact of Power Matching on Grid Distortion  
Putting these observations together: **how well regen power is matched to loads affects how much harmonic current ends up on the utility side.**  When many trains run (high density) and scheduling or controllers successfully balance regen with traction, the net power fed onto the grid is smaller. In that case, even though each braking train’s inverter produces harmonics, those currents largely circulate *within the traction network* or are absorbed by other trains. Empirical experience (and models) suggest this reduces the *net harmonic injection* at the point of common coupling.  

By contrast, if train density drops or if matching is poor, regenerated energy “spills” into the grid. Each regenerative converter then presents a non-linear pulse current into the public supply.  The cumulative effect is higher THD at the substation: more trains in full braking without matching consumer means more distortion current drawn from the grid. 

In summary, **good regenerative-power matching (common in dense traffic) helps mitigate grid harmonics**, while mismatches (common in sparse traffic) exacerbate them.  Poorly matched regen forces the converters to hunt for a path, injecting larger high-order currents into the grid and raising harmonic distortion.  This is why railway power systems often actively manage the timing or storage of regeneration (and use filters) – to smooth out these effects and protect the grid’s voltage waveform.

back, resulting in greater grid harmonic distortion
