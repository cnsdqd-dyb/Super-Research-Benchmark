# Latency and the “Speed Race” in Continuous Markets  

Most modern exchanges use price–time priority in a continuous *limit order book* (LOB). By design, this model **rewards the fastest traders**.  As one analysis notes, in a continuous auction “the model rewards speed: the fastest traders can… be at the top of the order queue or avoid being picked off when prices move in real time” ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=The%20most%20common%20trading%20model,in%20a%20%204%20CFA)). In practice, data updates (the NBBO) never reach all participants at once.  There is literally *no universal agreement on price at any given instant* ([www.iex.io](https://www.iex.io/article/modern-day-latency-arbitrage-predicting-price-changes#:~:text=participants%20%E2%80%94%20again%2C%20including%20the,exact%20same%20moment%20in%20time)).  Faster firms (with co‐location, microwave links, etc.) see price changes slightly sooner, and thus can trade against *stale quotes* from slower participants.  Such “latency arbitrage” windows allow low-risk profits for HFTs at the expense of slower investors ([www.iex.io](https://www.iex.io/article/modern-day-latency-arbitrage-predicting-price-changes#:~:text=The%20discrepancy%20among%20different%20views,more%20slowly%20than%20the%20fastest)) ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=feature%20and%20mitigates%20so,unfair%20or%20increase%20transaction%20costs)).  In fact, one model of a US‐UK stock pair finds that under today’s continuous design **96% of all trades** would be exploitable by a faster trader (only ~4% would execute truly simultaneously) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=latencies%20Albany,56%20%28right)).  This built-in speed advantage has been called a fundamental flaw of continuous trading (Budish *et al.* 2015) – essentially an arms race that confers “mechanical arbitrage” rents to whoever is fastest.  

When these extreme speed gaps exist, **price discovery can suffer for ordinary investors**.  Empirical studies report that latency arbitrage accounts for ~20% of trading volume on some venues and imposes about **0.5 basis points** of extra cost per trade ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=,5%20BP%2C%20that)).  In relative terms, that added cost is roughly a 20–33% increase in effective bid–ask spreads (e.g. spreads rising from 3.0 bp to 3.5 bp, a ~17% increase in trading cost) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=latency%20arbitrage%20represents%20a%200,costs%20would%20arguably%20reduce%20market)).  In other words, a continuous LOB with a big speed gap is like having a hidden tax benefiting ultra-fast traders.  (That said, HFTs are not purely parasitic: aggressive HFT firms often do provide some price discovery by immediately trading on new information.  Some studies find HFTs account for ~10–14% of new information impounded into price ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1386418116300672#:~:text=HFTs%20collectively%20account%20for%2014,related%20price%20discovery)).  But even if their information processing is positive, the near-guarantee of stale-quote arbitrage remains a serious issue in continuous markets.)  

## Design Fixes: Slowing the Race  

The good news is that matching engines *can* be redesigned to blunt these advantages. One approach is simply **slowing down all traders slightly or batching orders**, so that tiny speed differences no longer matter.  For example, the IEX exchange introduced a fixed *“speed bump”* (~350 microseconds) on all orders.  This micro-delay deters sub-millisecond latency arbitrage: IEX itself advertises that the delay “mitigates so-called *‘latency arbitrage’* strategies in which fast… traders profit from trading against stale quotations” ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=feature%20and%20mitigates%20so,unfair%20or%20increase%20transaction%20costs)).  Importantly, academic observers note that even a random or small delay can quash the HFT arms race: Professor Larry Harris argues in *Financial Analysts Journal* that a slight randomized lag would “eliminate the costly HFT arms race, whilst still allowing markets… to benefit from the sharp reductions in trading costs” earned by automation ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=Moreover%2C%20the%20concept%20of%20slowing,automated%20trading%20strategies%20have%20brought)).  In practice, IEX’s speed-bump has attracted some market share (from <0.1% in 2013 to ~1.6% in 2015 ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=If%20the%20aforementioned%20speed%20bump,in%20October%202015))) by offering a fairer playing field. 

Other proposals take this further.  Budish *et al.* (2015) suggest replacing continuous matching with **frequent batch auctions** (e.g. clearing orders every 50–100 ms).  By forcing traders to compete at discrete time points, any microsecond speed edge becomes worthless – you only get in the next batch, not ahead of it.  This “discrete time” model transforms the contest from racing to beat prices to simply providing the best order at the auction.  Similarly, recent theory has devised an explicit “timestamped order” type: traders submit orders specifying *when* in the future they should execute.  Using real latency data, such models show that by choosing a suitable delay one could make **~97–99% of cross-venue orders execute simultaneously** – compared to only ~4% under today’s design ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=_L%3D25.7%5C%2C%5Ctextrm,on%20the%20contemporary%20market%20design)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=latencies%20Albany,56%20%28right)).  In fact, as the chosen delay grows, the probability of simultaneous execution approaches 100% (and the chance for a fast trader to get a head start goes to 0) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=Equations%20,pi%20_S%3D0%5C%29.%20%5C%28%5Csquare)). 

In summary, these design changes (speed bumps, discrete auctions, execution-delay orders, etc.) can largely **neutralize tiny latency advantages**. They do so at the cost of reduced top‐tier speed (everyone is slowed to a common speed or batched together). But markets *can* trade off a bit of throughput for fairness: as the CFA blog notes, “plenty recognize that speed-based trading is *not always* the most effective market model” ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=Moreover%2C%20the%20concept%20of%20slowing,automated%20trading%20strategies%20have%20brought)) ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=The%20most%20common%20trading%20model,in%20a%20%204%20CFA)). Exchanges that transparently adopt such features let investors self-select; in theory, users will choose the venue (or mode) that best balances speed vs. cost for them ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=The%20principles%20of%20transparent%2C%20nondiscretionary%2C,features%20should%20be%20somewhat%20irrelevant)) ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=If%20the%20aforementioned%20speed%20bump,in%20October%202015)). 

## Conclusion: Trade-offs, Not Fate  

In a **pure continuous** auction system, the advantage to HFTs is essentially built in, so without intervention one will *always* be small windows for latency arbitrage (and thus an incentive for manipulative front-running of stale prices) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=latencies%20Albany,56%20%28right)) ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=feature%20and%20mitigates%20so,unfair%20or%20increase%20transaction%20costs)).  In that sense, if an exchange races uncritically for nanoseconds, it *inevitably* creates attractive arbitrage opportunities.  However, it is by no means impossible to design around this.  Numerous proposals – from IEX’s speed bump to bulk auctions to advanced order types – demonstrate that a matching engine **can** balance speed with fairness if so desired.  The catch is that doing so means deliberately giving up a slice of pure speed advantage to ensure quotes are disseminated or matched in sufficiently synchronized fashion ([blogs.cfainstitute.org](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/blog/2015/12/01/iex-exchange-application-is-level-playing-field-possible-with-speed-bump/#:~:text=Moreover%2C%20the%20concept%20of%20slowing,automated%20trading%20strategies%20have%20brought)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=_L%3D25.7%5C%2C%5Ctextrm,on%20the%20contemporary%20market%20design)).  Markets today largely leave the choice up to each venue: continuous microsecond markets yield razor-thin spreads but higher dark‐pool of arbitrage rent, whereas slower or periodic systems sacrifice a bit of immediacy to improve price discovery for ordinary traders.  In short, latency-based arbitrage isn’t an inescapable force of nature—**it’s a market-design choice**. With the right matching‐engine rules, the advantage of speed can be reduced or eliminated ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=Equations%20,pi%20_S%3D0%5C%29.%20%5C%28%5Csquare)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40854-023-00491-5#:~:text=_L%3D25.7%5C%2C%5Ctextrm,on%20the%20contemporary%20market%20design)), though doing so requires conscious trade-offs and likely regulatory or industry consensus on fairness.
