# Genome editing and cross-species justice  

Classical bioethics has been anthropocentric – focused on human welfare, rights and values – but powerful new biotechnologies force us to rethink that stance.  Genome editing tools (e.g. CRISPR, gene drives) are now being used not only on human embryos but on animals and even ecosystems (for conservation, agriculture, pest control, etc.).  This raises urgent moral questions about the animals and environments affected, and whether these non-human actors have interests or rights of their own.  In response, ethicists are developing “more-than-human” frameworks that explicitly **de-center the human** in justice considerations.  For example, recent analyses argue that justice should “[include] all Earth others” – animals, plants, microbes and the ecosystems they form – and the relationships that allow them to flourish ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/institutionalising-multispecies-justice/509E27E9B328360ABDEEA184EE0492DE#:~:text=Multispecies%20Justice%20,the%20prefigurative%20work%20happening%20within)).  In other words, true justice is not limited to people.  

Traditional ethical debates about gene editing often treated animals instrumentally: animals were edited solely as means to human ends (e.g. livestock with faster growth, mosquitoes eradicated to prevent malaria).  Critics now point out that this “instrumentalization” violates the animals’ intrinsic value.  As one systematic review notes, genome editing “instrumentaliz[es] animals by using them as mere objects to serve human purposes,” ignoring that animals “have intrinsic value” ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271/#:~:text=harmed%20in%20other%20ways,editing%20could%20be%20viewed%20as)).  Such critique reflects a shift toward cross-species thinking: instead of treating animals only in terms of human benefit, ethicists insist we respect species-specific capacities and welfare.  For instance, editing an animal’s genome could be seen as an affront to its *telos* (essence) or dignity, preventing it from living according to its nature ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271/#:~:text=could%20impinge%20on%20animals%27%20dignity,ZFNs%2C%20TALEN%2C%20CRISPR)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271/#:~:text=harmed%20in%20other%20ways,editing%20could%20be%20viewed%20as)).  From this perspective, even a welfare gain (e.g. making pigs healthier) might be morally offset if the animal’s ability to behave naturally or exercise its species-typical “capabilities” is lost.  

## Beyond anthropocentrism: multispecies justice frameworks  

In response to these challenges, scholars are proposing new justice frameworks that **transcend human exceptionalism**.  A rich literature on *multispecies* or *interspecies justice* calls for moral consideration of all beings, human and nonhuman alike.  As one recent review argues, the anthropocentric framing “renders invisible the myriad non-humans that are central to the viability of ecosystem services and human survival” ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-1547/15/4/45#:~:text=health%2C%20this%20anthropocentrism%20renders%20invisible,humans)).  Interspecies justice urges us to ask: who and what are included in the scope of justice?  Instead of centering humans, these approaches seek to “decentralize the human” and “centralize more-than-human” interests ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40592-024-00197-z#:~:text=In%20this%20article%2C%20building%20on,important%20role%20in%20environmental%20and)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40592-024-00197-z#:~:text=justice%2C%20offering%20anthropological%20narratives%20throughout,human)).  For example, a “multispecies justice” perspective might grant standing or rights to ecosystems and animals (some legal scholars are indeed extending rights to rivers, forests or entire biomes) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40592-024-00197-z#:~:text=of%20each%20more,realities%20of%20communities%20most%20implicated)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40592-024-00197-z#:~:text=such%20as%20the%20rights%20of,impacts%20of%20their%20judicial%20decisions)).  Also, philosophers like Martha Nussbaum have extended the *capabilities approach* to animals, suggesting each species has its own essential capabilities to flourish.  Ethicists propose measuring the impact of gene editing on those capabilities: will the edited animal still be able to live and behave as its kind does naturally?  By refocusing on “ecosystem capabilities” rather than mere “ecosystem services”, we put nonhuman needs “on equal footing with those of humans” ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-025-00205-z#:~:text=We%20thus%20propose%20a%20critical,term%20viability.%20Our%20findings%20contribute)).  

Concretely, genome editing ethics is beginning to adopt these ideas.  For example, debates about gene drives in mosquitos now explicitly weigh human health against the ecological role of the mosquito and its value in nature – an appraisal impossible under a strictly human-centered view.  Similarly, editing pigs for organ transplants raises questions not just of human benefit but of the pig’s welfare and dignity.  In all these cases, ethicists argue that decision-making must move *beyond* anthropocentric utilitarian calculations.  They advocate principles like precaution and solidarity that span species: we share a “co‐existence with other species” and thus bear duties to them ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-1547/15/4/45#:~:text=Interspecies%20justice%20is%20not%20consistently,that%20have%20animated%20Indigenous%20knowledge)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-1547/15/4/45#:~:text=health%2C%20this%20anthropocentrism%20renders%20invisible,humans)).  

## Rebuilding justice foundations  

In effect, genome editing ethics is helping **rebuild the foundations of justice** to be truly cross-species.  Instead of starting from the idea that only humans count, the new frameworks start from the assumption that “justice is not limited to humans” ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/institutionalising-multispecies-justice/509E27E9B328360ABDEEA184EE0492DE#:~:text=Multispecies%20Justice%20,the%20prefigurative%20work%20happening%20within)).  This means crafting institutions and policies that respect nonhuman interests.  For instance, some proposals call for inclusive governance structures where even representatives of environmental and animal interests have a voice in determining gene-editing projects.  Others suggest formalizing duties of care toward wild ecosystems that might be altered by human biotechnology.  In every case, the goal is to transform our moral imagination: to see animals, plants and microbes not as mere background resources, but as stakeholders in justice.  

In summary, **cross-species justice** reframes genome editing ethics by decentering people.  It emphasizes that altering a species’ genome implicates that species in justice issues, not just humans.  As one bioethics review puts it, we need a “radical reorientation” away from human exceptionalism ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40592-024-00197-z#:~:text=In%20this%20article%2C%20building%20on,important%20role%20in%20environmental%20and)).  This new orientation leads us to demand fairness and care across species lines.  Genome editing, far from being just a medical technology, thus becomes a catalyst for a wider moral revolution – one that envisions a justice inclusive of all life on Earth ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/institutionalising-multispecies-justice/509E27E9B328360ABDEEA184EE0492DE#:~:text=Multispecies%20Justice%20,the%20prefigurative%20work%20happening%20within)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-025-00205-z#:~:text=We%20thus%20propose%20a%20critical,term%20viability.%20Our%20findings%20contribute)).  

**Key ideas:** Traditional genome-editing debates are human-centered, but editing animals/ecosystems forces a broader view.  Emerging “multispecies” or “interspecies” justice theories insist animals and environments count in justice, challenging anthropocentrism.  In practice, this means acknowledging animal dignity and ecosystem integrity in gene-editing decisions, extending rights or capabilities-based thinking to nonhumans, and crafting new laws/policies that care for all species, not just people ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452271/#:~:text=harmed%20in%20other%20ways,editing%20could%20be%20viewed%20as)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-1547/15/4/45#:~:text=health%2C%20this%20anthropocentrism%20renders%20invisible,humans)) ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/institutionalising-multispecies-justice/509E27E9B328360ABDEEA184EE0492DE#:~:text=Multispecies%20Justice%20,the%20prefigurative%20work%20happening%20within)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42949-025-00205-z#:~:text=We%20thus%20propose%20a%20critical,term%20viability.%20Our%20findings%20contribute)).
