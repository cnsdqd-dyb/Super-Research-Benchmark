**Adsorption‐energy distribution** refers to the range of binding strengths with which electrode surfaces (catalysts, supports, GDLs, etc.) hold molecules (reactants, water, ions, impurities).  In a catalyst layer or porous support, different sites (e.g. micropores vs. larger pores, different crystal facets) have different adsorption energies.  A broad distribution means some sites bind species much more strongly (high adsorption energy) and others much more weakly. Under thermal swings, this heterogeneity becomes a liability.  Strongly adsorbed species – especially water or acid – can remain trapped at cold temperatures or crystallize in tiny pores, then unexpectedly expand or desorb at high temperature.  Over many freeze–thaw or heat–cool cycles, these effects strain the materials. For example, in microporous carbons narrow pores fill first and hold water in a “solid‐phase” (ice‐like) form ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jp014388b#:~:text=The%20phenomenon%20of%20water%20adsorption,3)).  These solidified pockets expand, deforming pore structure and creating cracks.  Indeed, freeze–thaw tests have shown that repeated cycling *“volumetrically expand[es] the small-scale pores”* in catalyst layers, shrinking active area and sharply raising ohmic/charge-transfer resistances ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360319918334724#:~:text=are%20reported%20to%20negatively%20affect,placement%20is%20only%20beneficial%20to)).  In short, high-energy adsorption sites (like tiny pores or strongly binding facets) tend to trap water or other species that damage the electrode during temperature swings. 

Because of this, **durability design is constrained** by the need to limit extreme adsorption.  In practice, engineers avoid materials with excessive microporosity or overly high surface reactivity.  Supports and GDLs are made more hydrophobic or more meso-porous so that water is less strongly bound.  Ionomers and hydrophobic treatments are chosen to prevent water condensation in tight pores.  Thermal‐cycling tests bear this out: aggressive cycling (e.g. 50–160 °C) drives voltage loss rates up to ~50–75 μV/hr (versus ~14 μV/hr under steady 160 °C) and causes large rises in ohmic resistance ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378775324013636#:~:text=peaking%20at%2075.6%20%5Cmathrm,underscore%20the%20crucial%20need%20for)).  That rise is largely due to membrane drying and pore distortion under cycling, a direct consequence of uneven adsorption/desorption. 

In sum, the adsorption‐energy spectrum sets the “weakest links” in a fuel cell.  Sites that bind species very strongly (high adsorption energy) or very weakly (low adsorption energy) can fail under thermal stress.  Thus durable fuel‐cell designs under thermal cycling *minimize* such extremes – for example by using catalyst supports with controlled pore size distributions and surfaces engineered for uniform, moderate adsorption energies ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jp014388b#:~:text=The%20phenomenon%20of%20water%20adsorption,3)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360319918334724#:~:text=are%20reported%20to%20negatively%20affect,placement%20is%20only%20beneficial%20to)).  This ensures that adsorption/desorption of water and reactants happens more gently and uniformly with temperature, greatly improving freeze–thaw and heat–cool stability.  

**References:** Studies of carbon supports and GDLs under freeze–thaw show that water in micropores (high E_ads sites) freezes into a solid-like phase ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jp014388b#:~:text=The%20phenomenon%20of%20water%20adsorption,3)) and that repeated cycling “volumetrically expand[s]” small pores, raising resistances ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360319918334724#:~:text=are%20reported%20to%20negatively%20affect,placement%20is%20only%20beneficial%20to)).  Likewise, high-temperature PEM tests report much faster degradation and rising ohmic resistance when cycled (50–160 °C) versus steady temperature ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378775324013636#:~:text=peaking%20at%2075.6%20%5Cmathrm,underscore%20the%20crucial%20need%20for)), underscoring that thermal swings amplify the stresses set by the adsorption‐energy distribution.
