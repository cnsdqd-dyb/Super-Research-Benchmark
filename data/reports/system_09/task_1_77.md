# Thermodynamic limits and entropy in circular economies

Thermodynamic laws set **fundamental limits** on how efficiently any cyclic process can run.  In a closed system at constant temperature and pressure, *Gibbs free energy* (G = H – T S) always decreases in a spontaneous process until equilibrium (ΔG = 0) is reached.  Equivalently, the Second Law demands total entropy ↑ over time.  In practice this means **no real process is perfectly reversible**, and 100% of a product or material can never be reconverted to its original form without **external energy input**.  In other words, the closer an economy’s recycling loops approach “perfect circularity,” the more energy must be supplied to overcome ever-growing entropy.  

- **Free energy vs. entropy.**  At constant T,P, minimizing Gibbs free energy corresponds to maximizing entropy (no remaining ability to do work).  Systems with low entropy have a high fraction of *free* (usable) energy, whereas higher entropy means most energy is “unavailable” ([www.wisdomlib.org](https://www.wisdomlib.org/science/journal/sustainability-journal-mdpi/d/doc1807133.html#:~:text=the%20initial%20and%20final%20states,of%20usable%20energy%20they%20contain)).  Thus, **every time we reuse or refine a material, we consume free energy and increase entropy**.  For example, assembling raw materials into an ordered product always requires energy, and even then “more energy will have to be used than needed for the production, since a part of it needs to be dissipated as heat” ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=with%20the%20thermodynamics,entropy%20and%20a%20spontaneous%20process)).  In disposing or recycling that product, its constituents become more dispersed (higher entropy) unless we expend further energy.  As Walter explains, any process that *increases order* (lowers system entropy) must send even more entropy into the environment ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=Processes%20that%20create%20more%20disorder,machine%20or%20factory%20can%20be)).  

- **Inevitably irreversible cycles.**  A truly lossless circular process (zero net entropy change) is a limiting ideal – it parallels a Carnot heat engine operating reversibly, or a perfectly reversible chemical cycle.  But real “loops” always generate entropy.  In fact, studies emphasize that **“material and energy dissipation will always happen”** in practice ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=Therefore%2C%20I%20doubt%20very%20much,practically%29%20inexhaustible%20energy%20source)).  Every pump, furnace or recycling plant operates below the Carnot efficiency (ε<1) due to irreversibilities ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=thermodynamic%20necessity%2C%20which%20is%20typically,conversion%20efficiency%20for%20every%20process)).  As a consequence, one **must continuously input energy** to sustain any cyclic process.  Walter notes that even if we repeatedly recycle a product (say, melting and reforming plastic or metal), energy is always lost and must be replaced – “it is simply not possible to reuse all the dissipated energy and thus energy is constantly needed as input” ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=the%20raw%20materials%2C%20such%20as,a%20circular%20economy%20in%20practice)).  In effect, **each recycling step requires external work**, and yields waste heat or lower-grade materials (entropy).  

- **Scaling and diminishing returns.**  These thermodynamic losses grow with scale.  To recover or “upcycle” each additional tonne of material, the required energy (and entropy production) rises, often nonlinearly.  For instance, extracting trace metals from used electronics or purifying heavily mixed plastics demands huge energy inputs relative to virgin production ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=the%20raw%20materials%2C%20such%20as,a%20circular%20economy%20in%20practice)) ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=Therefore%2C%20I%20doubt%20very%20much,practically%29%20inexhaustible%20energy%20source)).  As Walter points out, approaching perfect circularity forces an economy toward infinite energy demand: “in a real implementation… enormous amounts of energy would be required in order to enable complete circularity. *The closer one gets to perfect circularity the more energy is needed*” ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=Therefore%2C%20I%20doubt%20very%20much,practically%29%20inexhaustible%20energy%20source)).  Similarly, Korhonen et al. note that while moving to cyclical flows can greatly improve resource use, **“a cyclic flow does not secure a sustainable outcome”** – every loop must be analyzed for its net entropy cost ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Korhonen%20et,this%20is%20so%20even%20in)).  In short, **there is a practical ceiling on efficiency**: because of entropy, one can mitigate but not eliminate waste. Perfect recycling would imply ΔS_total = 0, which is thermodynamically forbidden for real processes.  

- **Gibbs free energy’s role.**  Gibbs free energy minimization itself is a statement of equilibrium: a truly self-sustaining loop would have ΔG=0 around the cycle, meaning no net work output.  But to keep circulating materials (prevent them from settling at equilibrium), free energy must be repeatedly supplied.  In chemical terms, if a recycling reaction has ΔG>0 (non‐spontaneous), external energy is required; if ΔG<0 (spontaneous), it will proceed until equilibrium (ΔG=0) and then stall.  Thus, on balance, **the economy must draw on new low-entropy energy** (e.g. fuel, sunlight, electricity) to compensate for every entropy increase.  Without that input, resources would 'relax' into waste forms of minimum G, halting any further work.  In practice this means a growing circular economy still burns energy (and increases entropy) to keep run­ning. As one review puts it: *“entropy is the measure of unavailable energy, with free energy corresponding to low entropy”* ([www.wisdomlib.org](https://www.wisdomlib.org/science/journal/sustainability-journal-mdpi/d/doc1807133.html#:~:text=the%20initial%20and%20final%20states,of%20usable%20energy%20they%20contain)) – every time entropy rises, available (Gibbs) energy drops, limiting what the next cycle can do unless more energy is added.  

**In summary:** Gibbs‐energy minimization (or equivalently the Second Law) does impose a bound on circular economy efficiency.  A perfectly efficient, closed-loop economy without entropy production is thermodynamically impossible.  Real recycling and reuse loops always cost energy and produce entropy (heat, dispersed matter, lower concentrations).  The more thoroughly one tries to close the loop, the more energy per unit throughput is needed ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=Therefore%2C%20I%20doubt%20very%20much,practically%29%20inexhaustible%20energy%20source)) ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=the%20raw%20materials%2C%20such%20as,a%20circular%20economy%20in%20practice)).  Thus, while circularity can **greatly reduce** waste and resource consumption, it cannot transcend fundamental thermodynamic limits. Each cycle will be less than 100% efficient, and scalability is constrained by increasing irreversibility.  

**Sources:**  Thermodynamic analyses of circular systems show that any practical cycle requires extra energy to offset entropy gains ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=the%20raw%20materials%2C%20such%20as,a%20circular%20economy%20in%20practice)) ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=Therefore%2C%20I%20doubt%20very%20much,practically%29%20inexhaustible%20energy%20source)).  For example, Christof Walter explains that even an ideal reversible cycle (Carnot) is only a limit case, whereas actual processes “always [dissipate] energy” and need constant input ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=the%20raw%20materials%2C%20such%20as,a%20circular%20economy%20in%20practice)).  Likewise, Korhonen et al. stress that while circular flows offer benefits, **“the second law of thermodynamics means that every circular economy-type process…should be carefully analyzed”** – no cycle erases the need to pay entropy costs ([www.linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/circular-economy-boundaries-second-law-thermodynamics-christof-walter#:~:text=The%20publication%20of%20Korhonen%20et,this%20is%20so%20even%20in)).  These analyses align with the Gibbs‐energy principle: if a process is to occur, ΔG must be driven negative (by adding energy), and any finite loop will tend toward ΔG=0 (max entropy) if left unpowered.
