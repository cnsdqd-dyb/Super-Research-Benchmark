High‐penetration PV is technically **feasible**, but requires planning for its variability rather than being “forbidden” by it.  By definition, solar PV output fluctuates chaotically with clouds and day/night, so power‐system models often include **stochastic** PV profiles (random or weather‐driven variations) when testing reliability.  Those models indeed show that as PV share rises, the grid must cope with large swings (for example, sudden drops in sunshine) that stress frequency, voltage and reserve margins ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/12/10/1996/htm#:~:text=Due%20to%20the%20irregularities%20and,comparison%20of%20RES%20with%20a)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/12/8/1445#:~:text=to%20frequency%20variations,In%20addition%2C%20the)).  In an isolated microgrid, for instance, analysts actually compute a **maximum PV penetration** so that frequency and inertia remain within limits ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/12/8/1445#:~:text=to%20frequency%20variations,In%20addition%2C%20the)).  In large grids the same issues arise: conventional “inertia” (from spinning machines) falls as PV replaces synchronous generators, slowing frequency response; and the effective **capacity credit** of solar (its reliable fraction during peak load) shrinks dramatically at high share ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/18/21/5636#:~:text=match%20at%20L80%20the%20relatively,secure%20and%20reliable%20energy%20transition)).  In plain terms, stochastic PV models reveal a need for extra reserves, storage or backup generation as PV penetration grows ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/18/21/5636#:~:text=match%20at%20L80%20the%20relatively,secure%20and%20reliable%20energy%20transition)). 

However, these modeling results **do not impose a hard ceiling** on renewable penetration – they just quantify how much flexibility is needed.  In practice, grids can be redesigned and new technologies deployed to accommodate the variability.  For example, numerous studies show that spreading PV over a wide area dramatically **smooths output**: clouds rarely cover an entire region at once.  An Ontario study found that dispersing identical PV farms across the province produced energy profiles with “lower variability” and higher peak‐time output than clumping them at one site ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148114000639#:~:text=and%202005,use%20implications)).  Likewise, smart forecasting, demand response and geographic interconnections can mitigate the apparent “volatility”.  Likewise, modern PV inverters can be configured to provide synthetic inertia or rapid voltage/frequency control, so that a high–PV grid behaves more like one with conventional generators.  

In short, the **engineering “limit”** is really determined not by PV’s physics but by how much storage and flexibility one is willing to build.  Stochastic models simply tell us how much backup power or storage hours are needed to meet a given reliability target.  For example, one planning study emphasizes that *if* system flexibility (from batteries, fast turbines, demand management, etc.) is increased, the required backup capacity to cover PV variability falls accordingly ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/10/6/709#:~:text=Korea%20over%20the%20past%20five,and%20increases%20the%20total%20electricity)).  Conversely, if no new flexibility is added, models will suggest a practical cap because reliability would suffer.  This is a planning constraint – not a law of nature.  In fact, many grid‐integration studies (and real systems) show very high renewables fractions are achievable with adequate supports.  (For instance, studies of California and Europe project 80–100% clean electricity by mid-century, provided enough storage, long‐distance transmission and demand flexibility are added.) 

**Bottom line:** stochastic PV models are a valuable tool to **quantify** the reliability impact of solar variability, but they do *not* inherently “forbid” high PV penetration.  Rather, they highlight the need for complementary resources (storage, dispatchable backup, smart controls, transmission, etc.) as renewables grow ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/18/21/5636#:~:text=match%20at%20L80%20the%20relatively,secure%20and%20reliable%20energy%20transition)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/10/6/709#:~:text=Korea%20over%20the%20past%20five,and%20increases%20the%20total%20electricity)).  With those engineering solutions, there is no fundamental fixed upper bound imposed by PV volatility itself – only practical/economic limits.  In other words, variability modeling informs how to safely push PV and wind to high levels; it doesn’t by itself set an immutable penetration ceiling. 

**Sources:** Integration studies and reviews note that high-VRE penetrations raise stability challenges (voltage/frequency swings, low inertia, low capacity credit) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/12/10/1996/htm#:~:text=Due%20to%20the%20irregularities%20and,comparison%20of%20RES%20with%20a)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/12/8/1445#:~:text=to%20frequency%20variations,In%20addition%2C%20the)).  But they also show how flexibility measures (aggregation, storage, fast controls) can overcome these issues.  For example, geographical dispersion “reduces variability” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960148114000639#:~:text=and%202005,use%20implications)), and adding storage or fast-ramping plants cuts required reserves ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/10/6/709#:~:text=Korea%20over%20the%20past%20five,and%20increases%20the%20total%20electricity)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/18/21/5636#:~:text=match%20at%20L80%20the%20relatively,secure%20and%20reliable%20energy%20transition)).  Thus the stochastic variability only **constrains** penetration in the sense that one must design adequate backup – it does not impose a fundamental limit on renewables’ share.
