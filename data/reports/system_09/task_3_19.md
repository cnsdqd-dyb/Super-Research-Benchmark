# Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) and diversity 

The **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)** is a classical concentration measure – essentially the sum of squared shares of categories (e.g. topics or providers).  A high HHI means a few categories dominate (low diversity), while a low HHI means the distribution is spread out (high diversity).  In fact, HHI and diversity are inversely related: “concentration and diversity are opposite and complementary concepts. Higher diversity means lower concentration and vice versa” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0304397521000256#:~:text=match%20at%20L301%20Hirschman%20,means%20lower%20concentration%20and%20vice)).  Equivalently, HHI can be seen as the probability that two randomly chosen recommendations share the same type ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0304397521000256#:~:text=The%20Herfindahl,is%20the%20probability%20of%20them)).  Thus, minimizing HHI is effectively maximizing (2nd-order) diversity.  In a knowledge-graph recommendation context, a low-HHI solution corresponds to a “decentralized” subgraph with many topics appearing, whereas a high-HHI solution focuses on a few dominant nodes ([www.scitepress.org](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2024/129361/pdf/index.html#:~:text=ness%20in%20ascending%20order%20of,the%20associated%20HHI%20exhibit)).  Studies on knowledge-graph recommenders have shown that re-ranking by ascending HHI yields recommendations with *high diversity* (if HHI is low, there is less monopolization of concepts) ([www.scitepress.org](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2024/129361/pdf/index.html#:~:text=ness%20in%20ascending%20order%20of,the%20associated%20HHI%20exhibit)).  

# Fairness constraints in recommendation 

In educational recommendation (or any recommender) **fairness constraints** typically mandate that different groups or providers receive similar treatment.  For example, one may require that each course provider or topic category gets roughly equal exposure to users.  In multi-stakeholder recommender systems this is often phrased as: *“the items of each provider are recommended to a similar number of users as items of other providers,”* and likewise that the users covered by each provider’s items are comparably diverse ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/10/4984#:~:text=provider%E2%80%99s%20items%20are%20recommended,items%20of%20other%20providers%20are)).  In other words, fairness constraints force the distribution of recommendations to be more uniform across the protected groups (e.g. subjects, departments, demographic groups).  Such constraints explicitly balance coverage or exposure, and implicitly limit how concentrated recommendations can be.  

# Effect of HHI under diversity optimization with fairness 

Because fairness constraints tend to equalize the recommendation distribution, they *indirectly* drive HHI down (increasing diversity).  A perfectly fair solution (each category equally represented) yields the minimum possible HHI (the reciprocal of the number of categories).  In practice, when an optimizer maximizes diversity subject to fairness constraints, it is already pushing toward a uniform split of items.  This means the resulting recommendation lists have low HHI (high variety of topics) by construction.  In fact, experimental work on KG-based recommender re-ranking shows that favoring low-HHI nodes leads to more “surprising” and diverse recommendations ([www.scitepress.org](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2024/129361/pdf/index.html#:~:text=ness%20in%20ascending%20order%20of,the%20associated%20HHI%20exhibit)).  

Importantly, **HHI itself is not an independent constraint** in such optimizations – it is simply a way to quantify the outcome.  Under fairness constraints, the system will balance group representation (e.g. equal coverage per subject or provider) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/10/4984#:~:text=provider%E2%80%99s%20items%20are%20recommended,items%20of%20other%20providers%20are)).  Any such balanced solution automatically has lower HHI (because the square-sum of equal shares is minimal).  Thus, HHI does not *drive* the optimization when fairness constraints are in place; rather, fairness and diversity objectives ensure a low-HHI outcome.  In summary, imposing fairness leads to a more uniform (and thus lower-HHI) distribution of educational content ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0304397521000256#:~:text=match%20at%20L301%20Hirschman%20,means%20lower%20concentration%20and%20vice)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/10/4984#:~:text=provider%E2%80%99s%20items%20are%20recommended,items%20of%20other%20providers%20are)). HHI simply reflects the degree of diversity achieved – it drops as diversity rises under fairness – but it is not itself a separate factor beyond measuring the result.

However, the optimization itself uses explicit fairness and diversity objectives, not HHI directly.  In other words, HHI is an outcome measure: fairness tends to push HHI low (higher diversity), but HHI does not independently alter the constrained optimization beyond signaling the concentration of the result.
