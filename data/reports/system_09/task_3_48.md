# Inventory Synchronization and Correlated Demand

Synchronizing inventory across products can indeed boost turnover (sales relative to stock) by exploiting demand correlations.  The key idea is that if multiple products have related demand patterns, coordinated policies (pooled stocking, aligned replenishments, etc.) can reduce total safety stock and increase sales velocity. For example, inventory pooling across SKUs (treating them in a central “bundle” rather than independently) leverages the risk‐pooling effect: independent demands see their combined safety stock shrink by the √n rule ([pressbooks.pub](https://pressbooks.pub/supplychainmanagement3005/chapter/8-5-special-topics-in-inventory-management/#:~:text=Suppose%20a%20single%20store%20requires,x%20100%20%3D%20400%20units)), so average inventory falls and turnover rises.  In practice, “lean” and Just-In-Time synchronization practices embody this: firms widely applying lean/synchronized production have been shown to achieve higher inventory turnover than those that don’t ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925527310000393#:~:text=Lean%20manufacturing%20,do%20not%20rely%20on%20LM)).  

Likewise, modern demand-forecasting that **decomposes inter-product correlations** can improve inventory alignment.  For instance, Amazon researchers build a graph of products and sellers (with “demand” edges linking sellers→products and “substitute” edges linking related products) and train graph-neural-network forecasts.  Incorporating cross-product demand correlations boosted forecast accuracy by ∼16% over independent forecasts ([www.amazon.science](https://www.amazon.science/blog/data-on-correlated-products-and-sellers-helps-improve-demand-forecasting#:~:text=In%20experiments%2C%20our%20approach%20increased,relative%20improvement%20of%20almost%2025))—leading to tighter inventory and higher turnover.  In multi-item inventory models, analysts have shown that a bivariate Gaussian demand can be *normalized* to remove correlation, decoupling each item’s order policy ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716017300532#:~:text=continuous%20review%20inventory%20model%20in,inventory%20model%20in%20which%20the)).  Such “correlation decomposition” lets each SKU’s reorder point be computed independently, reducing high aggregate inventory.  

**Key strategies and evidence:** Synchronize replenishment and pool stock among related SKUs whenever possible.  Centralizing inventory (or aligning order cycles) exploits the *law of large numbers* – if demands are nearly independent, safety stock drops by the square-root of the number of items ([pressbooks.pub](https://pressbooks.pub/supplychainmanagement3005/chapter/8-5-special-topics-in-inventory-management/#:~:text=Suppose%20a%20single%20store%20requires,x%20100%20%3D%20400%20units)). Using **data-driven correlation decomposition** (e.g. principal components or graph models) identifies these nearly independent factors so inventory can be managed jointly.  For example, Yang et al. decoupled a correlated two-product (vanilla box + options) model into independent components via normalization ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716017300532#:~:text=continuous%20review%20inventory%20model%20in,inventory%20model%20in%20which%20the)).  In practice, lean “pull” systems and postponement further synchronize production to demand, empirically improving turnover: firms with broader lean adoption show significantly higher inventory turnover ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925527310000393#:~:text=Lean%20manufacturing%20,do%20not%20rely%20on%20LM)).  

- **Risk-pooling/centralization:** Pooling inventory across products or locations cuts overall variability.  If N identical items each need safety stock S, combining them yields roughly S·√N instead of N·S ([pressbooks.pub](https://pressbooks.pub/supplychainmanagement3005/chapter/8-5-special-topics-in-inventory-management/#:~:text=Suppose%20a%20single%20store%20requires,x%20100%20%3D%20400%20units)).  Thus synchronizing (e.g. pooling SKUs or using a common reorder) lowers average inventory and raises turnover.  
- **Correlation-aware forecasting:** Models that incorporate product-to-product (and seller) correlations can align replenishments more tightly.  Graph-based methods, for instance, explicitly model substitutes and complement relationships ([www.amazon.science](https://www.amazon.science/blog/data-on-correlated-products-and-sellers-helps-improve-demand-forecasting#:~:text=We%20represent%20correlations%20between%20product,link%20products%20to%20each%20other)); they achieved ~16% better demand forecasts than naïve models ([www.amazon.science](https://www.amazon.science/blog/data-on-correlated-products-and-sellers-helps-improve-demand-forecasting#:~:text=In%20experiments%2C%20our%20approach%20increased,relative%20improvement%20of%20almost%2025)).  Better forecasts let you order closer to true demand, reducing excess inventory.  
- **Decoupled inventory calculations:** Mathematically “decomposing” correlated demands (via normalization or PCA) can split a multi-item problem into independent ones ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214716017300532#:~:text=continuous%20review%20inventory%20model%20in,inventory%20model%20in%20which%20the)).  This lets each SKU’s safety stock be set based on its own (reduced) variance rather than the sum of variances, effectively exploiting correlation to lower stock.  
- **Lean/synchronization practices:** Coordinating production and inventory (e.g. Just-In-Time, shared kanbans, vendor-managed inventory) aligns supply to fluctuating demand.  Empirical studies find lean-synchronized firms hold less inventory per unit of sales – i.e. higher turnover ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925527310000393#:~:text=Lean%20manufacturing%20,do%20not%20rely%20on%20LM)).  In short, **synchronized ordering** across products avoids redundant stock.

In summary, **yes** – by using synchronization strategies that explicitly account for inter-product demand correlations, companies can minimize redundant safety stock and boost inventory turnover.  Correlation decomposition techniques (from statistical factorization to graph models) help planners see which products move together, so replenishments can be timed or pooled accordingly.  Combined with lean/pooled inventory practices, these methods cut average inventory while keeping service levels, thereby raising the inventory turnover ratio ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925527310000393#:~:text=Lean%20manufacturing%20,do%20not%20rely%20on%20LM)) ([pressbooks.pub](https://pressbooks.pub/supplychainmanagement3005/chapter/8-5-special-topics-in-inventory-management/#:~:text=Suppose%20a%20single%20store%20requires,x%20100%20%3D%20400%20units)) ([www.amazon.science](https://www.amazon.science/blog/data-on-correlated-products-and-sellers-helps-improve-demand-forecasting#:~:text=In%20experiments%2C%20our%20approach%20increased,relative%20improvement%20of%20almost%2025)).
