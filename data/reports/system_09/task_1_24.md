**VaR is just a quantile, not a built‐in optimizer.** In practice, a VaR model doesn’t *choose* a balance between “efficiency” and tail sensitivity – rather, that balance is set by your distributional assumptions and data‐weighting scheme.  For example, a simple parametric VaR (e.g. Gaussian or Student‐t) is statistically efficient (low estimation variance) under its assumed distribution, but will understate extreme losses if the real data have fatter tails.  Conversely, pure historical simulation captures realized tails by construction, but it relies entirely on the chosen sample window or weights.  No VaR algorithm magically “optimizes” this trade‐off – you simply get whatever tail capture or variance your method and parameters give you.

**Historical windows can bias VaR under changing markets.** A major practical issue is that financial returns aren’t usually IID or stationary.  Naïve historical‐simulation VaR typically uses a fixed rolling window with equal weights, implicitly treating all past returns as equally relevant.  This is problematic if the distribution shifts.  For example, as Shimko et al. (1998) note, seasonal or regime shifts in volatility mean equal-weight HS will *underestimate* risk during high-volatility periods and *overestimate* it during quiet periods ([www.frm.midhafin.com](https://www.frm.midhafin.com/part-2/non-parametric-approaches#:~:text=sample%20period%20the%20same%20weight%2C,overestimate%20them%20in%20the%20summer)).  In general, HS assumes IID returns – ignoring volatility clustering and regime breaks – so a sudden jump in volatility will only show up in the VaR gradually as the window moves ([www.frm.midhafin.com](https://www.frm.midhafin.com/part-2/non-parametric-approaches#:~:text=sample%20period%20is%20equally%20likely,should%20have%20a%20weight%20that)).  In other words, if markets change, a fixed historical window yields a biased VaR: calm old data can dilute current risk (understating tail risk), or past crises can linger in the window (overstating it).  

**Specialized methods illustrate the trade-offs.**  Consider extreme-value–theory (EVT) VaR: it fits a parametric tail distribution.  This *greatly increases statistical efficiency* at very high quantiles (capturing the far tail) but it only “works” at the most extreme levels – it performs poorly for more moderate VaR levels – and it still assumes IID, which often fails in real markets ([file.scirp.org](https://file.scirp.org/Html/5-2410140_60075.htm#:~:text=of%20this%20approach%20is%20that,is%20also%20based%20on%20the)).  Filtered–HS or EWMA schemes try to adapt to non‐stationarity (by weighting recent data more or filtering out volatility swings), but *those* choices must be made by the modeler.  In short: there’s no magic VaR formula that self-balances tail vs. efficiency.  In real-world VaR systems, *window length, weighting and distributional assumptions* determine how much tail risk is captured, and a mismatched window under a non-stationary regime will bias the VaR ([www.frm.midhafin.com](https://www.frm.midhafin.com/part-2/non-parametric-approaches#:~:text=sample%20period%20the%20same%20weight%2C,overestimate%20them%20in%20the%20summer)) ([file.scirp.org](https://file.scirp.org/Html/5-2410140_60075.htm#:~:text=of%20this%20approach%20is%20that,is%20also%20based%20on%20the)). 

**Bottom line:** VaR itself is not a trade-off optimizer – it simply returns the chosen quantile given your inputs.  If returns are non-stationary, a fixed historical window can *bias* that estimate.  Practitioners mitigate this via rolling windows, decay weights, volatility filters or multivariate models, but any static window under nonstationarity will either miss new tail risk or overhang old events ([www.frm.midhafin.com](https://www.frm.midhafin.com/part-2/non-parametric-approaches#:~:text=sample%20period%20the%20same%20weight%2C,overestimate%20them%20in%20the%20summer)) ([file.scirp.org](https://file.scirp.org/Html/5-2410140_60075.htm#:~:text=of%20this%20approach%20is%20that,is%20also%20based%20on%20the)).
