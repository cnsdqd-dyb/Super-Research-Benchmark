# Privacy vs Traceability in CBDCs  
CBDC designs must thread the needle between **cash-like privacy** and regulatory auditability.  In practice, distributed-ledger CBDCs can incorporate privacy layers (e.g. encrypted transactions, selective disclosure) so that ordinary users enjoy anonymity similar to cash, while still allowing authorities to trace funds if needed.  For example, the BIS’s Project *Tourbillon* built a two-tier “e-cash” prototype in which **payers remain anonymous** even to merchants, banks and the central bank – only the transaction amount is visible centrally ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/cbdc/tourbillon.htm#:~:text=Project%20Tourbillon%20tested%20the%20concept,the%20consumer%20or%20the%20merchant)).  In fact, academic analyses note that *only* token-based (cash-like) designs can truly grant anonymity; account-based ledgers inherently reveal identity ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S027553192500025X#:~:text=i,in%20the%20digital%20medium%20of)).  (That said, most proposals do enforce some traceability: e.g. requiring large transactions to collect identity or enabling revocable anonymity via blind signatures or KYC modules.)  Crucially, digital currency schemes cannot simply adopt a static “privacy threshold” like cash: experts warn that tying privacy to small-amount limits is hard to enforce in a purely digital CBDC ([blogs.oracle.com](https://blogs.oracle.com/blockchain/privacy-ledger-architecture-and-crossborder-integrations-in-central-bank-digital-currency#:~:text=requests%2C%20etc,not%20viable%20in%20digital%20currency)).  

# Distributed Ledger Architectures  
A CBDC ledger can be **permissioned or hybrid**: many central banks study a two-tier model where a distributed ledger is shared among banks/PSPs and the core system handles settlement ([blogs.oracle.com](https://blogs.oracle.com/blockchain/privacy-ledger-architecture-and-crossborder-integrations-in-central-bank-digital-currency#:~:text=Ledger%20Architecture%3A%20Centralized%20or%20Distributed)) ([blogs.oracle.com](https://blogs.oracle.com/blockchain/privacy-ledger-architecture-and-crossborder-integrations-in-central-bank-digital-currency#:~:text=Table,s)). In such architectures, privacy engines can be inserted between users and the core.  For instance, a “user proxy” or wallet might generate zero-knowledge proofs (zk-proofs) about balances or KYC without revealing details, then send only the proof plus minimal metadata to the network ([johal.in](https://johal.in/data-minimization-for-cbdc-privacy-by-design-principles/#:~:text=Building%20a%20data,1s%20to%20190ms)).  One can also use **privacy proxies or enclaves** (e.g. Intel SGX) to strip identifying info before ledger entry.  In effect, these designs create “one-way mirrors”: users prove compliance to the system while concrete identities stay hidden from ordinary observers ([johal.in](https://johal.in/data-minimization-for-cbdc-privacy-by-design-principles/#:~:text=Building%20a%20data,1s%20to%20190ms)). Regulatory traceability is preserved by keeping an audit trail of the cryptographic proofs themselves or by granting authorities a selective “view key” or backdoor.  

For example, one prototype architecture uses three layers: light clients (user wallets doing local ZKPs), a validation layer that checks proofs and strips metadata, and a core settlement layer holding only encrypted or committed transaction data ([johal.in](https://johal.in/data-minimization-for-cbdc-privacy-by-design-principles/#:~:text=Building%20a%20data,1s%20to%20190ms)).  This lets the system verify transactions without ever revealing a user’s identity or exact balance.  The identity module can be offline/stored by intermediaries, so only proofs and commitments hit the public ledger. In short, *designs exist to balance privacy and traceability* – using blind signatures, strong encryption, or ZK-proofs to hide details, while still logging proof-of-compliance for audits ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/cbdc/tourbillon.htm#:~:text=Project%20Tourbillon%20tested%20the%20concept,the%20consumer%20or%20the%20merchant)) ([johal.in](https://johal.in/data-minimization-for-cbdc-privacy-by-design-principles/#:~:text=Building%20a%20data,1s%20to%20190ms)).  

# Zero-Knowledge Proofs: Promise and Limits  
Zero-knowledge proofs (zk-SNARKs, zk-STARKs, Bulletproofs, etc.) offer powerful privacy: they let a user **prove statements** (e.g. “I have sufficient balance” or “I passed KYC”) without revealing underlying data.  In a CBDC, ZKPs can validate every transaction’s integrity and AML filters off-chain.  However, this power comes at a cost.  Generating a non-interactive proof currently takes **orders of magnitude more time** than a simple digital signature.  In benchmarks, even an optimized zk-SNARK proof needed on the order of **3–5 seconds** of CPU time for a single payment check ([johal.in](https://johal.in/zero-knowledge-proofs-for-cbdc-privacy-and-verification/#:~:text=When%20testing%20ZKP%20implementations%20for,composition%2C%20where%20multiple%20proofs%20are)), whereas everyday POS systems require sub-second latency.  One BIS report found that switching to advanced *quantum-safe* blind signatures (a similar privacy enhancement) made payments ~5× slower and cut throughput by a factor of ~200 ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/cbdc/tourbillon.htm#:~:text=it%20requires%20specialised%20expertise%2C%20and,safe%20cryptography)).  In summary, advanced proofs dramatically shrink throughput unless special measures are taken.  

Experts emphasize this bottleneck.  For example, analysts at Irreducible note that today’s ZK proof generation is “still too slow” for *real-time* transaction validation; protocols like zkSync must decouple proof from block time to compensate ([www.irreducible.com](https://www.irreducible.com/posts/the-need-for-high-performance-zero-knowledge-proving#:~:text=thereby%20scaling%20L1s%20without%20sacrificing,and%20surpassing%29%20the%20Internet%E2%80%99s)).  In other words, without massive acceleration, ZKPs alone cannot sustain high-frequency payments.  The only way to scale is through **hardware acceleration and batching**.  Techniques like proof aggregation/recursion (verifying many transactions inside one “meta-proof”), GPU/ASIC offloading, and cached proof templates can cut amortized cost.  One implementation report showed that moving proving onto NVIDIA A100 GPUs slashed per-transaction zk-SNARK time from ~2.1 s to ~0.19 s ([johal.in](https://johal.in/data-minimization-for-cbdc-privacy-by-design-principles/#:~:text=the%20core%20settlement%20layer,1s%20to%20190ms)).  Even so, this is still tens of milliseconds versus microsecond-scale for plain signatures.  

# Throughput Trade-offs  
By contrast, **non-privacy-augmented designs** can achieve blockbuster throughput.  For instance, MIT’s Project Hamilton (a centralized ledger experiment) achieved ~1.7 million transactions/sec (99% settled within <1 second) simply by optimizing a conventional database-backed architecture ([news.mit.edu](https://news.mit.edu/2022/digital-currency-fed-boston-0203#:~:text=The%20researchers%20developed%20two%20complete,vital%20to%20a%20potential%20CBDC)).  That shows what is possible if privacy layers are stripped away – essentially treating CBDC like a super-fast payment rail.  Attempting that same speed with per-transaction zk-proofs is currently unrealistic.  In a distributed ledger model, every node or validator would have to verify these heavy proofs, drastically limiting TPS.  Even permissioned blockchains (Hyperledger, Corda, etc.) would see major slowdowns if every transaction required on-chain ZK verification.  

In practice, a balance is struck: Many CBDC pilots use performance-friendly designs, adding privacy only where feasible.  For example, they might allow **limited anonymity** by default (small-value payments are pseudonymous), but require identity for high-value transfers or use off-chain analytics for AML.  Others invest in hybrid architectures: retail banks handle user onboarding/KYC off-chain and only push anonymized tokens onto the core ledger.  In all cases, the more cryptographic privacy is demanded (especially non-interactive ZK cryptography), the more it constrains throughput. As one industry summary puts it, enabling “real-time transaction validation” with ZK tech requires **HPC-grade hardware**; otherwise the public demand for cheap, fast payments will quickly outgrow proof generation speeds ([www.irreducible.com](https://www.irreducible.com/posts/the-need-for-high-performance-zero-knowledge-proving#:~:text=thereby%20scaling%20L1s%20without%20sacrificing,and%20surpassing%29%20the%20Internet%E2%80%99s)).  

# Conclusion: Trade-offs Remain  
In summary, **distributed-ledger CBDC systems *can* be architected to balance privacy and traceability** – using tokenization, homomorphic commitments and selective ZK-proofs to hide personal data, yet still provide audit trails for regulators ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/cbdc/tourbillon.htm#:~:text=Project%20Tourbillon%20tested%20the%20concept,the%20consumer%20or%20the%20merchant)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S027553192500025X#:~:text=i,in%20the%20digital%20medium%20of)).  However, there is an inherent performance trade-off.  Every ounce of extra privacy (be it quantum-safe crypto or complex ZK proofs) adds computational weight.  Today’s zero-knowledge protocols are impressive, but their proof-generation complexity **does** introduce throughput limits.  Unless new breakthroughs (faster ZK schemes, widespread specialized hardware, recursive batching, etc.) arrive, CBDC architects must choose a compromise: either accept lower TPS for stronger privacy, or relax privacy (e.g. use pseudonyms or trust boundaries) to keep the system fast.  

**Key takeaway:** Modern CBDC designs do leverage advanced cryptography to protect users, but at present those gadgets slow things down.  As BIS and industry pilots show, *you can optimize privacy*, but heavy ZKP-like techniques will generally cap transaction rates unless mitigated by extra engineering or tiered system design ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/cbdc/tourbillon.htm#:~:text=it%20requires%20specialised%20expertise%2C%20and,safe%20cryptography)) ([www.irreducible.com](https://www.irreducible.com/posts/the-need-for-high-performance-zero-knowledge-proving#:~:text=thereby%20scaling%20L1s%20without%20sacrificing,and%20surpassing%29%20the%20Internet%E2%80%99s)).
