The key fact is that different modes have wildly different carbon‐intensities, so a carbon‐aware capacity plan will **bias capacity toward the lowest‐carbon modes**.  For example, road vehicles (especially private autos) currently dominate transport CO₂: roughly 70% of global transport energy CO₂ comes from road vehicles, whereas rail accounts for only about 1% ([www.ipcc.ch](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-10/?trk=public_post_comment-text#:~:text=emissions%20from%20the%20transport%20sector,Emissions%20from%20shipping%20and)).  In passenger travel specifically, cars, two‐wheelers and vans produce about 75% of passenger CO₂, while buses and trains together emit only ≈7% even though they carry roughly 20% of all passenger trips ([www.ipcc.ch](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-10/?trk=public_post_comment-text#:~:text=Passenger%20cars%2C%20two,30%3B%20SLoCaT%202018a)).  In short, the majority of transport emissions today come from high‐carbon modes (cars, trucks, airplanes), and collective (bus/rail) modes contribute only a small share despite moving many people.

When we “optimize” capacity (e.g. network, fleet size, infrastructure) under a carbon criterion, this emission disparity pushes the optimal solution to **favor low‐carbon modes**.  In practice, an optimization that includes a carbon cost or constraint effectively penalizes capacity expansions on high‐emission modes.  Adding roadway or flight capacity may reduce travel time, but it also induces many tons of extra CO₂.  By contrast, adding a lane of metro or more buses serves passengers at far lower emissions per passenger‐kilometer.  Thus the model will allocate relatively more capacity (vehicles, frequency, track‐miles, etc.) to modes like rail, electric buses or active transport, and limit capacity on cars or planes.  Mathematically, if each mode \(i\) has a carbon intensity \(e_i\) (e.g. grams CO₂/pax‐km), the optimal allocation equalizes the marginal benefit per unit of \(e_i\) – so modes with large \(e_i\) (dirty modes) get less marginal capacity than modes with small \(e_i\). 

Indeed, empirical studies confirm that substantial emission reduction only comes from shifting large travel shares into the clean modes.  For instance, one data‐driven study of urban trips found that meaningful CO₂ cuts arise only when the shares of bus, metro and cycling rise sharply ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/17/15/6983#:~:text=account%20for%2075.1,of%20bus%2C%20metro%2C%20and%20cycling)).  In other words, an optimal, low‐carbon network **raises capacity and share of mass transit and non‐motorized modes**, while de‐prioritizing high‐carbon modes.  The result is a more balanced network: for a given total travel demand, the “emissions‐aware” optimum will look quite different from a business‐as‐usual plan.  High-carbon modes (like extra highway lanes or new short‐haul flights) are curtailed, whereas investment in low-carbon options (trains, buses, bike lanes) is increased. 

In summary, knowing the emission distribution by mode fundamentally shifts the capacity mix.  If cars/planes currently emit most CO₂, the optimal configuration under an emissions objective will **allocate much more capacity to greener modes**.  By contrast, if a mode already has very low emissions per passenger‐km, the planner will expand it first.  Thus carbon emissions act like a “cost” multiplier: modes with high carbon intensities become effectively more expensive to scale up, so the peak of capacity in the optimal solution lies with the modes that emit least per passenger ([www.ipcc.ch](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-10/?trk=public_post_comment-text#:~:text=emissions%20from%20the%20transport%20sector,Emissions%20from%20shipping%20and)) ([www.ipcc.ch](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-10/?trk=public_post_comment-text#:~:text=Passenger%20cars%2C%20two,30%3B%20SLoCaT%202018a)).
