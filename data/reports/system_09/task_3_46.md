**Definition (Response Resilience):**  In supply chains, *resilience* of an inventory system is typically understood as its ability to maintain supply (meet demand) despite disruptions.  In practical terms it is often measured as the time or effort needed to recover after a disruption and restore service levels ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12063-024-00487-z#:~:text=resilience%20can%20be%20operationalized%20quantitatively,2023)).  

**Asymmetric Delays Increase Uncertainty:**  When transport delays have an asymmetric (e.g. skewed or heavy‐tailed) distribution, shipments are unpredictably late much more often than they are early.  In effect, this creates a few extremely long delays.  Such uneven delay patterns inject extra lead-time variability into the system.  Supply‐chain studies show that high lead‐time variability forces more conservative (risk-averse) inventory policies.  For example, one study finds that only *high* levels of lead‐time variability (akin to skewed delays) “justify more the need for a coordinated supply chain,” requiring additional hedging or buffer strategies ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925527316301608#:~:text=risk%20management%20approach%20using%20operational,of%20lead%20time%20variability%20are)).  In general, increased uncertainty (especially heavy right‐tail delays) means planners must hold larger safety stocks and allocate inventory more redundantly to guard against the rare extreme delays.  

**Slower Response Time:**  The need for larger buffers and slower replenishment under asymmetric delays inevitably *slows* the system’s response.  Unpredictable long delays exacerbates the “bullwhip” effect: order variability amplifies upstream as firms chase erratic deliveries ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305048317301111#:~:text=Bullwhip%20increases%20significantly%20when%20stochastic,lead%20times%20must%20be%20forecasted)).  This makes the supply chain less agile.  Practically, asymmetric delays lengthen recovery times from a disruption, since inventory cannot be reallocated or replenished as quickly as planned.  In other words, resilience (the ability to bounce back) is reduced.  As one review notes, resilience can be quantified as the time needed to recover from disruptions ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12063-024-00487-z#:~:text=resilience%20can%20be%20operationalized%20quantitatively,2023)); heavy-tailed delays stretch that time.  

**Strategic Shift (Visibility over Agility):**  Simulation studies show that when lead times grow longer (as with skewed delays), the best resilience tactics shift.  In fast (short‐lead‐time) chains, building capacity and flexibility is key.  But in slow chains (long or highly variable lead times), *visibility and coordination* become critical ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12063-024-00487-z#:~:text=operational%20dynamics.%20For%20fast,responsiveness%20is%20important%20moderator%20of)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12063-024-00487-z#:~:text=consequence%2C%20as%20they%20shift%20the,information%20sharing%20as%20a%20relatively)).  In practice, this means that with asymmetric delays, firms must rely more on improved information sharing, demand sensing and careful inventory positioning rather than just capacity expansions.  For example, recent research finds that “with the increase in lead times, the flexibility to increase capacity is replaced by increased information sharing as a relatively stronger strategy” ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12063-024-00487-z#:~:text=consequence%2C%20as%20they%20shift%20the,information%20sharing%20as%20a%20relatively)).  In short, skewed delays effectively slow the chain, so resilience hinges on coordination (visibility) and safety‐stock buffers, rather than on rapid shipment.  

**Net Effect on Inventory Resilience:**  Overall, an asymmetric (skewed) delay distribution *degrades* the response resilience of inventory allocation.  It forces higher safety thresholds and slower reaction, making it harder to quickly reallocate stock once a disruption hits.  Unless compensated by extra stock or alternate routing, skewed delays increase stock-out risk and slow recovery.  In fairness, some research notes that *mild* variability alone doesn’t always harm performance ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925527316301608#:~:text=risk%20management%20approach%20using%20operational,of%20lead%20time%20variability%20are)), but as asymmetry (and tail risk) grows the need for conservative buffering becomes clear.  In sum, more asymmetric transport delays make an inventory system less nimble – requiring larger strategic buffers and shifting strategy toward visibility – which ultimately reduces its quick-response resilience.
