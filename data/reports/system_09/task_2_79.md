# Tension in National Identity: Balancing the Particular and the Universal 

National identity is often constructed around **particular cultural elements** – language, history, religion or folk traditions that give a people a unique “flavour” – while simultaneously claiming adherence to **universal values** such as human rights, democracy or freedom.  This creates an inherent tension: uniqueness requires distinguishing one’s nation from others, whereas universal values stress commonalities across humanity.  In practice, countries manage this tension by emphasizing that these universal ideals are part of the nation’s own story, and by portraying their distinctive culture as the nation’s contribution to human civilization. For example, UNESCO has championed a *“two-level loyalty”* model where citizens remain patriotic to their country **and** committed to global humanity ([www.inst.at](https://www.inst.at/kulturen/unesco/strategy_english.html#:~:text=confiance%20dans%20les%20institutions%20internationales,conservative%2C%20this%20conception%20was%20still)). Likewise, international conventions often reconcile local and global interests: a World Heritage site is officially described as symbolizing **both** its nation *and* all humankind ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/icon/article-abstract/19/5/1756/6515705#:~:text=one%20starting%20point%20for%20further,national%20and%20a%20global%20one)). In short, identity narratives are crafted to hold particularist pride and universalist principles together, rather than resolving the tension. 

- **Dual-cultural loyalty:**  One way the tension is managed is by teaching a *two-tiered identity*.  UNESCO’s early education reforms, for example, insisted that national loyalty should **not** be replaced by internationalism, but that citizens can fulfill international obligations through patriotic conduct ([www.inst.at](https://www.inst.at/kulturen/unesco/strategy_english.html#:~:text=confiance%20dans%20les%20institutions%20internationales,conservative%2C%20this%20conception%20was%20still)).  In other words, people learn to see themselves as both nationals and as members of the global community, so neither level of loyalty is abandoned. This “civisme international” model explicitly accepts that national identity remains primary while also granting a place to universal commitments ([www.inst.at](https://www.inst.at/kulturen/unesco/strategy_english.html#:~:text=confiance%20dans%20les%20institutions%20internationales,conservative%2C%20this%20conception%20was%20still)). 

- **World Heritage as shared culture:**  Powerful symbols often carry dual meanings.  UNESCO World Heritage sites must meet “outstanding universal value” criteria, and their descriptions routinely highlight a site’s significance for humanity in general *and* for the local nation ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/icon/article-abstract/19/5/1756/6515705#:~:text=one%20starting%20point%20for%20further,national%20and%20a%20global%20one)).  As one analysis notes, the most iconic cultural monuments thus acquire a “twofold cultural identity: a national and a global one” ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/icon/article-abstract/19/5/1756/6515705#:~:text=one%20starting%20point%20for%20further,national%20and%20a%20global%20one)).  This overlap turns unique national heritage into part of a common human patrimony, illustrating how universal values (the importance of heritage preservation) are woven into particularistic pride. In effect, education and policy present these landmarks as affirmations of both the nation’s uniqueness **and** its contributions to humankind. 

- **Education and narratives:**  In school curricula and national symbols, countries routinely **recast universal ideals as homegrown**.  For instance, many histories and civic lessons portray national heroes or founding figures as champions of values like freedom or equality – values then presented as part of the nation’s legacy.  In Europe today, some governments even explicitly highlight the human rights they uphold as *the core of their own national identity* ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/ojlr/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/ojlr/rwu038#:~:text=Largely%20due%20to%20the%20increasing,and%20national%20identities%20that%20overlooks)).  By embedding universal norms in the country’s traditions or laws, the universal becomes particular. At the same time, civic education often teaches respect for local culture (the unique heritage and traditions) alongside global principles, so students internalize both. 

- **Philosophical justification:**  Intellectual frameworks also support the blend.  Pluralists argue that **cultural diversity itself is a universal value**.  One scholar puts it bluntly: *“the diversity of cultures (preserved in a relatively pure state) is an important good”* ([books.openedition.org](https://books.openedition.org/ceup/475?lang=en#:~:text=,One%5D%20reason%20there%20is)).  From this perspective, each nation’s unique culture enriches humanity, so protecting particular traditions is consistent with a broader universalist ethic.  By viewing every national culture as a complementary piece in a global “mosaic” of ways of life, the inherent tension is reframed as mutually reinforcing: national uniqueness is prized not only for its own sake but because it contributes to the common good ([books.openedition.org](https://books.openedition.org/ceup/475?lang=en#:~:text=,One%5D%20reason%20there%20is)). 

- **Resisting homogenization:**  Of course, universalism and particularism can conflict in practice.  Some political movements warn that adopting external “universal” norms without adaptation can feel inauthentic.  For example, studies of post-Soviet Russia describe how media and elites at times disparaged “illusory universal human values” imposed by outside powers, arguing instead for a return to traditional Russian ethics ([www.europeanproceedings.com](https://www.europeanproceedings.com/article/10.15405/epsbs.2019.12.04.238#:~:text=globally%20standardized%20life%20well,from%20illusory%20universal%20human%20values)).  This criticism underscores the ongoing balancing act: national identity construction often involves choosing which universal ideals to embrace and how to blend them with local values. In volatile global times, many publics react by defending cultural uniqueness as threatened, leading governments and educators to reaffirm distinctive national values even as they pledge support for widely shared principles ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/jope/article/59/3-4/464/8104042#:~:text=a%20global%20order%20are%20in,Beyond%20the)). 

In sum, the tension is **maintained** not by resolving one side in favor of the other, but by the continual negotiation and narrative framing of both.  National identity discourses typically claim to embody universal values *on their own terms* (for example, by teaching that “our” tradition already includes democracy or human rights), and likewise present cultural uniqueness as the nation’s particular way of realizing humane ideals.  In this way, the unique and the universal coexist indefinitely – national identity becomes a tapestry where local heritage and global values are each given a thread, and citizens learn to see the nation as both distinct and yet part of a larger human family ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/icon/article-abstract/19/5/1756/6515705#:~:text=one%20starting%20point%20for%20further,national%20and%20a%20global%20one)) ([www.inst.at](https://www.inst.at/kulturen/unesco/strategy_english.html#:~:text=confiance%20dans%20les%20institutions%20internationales,conservative%2C%20this%20conception%20was%20still)).
