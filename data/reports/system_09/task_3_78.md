# Attribution Weight in Conversion Models

In a purchase conversion model, *attribution weight* refers to the credit or share of conversions that are assigned to marketing efforts (ads, campaigns, etc.) versus other factors.  In practice, one typically compares the model’s predicted conversions **with** the marketing intervention to a counterfactual **baseline** scenario without it.  The difference (extra conversions caused by marketing) is then allocated back to the marketing channel(s).  For example, one might predict conversion counts given the actual marketing exposures and again with those exposures set to zero – the *incremental conversions* (the gap between the two forecasts) represent the effect of marketing.  Attribution weight is then essentially this incremental effect divided by the total conversions, so that all channel credits sum to 100%.   In short, we “ponderate” each campaign or touchpoint by its *responsibility* for driving the final purchase ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=attribution%20models,responsibility%20in%20the%20final%20conversion)).  

Concretely, a common data-driven method is to compute a *removal effect*: simulate the model with a particular marketing channel removed and measure the change in total conversions.  One uses the model (or an equivalent probabilistic framework) to forecast conversions under the original marketing mix and again under a scenario where the channel’s influence is zero.  The drop in conversions when the channel is removed is its contribution.  The marketing channel’s attribution weight is then its drop (incremental loss) divided by the overall conversions.  This approach is analogous to calculating *incremental conversions*, as often discussed in marketing analytics – basically, “the extra conversions that happen because of your marketing” ([insiderone.com](https://insiderone.com/glossary/incremental-conversion/#:~:text=Incremental%20conversion%20is%20the%20extra,campaign%20created%2040%20extra%20conversions)).  For instance, if a campaign causes 40 additional purchases (versus what would have happened without it) out of 200 total purchases, the campaign’s attribution weight is 40/200 = 20%.  Studies and guides on attribution make clear that the core idea is to allocate credit based on how conversions change when a channel is held out or included ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=The%20second%20step%20is%C2%A0%20to,This%20calculation%20is%20done%20by)) ([insiderone.com](https://insiderone.com/glossary/incremental-conversion/#:~:text=Incremental%20conversion%20is%20the%20extra,campaign%20created%2040%20extra%20conversions)). 

In more advanced (e.g. multi-channel) settings, algorithmic methods are used to ensure a fair split.  For example, cooperative‐game approaches like **Shapley value attribution** compute each channel’s average *marginal* contribution across all possible paths.  By construction, Shapley-based weights satisfy intuitive axioms – notably, the sum of all channels’ weights equals the total conversions (the *Efficiency* property) and a channel that adds no extra conversions gets zero weight ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=Shapley%20value%20has%20become%20popular,allocation%20which%20satisfies%20different%20axioms)).  Similarly, Markov-chain models compute each channel’s “removal effect” by recalculating the funnel dynamics without that channel ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=The%20second%20step%20is%C2%A0%20to,This%20calculation%20is%20done%20by)).  In either case, the final attribution weight for marketing effects is essentially (conversion forecast with marketing – baseline forecast without marketing) divided by total conversions, which yields the proportion of conversions credited to the marketing inputs ([data-science-blog.com](https://data-science-blog.com/blog/2019/04/18/attribution-models-in-marketing/#:~:text=The%20second%20step%20is%C2%A0%20to,This%20calculation%20is%20done%20by)) ([insiderone.com](https://insiderone.com/glossary/incremental-conversion/#:~:text=Incremental%20conversion%20is%20the%20extra,campaign%20created%2040%20extra%20conversions)).
