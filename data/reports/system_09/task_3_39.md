# Spatial vs. Temporal Demand Patterns

In practice, an OD (origin–destination) matrix varies both in space (different parts of a city or network) and time (rush hours vs. off‐peak).  Where trips concentrate in space or time, capacity must be concentrated as well.  In high‐density areas (e.g. a dense downtown core) or peak windows (e.g. morning rush), large numbers of travelers share the same corridors.  Conversely, in low‐density suburbs or late at night, demand is sparse.  Transport literature emphasizes that **transit capacity (service frequency, vehicle size, etc.) should track these patterns**.  For example, doubling residential density more than doubles (in fact, roughly quadruples) transit demand ([nap.nationalacademies.org](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24766/chapter/4#:~:text=match%20at%20L1311%20a%20person,7%20households)).  Thus steep spatial density gradients (a dense center, sparse outskirts) lead to very uneven OD flows: planners must allocate **more vehicles and higher frequency** on busy routes and far fewer where demand is light.  Similarly, strong temporal peaks require ramping up capacity at peak hours and scaling back off‐peak (for instance, very high passenger volumes in one hour vs. near-empty vehicles later, implying different capacity allocations by time slice).

# Effects of Density Gradients

Under a **shallow gradient** (roughly uniform or gently varying demand), OD trips are spread out.  Capacity can be distributed more evenly – all parts of the network see moderate use.  There is less “wasted” empty capacity, and fixed schedules with moderate frequencies often suffice.  Under a **steep gradient** (sharp density change between zones), one or a few zones dominate demand.  In those “hotspot” zones, capacity must be very high (more and larger vehicles, multiple concurrent routes) to avoid saturation.  The outskirts see far fewer riders, so allocating the same capacity there would be wasteful.  If not adjusted, routes serving the core become overcrowded while those to the periphery carry many empty seats.

Researchers have quantified this tradeoff.  Quadrifoglio and Li (2009) formulate a **critical demand density** for choosing service type: above this threshold (high-density area), fixed-route, high-capacity transit is best; below it (low-density), flexible on-demand vehicles serve more efficiently ([www.scribd.com](https://www.scribd.com/document/398063619/quadrifoglio2009-pdf#:~:text=critical%20demand%20density%20qc%20at,%2823)).  In effect, a steep density gradient (low average density) pushes parts of the network below this threshold, suggesting smaller vehicles or hybrid services, while high-density areas exceed it, justifying heavy-capacity modes.  Put simply, **as population/trip density rises, capacity must rise faster than linearly** to carry all riders: e.g. doubling households per area can quadruple total transit trips ([nap.nationalacademies.org](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24766/chapter/4#:~:text=match%20at%20L1311%20a%20person,7%20households)), so service frequency or vehicle size often must more than double.  In contrast, where density falls off, capacity can shrink rapidly (fewer lines, longer headways) without losing efficiency.

# Spatiotemporal Alignment of Supply

Because OD matrices also change over time, capacity allocation is inherently dynamic.  A route carrying 500 passengers/hour at 8 AM might carry only 50/hour at noon.  Good planning **matches capacity to the time‐varying OD demand**.  In periods of very high demand (steep temporal “peak”), operators may temporarily	insert extra vehicles or run express services, whereas in the off-peak the same vehicles may be idle or rebalanced elsewhere.  If planners ignore these temporal peaks, they risk either severe crowding (if capacity stays at average level) or wasted service (if fenced to off-peak demand). 

Similarly from a spatial standpoint, **service design adapts to OD patterns**.  For example, demand-responsive (on-demand) vehicles or flexible routing may be used in low-density wards (short, infrequent trips), whereas fixed-route buses or trains serve dense corridors at high frequency.  This matches the well-known supply‐meets‐demand principle: a transport network must “allocate capacity where trips occur” ([transportgeography.org](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter3/provision-and-demand-of-transportation/#:~:text=Transportation%20is%20a%20market%20composed,satisfy%20transport%20needs%20for%20the)). 

# Summary

In summary, **steeper spatial or temporal gradients of OD demand require more uneven capacity allocations**.  High–density, high-flow zones/times get disproportionately more vehicles (often replaced by larger vehicles or shorter headways), while low-density zones demand little service or on-demand shuttles.  Uniform demand patterns allow more uniform capacity.  Planners often use indicators (like peak-to-off-peak ratios or residential density) to calibrate service: for instance, doubling household density can lead to a fourfold increase in ridership ([nap.nationalacademies.org](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/24766/chapter/4#:~:text=match%20at%20L1311%20a%20person,7%20households)), so capacity is ramped up accordingly.  Likewise, transit agencies routinely run “peak schedules” (extra trips/cruises) during rush hours.  In all cases, the *spatial and temporal shape of the OD matrix* directly drives **how much capacity is needed where and when** – a concentrated OD pattern calls for concentrated capacity, while a diffuse OD pattern allows a more spread-out allocation ([transportgeography.org](https://transportgeography.org/contents/chapter3/provision-and-demand-of-transportation/#:~:text=Transportation%20is%20a%20market%20composed,satisfy%20transport%20needs%20for%20the)) ([www.scribd.com](https://www.scribd.com/document/398063619/quadrifoglio2009-pdf#:~:text=critical%20demand%20density%20qc%20at,%2823)).
