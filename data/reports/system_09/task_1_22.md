# Behavioral vs. Prospect-Theory Models

Behavioral game‐theoretic models typically **modify the equilibrium concept** rather than only changing payoffs.  For example, one common approach is the **quantal‐response equilibrium (QRE)**, where players still best‐respond in expectation but with “noise.”  In QRE, each player “plays a **noisy best response**” ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4336/6/4/637#:~:text=action,but%20otherwise%20have%20consistent%20beliefs)).  In practice this means the model is a compromise between the pure Nash equilibrium and random behavior: players usually choose the high‐payoff strategies but sometimes err.  In effect, a QRE or similar bounded‐rationality model **“balances”** the rational equilibrium with empirically observed decision errors.  For instance, Camerer et al. explain that QRE assumes players use consistent beliefs but allow probabilistic mistakes ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4336/6/4/637#:~:text=action,but%20otherwise%20have%20consistent%20beliefs)), which smooths out extreme risk attitudes without completely abandoning strategic rationality.  More generally, cognitive‐hierarchy or level-*k* models similarly layer in limited reasoning on top of a Nash‐like framework.  

By contrast, a pure **prospect‐theory parameterization** changes the **utility/payoff function** directly to be reference-dependent and highly loss‐averse.  Prospect theory typically uses an S‐shaped “value” function and weighs losses by a factor λ>1 (often calibrated around 2).  In practice, this means losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains.  Embedding such a utility in a strategic model tends to **amplify the impact of losses**.  Indeed, formal analyses show dramatic effects: one game‐theory study finds that under expectation‐based loss aversion, any randomization *hurts* the player.  In their model, “any mixture over pure strategies **decreases a player’s utility**” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899825617301082#:~:text=For%20the%20case%20of%20choice,consequences%20of%20these%20pure%20strategies)), so loss‐averse players strictly avoid mixed (randomized) strategies.  The consequence is that many standard equilibria break down – e.g. a mixed‐strategy equilibrium may no longer exist ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899825617301082#:~:text=For%20the%20case%20of%20choice,consequences%20of%20these%20pure%20strategies)).  In short, prospect‐theory preferences make players extremely unwilling to risk losses, far more than in a typical Nash model.  

**Key points:** 

- **Behavioral equilibria (e.g. QRE):** Represent a noisy or bounded‐rational version of Nash.  Players mostly play best responses but with error, so the model *softens* pure rational predictions.  It *narrows* the gap between full rationality and observed bias by smoothing strategies ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4336/6/4/637#:~:text=action,but%20otherwise%20have%20consistent%20beliefs)).  

- **Prospect‐theory utility:** Directly encodes strong loss aversion (λ>1) into payoffs.  This typically *widens* the gap by heavily penalizing losses.  Players will behave much more conservatively relative to a Nash benchmark, often resisting randomness or risk to avoid losses ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899825617301082#:~:text=For%20the%20case%20of%20choice,consequences%20of%20these%20pure%20strategies)).  

- **Impact on outcomes:**  With a behavioral equilibrium approach, one still solves for something like an equilibrium, with biases only moderately changing strategy probabilities.  In contrast, prospect‐based models often produce very different outcomes – for example, a recent Bayesian game model of investment shows that introducing biases “distorts optimal portfolios and alters aggregate market dynamics” ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18835#:~:text=with%20economic%20game%20theory%2C%20the,making)), leading to inefficiencies (bubbles, crashes, etc.).  

**Conclusion:**  In practice, a behavioral‐game‐theory model can be constructed either way.  If you build in **bounded rationality** (QRE, level-*k*, etc.), the result is essentially a biased equilibrium that tempers rational play.  If you instead use a **prospect‐theory utility function**, you will by design amplify loss‐aversion effects – losses loom larger and push behavior further from the risk‐neutral (or classical risk‐averse) benchmark.  Most models that literally use prospect parameters will therefore emphasize the loss side of the S‐curve, whereas a behavioral equilibrium model tends to spread out behavior around the Nash prediction without inherently doubling losses ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899825617301082#:~:text=For%20the%20case%20of%20choice,consequences%20of%20these%20pure%20strategies)) ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18835#:~:text=with%20economic%20game%20theory%2C%20the,making)).

**Sources:**  The distinction between equilibrium‐adjustment models and prospect‐theory models is well documented.  For example, one study notes QRE assumes a “noisy best response” ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4336/6/4/637#:~:text=action,but%20otherwise%20have%20consistent%20beliefs)) (bounded rationality) whereas expectation‐based loss aversion leads players to avoid any risky mixing of strategies ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899825617301082#:~:text=For%20the%20case%20of%20choice,consequences%20of%20these%20pure%20strategies)).  Empirical/game‐theoretic results similarly show that prospect‐type biases distort portfolios and market equilibrium ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18835#:~:text=with%20economic%20game%20theory%2C%20the,making)).
