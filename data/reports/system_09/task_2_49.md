Institutional rebalancing routines today explicitly trade off tracking‐error and trading cost.  In practice managers augment the usual Markowitz-style optimization with cost models or constraints, so that slight deviations from the target (small tracking error) are allowed in order to save on execution costs.  For example, one can add a penalty or cap on transaction costs in the optimization, yielding an “efficient frontier” that jointly reflects portfolio risk (or tracking error) and execution cost ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305048312000680#:~:text=results%20when%20we%20have%20transaction,involving%20up%20to%201317%20assets)) ([breakingalpha.io](https://breakingalpha.io/insights/market-impact-minimization-large-portfolios.html#:~:text=Portfolio%20execution%20optimization%20must%20explicitly,off)).  This forces the algorithm to refrain from over‐trading: rebalance only when a portfolio drifts enough to justify the cost.  The fundamental trade‐off here is well known – rebalance too often (tight tracking) and costs explode; rebalance too infrequently and tracking error grows ([bookdown.org](https://bookdown.org/palomar/portfoliooptimizationbook/6.1-fundamentals.html#:~:text=6)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9091/12/10/155#:~:text=falls%20short%20in%20addressing%20the,could%20outweigh%20any%20benefit%20gained)). 

In modern practice, sophisticated algorithms *do* incorporate explicit cost models.  For instance, one can include a quadratic market‐impact term in the mean‐variance objective or impose a constraint on total expected slippage.  This stabilizes the portfolio weights: turnover is often cut in half or more, and execution costs can drop by 80–90% relative to an unconstrained strategy ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0275531923001885#:~:text=substantially%20higher%20,on%20average)).  Index‐tracking studies make the same point – adding a transaction‐cost penalty actually **improves** effective tracking.  Naïvely optimizing for tracking error alone may squeeze out only marginal gains while incurring huge costs, whereas jointly minimizing cost and error yields a better overall trade‐off ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9091/12/10/155#:~:text=falls%20short%20in%20addressing%20the,could%20outweigh%20any%20benefit%20gained)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9091/12/10/155#:~:text=transaction%20costs%20and%20implements%20this,lead%20to%20better%20tracking%20performance)). 

In short, true institutional rebalancers do balance tracking error against cost.  A pure mean‐variance reoptimization that ignores market impact will tend to “over‐trade” and suffer larger execution friction.  By contrast, including frictions in the optimization (or shrinking the efficient‐frontier weights toward a no‐trade band) lets one control turnover.  Empirically, cost-aware mean‐variance schemes dramatically reduce “turnover drag” – essentially driving execution costs toward zero in backtests ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0275531923001885#:~:text=substantially%20higher%20,on%20average)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9091/12/10/155#:~:text=transaction%20costs%20and%20implements%20this,lead%20to%20better%20tracking%20performance)) – whereas blind MVO without such controls can amplify market‐impact losses.  In other words, the solution is not to abandon mean‐variance altogether, but to fold in explicit cost (impact) terms so that the optimizer naturally trades off tracking error against execution cost.
