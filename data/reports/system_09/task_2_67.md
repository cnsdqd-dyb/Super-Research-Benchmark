In practice, most experts balance MRI’s very high sensitivity with its false‐positive burden by **tailoring the schedule and protocol to each woman’s risk**.  In very high‐risk women (e.g. BRCA carriers), for example, one model suggests starting annual contrast‐enhanced MRI at ~25 and then **alternating MRI with mammography after age 30** ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12536305/#:~:text=comprehensive%20simulation%20model%20to%20show,41)).  This approach captures aggressive cancers early (MRI finds ~90–100% of cancers in this group) while reducing unnecessary additional imaging from mammograms (which chiefly add detections of low‐grade DCIS) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6424827/#:~:text=lesions%20depicted%20solely%20by%20mammography,and%20reduce%20overdiagnosis%20and%20overtreatment)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12536305/#:~:text=Mammography%20can%20increase%20sensitivity%20by,of%20DCIS%20in%20the%20general)).  In fact, MRI‐detected lesions are much more often invasive, whereas mammography often finds indolent DCIS that may represent overdiagnosis ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6424827/#:~:text=lesions%20depicted%20solely%20by%20mammography,and%20reduce%20overdiagnosis%20and%20overtreatment)). 

Other strategies focus on the MRI exam itself.  **“Abbreviated” MRI protocols** (using only the first post‐contrast sequence and key reconstructions) maintain essentially the same cancer detection as a full MRI but dramatically cut scan/reading time and tend to **reduce benign false positives**.  Because cancers light up early after contrast and benign/background enhancement tends to build up slowly, the abbreviated exam “highlights” tumors and diminishes nonspecific findings ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6424827/#:~:text=The%20rationale%20for%20using%20the,and%20lower%20false%20positive%20interpretations)).  Similarly, having a baseline (prevalence) MRI on file makes subsequent annual MRIs far easier to interpret – recall rates and biopsy recommendations drop sharply on follow‐up scans once a prior image is available ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899707120301686#:~:text=The%20higher%20rate%20of%20abnormal,MRIs%20to%20decrease%20false%20positives)). 

Finally, many centers use **risk‐stratified triage** of MRI findings.  For example, adding clinical risk factors or AI tools to MR imaging has been shown to safely eliminate a large fraction of false‐positive recalls without missing cancers ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10973307/#:~:text=Interestingly%2C%20a%20further%20analysis%20of,biopsies%2C%20without%20missing%20any%20cancers)).  In short, the “sweet spot” in high-risk screening is usually annual DCE-MRI (often with a staggered mammogram schedule) coupled with modern approaches (abbreviated protocols, prior‐exam comparison, predictive algorithms) that preserve MRI’s sensitivity while cutting down on unneeded biopsies or over-diagnosed indolent lesions ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6424827/#:~:text=The%20rationale%20for%20using%20the,and%20lower%20false%20positive%20interpretations)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6424827/#:~:text=lesions%20depicted%20solely%20by%20mammography,and%20reduce%20overdiagnosis%20and%20overtreatment)).
