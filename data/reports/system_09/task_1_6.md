When training very large networks, the best practice is to **use mixed‐precision gradients** (e.g. FP16/BF16) with FP32 accumulators and dynamic loss scaling.  Mixed‐precision backprop (FP16 forward/backward with a FP32 “master” copy of weights) can **double throughput and halve memory use** without hurting accuracy ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=Mixed,34%3B%20Krishnamoorthi%202018)) ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=While%20FP16%20is%20efficient%2C%20its,model%20converges%20correctly%20during%20training)).  In practice one multiplies the loss by a large factor before backprop and then divides the gradients back (loss-scaling) to keep tiny gradients out of FP16 underflow ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=One%20of%20the%20key%20challenges,the%20representable%20range%20of%20FP16)) ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=hence%20the%20need%20for%20loss,effectively%20without%20sacrificing%20numerical%20stability)).  This preserves **convergence stability**: keeping weight updates in FP32 avoids overflow/underflow so the model converges correctly ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=While%20FP16%20is%20efficient%2C%20its,model%20converges%20correctly%20during%20training)).  If available, use **bfloat16 or NVIDIA’s TF32** instead of pure FP16 – BF16 offers the same exponent range as FP32 (vastly reducing gradient underflows) and TF32 (19-bit) adds extra range while still being faster than FP32 ([medium.com](https://medium.com/%40jbensnyder/solving-the-limits-of-mixed-precision-training-231019128b4b#:~:text=Newer%20training%20hardware%20offers%20a,been%20shown%20to%20degrade%20convergence)) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/%40jbensnyder/solving-the-limits-of-mixed-precision-training-231019128b4b#:~:text=If%20more%20precision%20is%20required%2C,the%20start%20of%20your%20training)).  These formats give a good precision/resource compromise (large range for stability, half-precision performance for speed).

## Mixed‐Precision and Loss Scaling  
- **Reduced precision for most ops:** Perform forwards and backwards in FP16/BF16, but keep a higher-precision (FP32) copy of weights and accumulators to update.  This uses ~2× less memory and runs much faster, yet (with proper scaling) yields accuracy on par with FP32 ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=Mixed,34%3B%20Krishnamoorthi%202018)) ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=While%20FP16%20is%20efficient%2C%20its,model%20converges%20correctly%20during%20training)).  
- **Dynamic loss scaling:** To avoid tiny gradients vanishing in FP16, scale up the loss (e.g. by 2^10) during backprop, then un-scale gradients before the optimizer step ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=One%20of%20the%20key%20challenges,the%20representable%20range%20of%20FP16)).  As the MLSystems text explains, this “allows FP16 to be used effectively without sacrificing numerical stability” ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=One%20of%20the%20key%20challenges,the%20representable%20range%20of%20FP16)) ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=hence%20the%20need%20for%20loss,effectively%20without%20sacrificing%20numerical%20stability)).  
- **FP32 accumulation:** Always accumulate gradients and apply weight updates in FP32.  This hybrid scheme “avoids the risk of gradient underflow or overflow, ensuring the model converges correctly during training” ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=While%20FP16%20is%20efficient%2C%20its,model%20converges%20correctly%20during%20training)). 

## Handling Large Batches and Stability  
- **Layer-wise/adaptive optimizers:**  For very large scale training (large batches), use optimizers that adjust learning rates per layer.  For example, LARS/LAMB scale each layer’s update by ||w||/||g||, which “helps stabilize the training process… when using larger batch sizes” ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/pretraining-bert-with-layer-wise-adaptive-learning-rates/#:~:text=%2A%20Layer,achieving%20convergence%20in%20BERT%20pretraining)).  NVIDIA’s LAMB (Adam-like with layerwise scaling and decoupled weight decay) is widely used for training giant language models; it preserves convergence speed without loss of accuracy ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/pretraining-bert-with-layer-wise-adaptive-learning-rates/#:~:text=%2A%20Layer,achieving%20convergence%20in%20BERT%20pretraining)) ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/pretraining-bert-with-layer-wise-adaptive-learning-rates/#:~:text=w,new%20version%20of%20Adam%20%E2%80%93)).  
- **Gradient clipping and warmup:** Clip or bound the gradient norm to prevent occasional spikes from destabilizing training.  Use learning-rate warmup (and bias-correction) in the first few steps to avoid large initial updates.  These techniques (along with loss scaling above) greatly improve convergence stability for deep networks.  
- **Regularized optimizers for generalization:**  If full precision isn’t a concern, using **SGD with momentum** (and decoupled weight decay) often yields better generalization than raw Adam ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/pretraining-bert-with-layer-wise-adaptive-learning-rates/#:~:text=w,new%20version%20of%20Adam%20%E2%80%93)).  (In practice many large-model trainings use AdamW for faster convergence plus weight decay for regularization.)  Layer-wise adaptive optimizers like LAMB essentially combine AdamW with SGD-like scaling ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/pretraining-bert-with-layer-wise-adaptive-learning-rates/#:~:text=w,new%20version%20of%20Adam%20%E2%80%93)) ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/pretraining-bert-with-layer-wise-adaptive-learning-rates/#:~:text=%2A%20Layer,achieving%20convergence%20in%20BERT%20pretraining)), balancing speed, stability and implicit regularization. 

In summary, the gold-standard strategy for large models is **automatic mixed precision with FP32 master weights and dynamic loss scaling** (saving memory and compute) combined with robust update rules (momentum/AdamW or LAMB) and gradient clipping.  This mix gives stable convergence, preserves generalization, and cuts resource use in half ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=Mixed,34%3B%20Krishnamoorthi%202018)) ([mlsysbook.ai](https://mlsysbook.ai/book/contents/core/training/training.html#:~:text=While%20FP16%20is%20efficient%2C%20its,model%20converges%20correctly%20during%20training)).
