One can think of “global governance” as a high‐dimensional complex system of states and organizations whose interactions may admit multiple attractors (stable regimes) or even strange (chaotic) attractors. In short, networked models of many interacting state‐like units tend to exhibit complex attractor structures with both periodic and chaotic behavior as parameters vary.  

When we introduce **time‐lags** into such a system, the effect is to increase its effective dimensionality and memory – which almost invariably makes the dynamics *more* complex.  Time delays are well known in dynamical systems theory to induce oscillations and chaos that were not present without delay.  As one review put it: “time delays … are hence ubiquitous, [and] likely to induce instabilities and with it various kinds of chaotic phases” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0370157319302601#:~:text=The%20time%20needed%20to%20exchange,results%2C%20of%20the%20standard%20numerical)).  In other words, even a simple feedback loop that would be stable with instantaneous response can become oscillatory or chaotic when a delay is added.  In the global governance context, “national sovereignty parameters” (e.g. the degree of domestic autonomy or the speed of adopting international rules) effectively act like delay terms in the feedback loops between states.  For instance, if countries routinely take years to ratify agreements or implement policies, then changes in one country will only influence the next rounds of global policy after a lag.  These lags change the timing of feedback and can create new cycles or irregular swings that reshape the attractor landscape.  In control‐theoretic language, adding delay often creates new Hopf bifurcation points or even strange attractors where none existed before.
