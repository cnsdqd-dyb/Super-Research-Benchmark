In practice, the most effective sparsification schemes for hardware are **structured pattern pruning** methods.  For example, NVIDIA’s A100 (Ampere) GPUs use a *fine‐grained 2:4 sparsity* pattern – exactly two zeros in every block of four weights ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerating-inference-with-sparsity-using-ampere-and-tensorrt/#:~:text=,gain%20on%20an%20A100%20GPU)).  This fixed pattern gives 50% weight reduction with virtually no accuracy loss after a simple retraining ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerating-inference-with-sparsity-using-ampere-and-tensorrt/#:~:text=,gain%20on%20an%20A100%20GPU)).  Because the zeros are regular and contiguous, the pattern is very hardware‐friendly: it compresses easily (low metadata overhead) and effectively doubles the compute density while halving off‐chip memory usage ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerating-inference-with-sparsity-using-ampere-and-tensorrt/#:~:text=The%20NVIDIA%20A100%20GPU%20adds,Figure%201)).

More aggressive block‐structured pruning can achieve even higher sparsity with minimal accuracy drop.  For instance, the **kernel-row pruning (KRP)** method removes whole rows of convolutional filters.  In published experiments it achieved roughly *27×* model‐size compression “losslessly” (i.e. without degrading accuracy) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/2/824#:~:text=models,model%20in%20FPGAs%20with%20a)).  By pruning in blocks (channels, filter rows, etc.), the zeros form contiguous blocks that are easy for hardware to skip, which greatly reduces memory I/O and boosts PE utilization ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/2/824#:~:text=models,model%20in%20FPGAs%20with%20a)).

In summary, algorithms that enforce *hardware‐friendly structured sparsity* give the best compression/accuracy trade‐off.  Fixed 2:4 patterns (as in NVIDIA’s Tensor Cores) yield ≈2× compression with near-zero accuracy loss ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerating-inference-with-sparsity-using-ampere-and-tensorrt/#:~:text=,gain%20on%20an%20A100%20GPU)), while larger block pruning schemes (rows or channels) can push to 10× or higher sparsity with only minor accuracy impact ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/2/824#:~:text=models,model%20in%20FPGAs%20with%20a)).  These structured sparsification methods maximize computational density and minimize memory bandwidth because the zeros are regular and low-overhead, making them the optimal choice for neural‐net accelerators ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerating-inference-with-sparsity-using-ampere-and-tensorrt/#:~:text=,gain%20on%20an%20A100%20GPU)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/2/824#:~:text=models,model%20in%20FPGAs%20with%20a)).

**Answer:**  Structured (block/pattern) pruning – e.g. NVIDIA’s 2:4 fine-grained sparsity or block-wise (row/channel) pruning – offers the best trade-off. These methods deliver very high compression (≈2× and up) with negligible accuracy loss, greatly improving compute density and reducing memory bandwidth demands ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerating-inference-with-sparsity-using-ampere-and-tensorrt/#:~:text=,gain%20on%20an%20A100%20GPU)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/2/824#:~:text=models,model%20in%20FPGAs%20with%20a)).
