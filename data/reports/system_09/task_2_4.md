The key is that a well-engineered CNT/matrix interface can **carry load efficiently but still yield locally via shear**.  In practice the nanotubes are strongly bonded (often via covalent or semi‐coherent interphases) so that under tension most of the stress transfers into the stiff CNTs.  However, the extreme stiffness mismatch creates very high shear stresses at the interface – in fact experiments show interfacial slip can begin at extremely low strains (on the order of 0.03 %) due to this shear concentration ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.5b01459#:~:text=slip%20mechanics%20associated%20with%20shear,Through%20micromechanics%20modeling%20and%20by)).  In other words, the interface first transfers load to the CNT, then **yields by a controlled “stick–slip”** process.  

Under tension, the reinforced interface behaves like a tough glue: intact bonded regions continue to carry stress (giving high load‐transfer), but local flaws or “defect zones” slip and debond in small increments.  These slip events (mechanical interlocking and van der Waals bonds breaking/reforming) generate nonlinear damping and dissipate energy ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.5b01459#:~:text=nonlinear%20variation%20of%20damping%20with,covalent%20bonding%20to%20shear%20interactions)).  At the same time, the CNTs act as strong anchors for dislocations in the matrix.  Dislocations pile up and bow around the CNT (an Orowan‐type loop), effectively pinning dislocations and forcing them to bypass the fiber ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379725002086#:~:text=gradually%20slip%20around%20the%20CNT,angle%20embedding)).  The motion and multiplication of these interface dislocations absorb strain energy.  In short, the interface reconciliation works by *concurrent* load‐transfer and plasticity: the CNT carries most of the tensile load (stress‐transfer efficiency), while the surrounding matrix at the CNT interface shears and emits dislocations (defect‐driven yielding) to soak up energy ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379725002086#:~:text=gradually%20slip%20around%20the%20CNT,angle%20embedding)) ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.5b01459#:~:text=nonlinear%20variation%20of%20damping%20with,covalent%20bonding%20to%20shear%20interactions)).  

This combined mechanism – strong binding for stiffness plus controlled interface slip for plastic deformation – lets the composite remain strong yet tough.  The CNT/matrix boundary “gives” by stick-slip and dislocation motion rather than by catastrophic cracking, so stress is efficiently funneled into the nanotube while excess energy is dissipated through interfacial plasticity and friction ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.5b01459#:~:text=nonlinear%20variation%20of%20damping%20with,covalent%20bonding%20to%20shear%20interactions)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379725002086#:~:text=gradually%20slip%20around%20the%20CNT,angle%20embedding)). 

**Answer:** By deliberately making the CNT interface strong yet able to “slip” at defects, so that under tension the CNT continued to carry load (high stress‐transfer), while the interface itself yielded through dislocation motion and stick–slip.  In practice the CNT/matrix boundary (often semi‐coherent or mechanically interlocked) pins dislocations (Orowan loops) and initially transfers stress into the tube, but when local shear stress is high it slips/decoheres at tiny scales (“slip–stick” events).  This causes matrix dislocations to accumulate and flow around the interface, dissipating strain energy, even as the nanotube still bears most of the load ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379725002086#:~:text=gradually%20slip%20around%20the%20CNT,angle%20embedding)) ([pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsami.5b01459#:~:text=nonlinear%20variation%20of%20damping%20with,covalent%20bonding%20to%20shear%20interactions)).  Such an interfacial stick–slip+Orowan mechanism thus reconciles efficient load transfer with defect‐driven energy dissipation.
