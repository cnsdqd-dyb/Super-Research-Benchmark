# Price adjustment amplitude boundaries in dynamic pricing  
In practice, the allowable “amplitude” of a price change is controlled by pre‐set business and algorithmic constraints.  Dynamic pricing systems typically impose **floors and ceilings** on prices to prevent extreme swings ([fastercapital.com](https://fastercapital.com/content/Dynamic-Pricing--Navigating-the-Waves-of-Dynamic-Pricing--Strategies-for-Maximizing-Revenue.html#:~:text=5,ceilings)).  A common approach is to define a **minimum price** (e.g. cost plus a target margin) and a **maximum price** (often tied to competitor pricing or demand thresholds). This ensures the algorithm never cuts prices below a sustainable level or pushes them unreasonably high. For example, many tools let you set a **minimum margin** so that the price never drops below (cost + X%). In one example, a retailer set a 5% margin rule so the price could never fall below the unit cost+5% ([www.pricesearch.io](https://www.pricesearch.io/how-to-use-dynamic-pricing-to-beat-the-market/#:~:text=rule%20changes%20the%20price%20of,percentage%20will%20be%20the%20margin)). When competitors temporarily slash prices, the algorithm will drop your price accordingly but will not violate this floor. 

- **Cost and margin constraints:**  Companies often embed their cost structure and profit targets as hard bounds.  A minimum price floor (cost + desired margin) is enforced, and sometimes a maximum price is set (e.g. based on a competitor’s price or premium strategy) ([www.pricesearch.io](https://www.pricesearch.io/how-to-use-dynamic-pricing-to-beat-the-market/#:~:text=rule%20changes%20the%20price%20of,percentage%20will%20be%20the%20margin)) ([fastercapital.com](https://fastercapital.com/content/Dynamic-Pricing--Navigating-the-Waves-of-Dynamic-Pricing--Strategies-for-Maximizing-Revenue.html#:~:text=5,ceilings)).  
- **Budget or ROI limits:**  Pricing algorithms may also use target ROI or subsidy budgets to cap adjustments.  For instance, an engine might increase or decrease prices only until a target ROI is met, effectively bounding the total adjustment.  
- **Demand elasticity and customer tolerance:**  Models often limit step‐size (e.g. “no more than ±X% per hour”) to avoid shocking the market. These limits (determined by historical elasticity or policy) serve as an “amplitude boundary” on how much price can move at once.  

In summary, the amplitude boundary is **not determined by one formula** but by the company’s rules and optimization goals.  It is typically set by factors like cost, competitive context, demand sensitivity, and risk management (often codified as min/max price rules) ([fastercapital.com](https://fastercapital.com/content/Dynamic-Pricing--Navigating-the-Waves-of-Dynamic-Pricing--Strategies-for-Maximizing-Revenue.html#:~:text=5,ceilings)) ([www.pricesearch.io](https://www.pricesearch.io/how-to-use-dynamic-pricing-to-beat-the-market/#:~:text=rule%20changes%20the%20price%20of,percentage%20will%20be%20the%20margin)), ensuring price changes stay within prescribed limits.  

**Sources:** Industry guidelines and dynamic pricing case studies emphasize setting price floors/ceilings as part of any algorithmic pricing strategy ([fastercapital.com](https://fastercapital.com/content/Dynamic-Pricing--Navigating-the-Waves-of-Dynamic-Pricing--Strategies-for-Maximizing-Revenue.html#:~:text=5,ceilings)) ([www.pricesearch.io](https://www.pricesearch.io/how-to-use-dynamic-pricing-to-beat-the-market/#:~:text=rule%20changes%20the%20price%20of,percentage%20will%20be%20the%20margin)). These constraints make the solution space well‐defined and prevent overly large adjustments.
