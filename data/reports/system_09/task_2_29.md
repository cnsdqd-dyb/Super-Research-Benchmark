A common solution is to build truly *graded* 3D‐printed scaffolds – for example by printing successive layers or regions with gradually changing porosity or fiber spacing.  In practice, one end of the scaffold is printed as a dense, fine‐pore “core” for strength and load‐bearing, while the other end is printed with larger pores and lower density to encourage cell ingrowth and nutrient flow.  This creates a built-in stiffness/porosity gradient that mimics natural tissue interfaces.  The fine‐pore layers maintain high mechanical stiffness and ensure structural integrity, while the more open layers (or fibers) guide cells by offering more surface area and diffusion paths.  Importantly, each zone can use a regular print pattern (constant filament diameter, consistent layer geometry) so that modern bioprinters (e.g. extrusion or melt-electrowriting systems) can fabricate it with high precision.  In effect, the scaffold’s design “steps” are small enough for the printer to resolve, and the material is continuous, so the overall construct remains robust. 

For example, Abbasi et al. demonstrated a melt-electrowritten PCL scaffold consisting of three horizontal layers with pore sizes of 750, 500, and 250 μm (large to small) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452199X22001773#:~:text=750%C2%A0%CE%BCm%29%20%28Fig,cell%20growth%20and%20infiltration%2C%20caused)).  The large‐pore top layer promoted nutrient/waste transport and cell infiltration, while the fine‐pore bottom layer bore most of the load.  In cell culture this graded design showed **far more cell proliferation and infiltration** than a uniform‐pore control, and in animal tests only the gradient scaffold achieved full bone healing ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452199X22001773#:~:text=750%C2%A0%CE%BCm%29%20%28Fig,cell%20growth%20and%20infiltration%2C%20caused)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452199X22001773#:~:text=gradient%20scaffolds%2C%20indicating%20that%20the,the%20inclusion%20of%20smaller%20pores)).  In other words, the mechanical gradient (stiff, fine‐pore base ➔ softer, coarse‐pore apex) supplied directional cues for cells without sacrificing strength.  As one review notes, such graded scaffolds “recapitulate the structural hierarchy of native tissues,” providing large‐pore regions for vascularization and diffusion *with only minimally reduced stability* thanks to the small‐pore load-bearing regions ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452199X22001773#:~:text=gradient%20scaffolds%2C%20indicating%20that%20the,the%20inclusion%20of%20smaller%20pores)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11814525/#:~:text=Current%20research%20efforts%20primarily%20focus,a%20matrix%20for%20cell%20adhesion)). 

In summary, a *functionally graded scaffold* – for instance a layered porosity/stiffness gradient – reconciles guided cell growth with printability.  Each layer uses a precise, consistent print pattern (assuring accuracy), while the overall gradation in filament thickness or spacing establishes the mechanical cues.  This approach gives the implanted construct both the strength to stay intact and a built-in directional scaffold that guides cells and tissue regeneration ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452199X22001773#:~:text=750%C2%A0%CE%BCm%29%20%28Fig,cell%20growth%20and%20infiltration%2C%20caused)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11814525/#:~:text=Current%20research%20efforts%20primarily%20focus,a%20matrix%20for%20cell%20adhesion)).

In practice this means, for example, printing an osteochondral scaffold with a stiff, small‐pore base (for support) and increasingly larger pores toward the other side.
