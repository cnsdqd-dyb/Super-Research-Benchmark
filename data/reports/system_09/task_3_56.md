# Phenotypic plasticity in invasives – definitions and role  
Phenotypic plasticity – the ability of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments – is a key trait that often underlies invasive success ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00213/full#:~:text=plasticity%20were%20found%20for%20clones,habitats%20across%20broad%20geographic%20areas)) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=evidence%20for%20a%20JM%20species,invasiveness%20in%20its%20range%20centre)).  Many invasive plants and animals show high plasticity, allowing them to tolerate a wide range of climate or habitat conditions even when genetic variation is low ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00213/full#:~:text=plasticity%20were%20found%20for%20clones,habitats%20across%20broad%20geographic%20areas)) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30697749/#:~:text=including%20trait%20variation%20over%20geographical,geographical%20gradients%20and%20new%20climates)).  In practice, plasticity broadens a species’ **fundamental niche** beyond what is inferred from any single environment or population. For example, a clonal weed invaded almost its entire potential climate space by relying on plasticity (not genetic diversity) to thrive in both wet and dry habitats ([www.frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00213/full#:~:text=plasticity%20were%20found%20for%20clones,habitats%20across%20broad%20geographic%20areas)).  

# Effects on predicted distributions and “at-risk” areas  
Standard ecological risk models – especially correlative species distribution or niche models (SDMs) – typically assume a fixed environmental tolerance derived from observed occurrences.  Invasives with high plasticity can occupy climates outside their native realized niche (a “niche shift”), undermining these models’ assumptions ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423003992#:~:text=Wood%2C%202014%2C%20Xian%20et%20al,in%20regions%20otherwise%20considered%20unsuitable)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L358%20The%20comparatively,models%20are%20more%20readily%20applicable)).  Empirical studies confirm that neglecting plasticity often leads models to **underestimate** invasion risk.  For instance, models built on native-range data alone often fail to predict invaded-area occurrences when the species has expanded its niche (and thus its range) through plasticity or rapid adaptation ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423003992#:~:text=Wood%2C%202014%2C%20Xian%20et%20al,in%20regions%20otherwise%20considered%20unsuitable)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L358%20The%20comparatively,models%20are%20more%20readily%20applicable)).  In one classic case, a mechanistic simulation showed that without plasticity, a tree invader would not reach the actual wet and dry range margins observed; plastic re-allocation of traits was needed to explain the full geographic spread ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=that%20temporal%20plasticity%20plays%20in,2010)) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=evidence%20for%20a%20JM%20species,invasiveness%20in%20its%20range%20centre)).

By contrast, models that **explicitly include plasticity effects** typically predict broader ranges and higher invasion risk.  In trait‐based or mechanistic models, parameters for tolerance or acclimation allow the species to “tolerate” climates beyond the observed niche ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=birds%20introduced%20to%20Europe%2C%20we,escalating%20impacts%20of%20invasive%20species)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423001737#:~:text=Incorporating%20intraspecific%20variation%20into%20species,spread%20plant%20species)).  For example, Martin‐Calvo et al. (2023) showed that mechanistic models using physiological thermal‐tolerance traits (implicitly integrating plastic responses) accurately identified climatically suitable regions for introduced birds – far better than simple correlative SDMs ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=birds%20introduced%20to%20Europe%2C%20we,escalating%20impacts%20of%20invasive%20species)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L358%20The%20comparatively,models%20are%20more%20readily%20applicable)).  Similarly, Benito Garzón et al. (2019) describe ΔTrait SDMs – models that include geographic variation in traits – and report that such models predict **“less alarming”** range contractions under climate change, because plasticity buffers species against environmental change ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30697749/#:~:text=including%20trait%20variation%20over%20geographical,geographical%20gradients%20and%20new%20climates)).  

# Impact on risk‐model accuracy  
In sum, phenotypic plasticity parameters can have a first‐order effect on risk predictions.  High plasticity usually **increases** the predicted area at risk, because the species can survive in environments that non‐plastic models would deem unsuitable.  Ignoring plasticity tends to **under‐predict** invaded or climatically suitable areas (false negatives), reducing the model’s sensitivity.  Conversely, including well‐estimated plasticity parameters makes predictions more accurate in two ways: it raises sensitivity (correctly forecasting invasion in marginal habitats) and can reduce bias in range‐shift projections ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423001737#:~:text=Incorporating%20intraspecific%20variation%20into%20species,spread%20plant%20species)) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=evidence%20for%20a%20JM%20species,invasiveness%20in%20its%20range%20centre)).  One study found that explicitly modeling intraspecific variation (including plastic responses) “increased [SDM] predictive ability” for a widespread tree, compared to a conventional model ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423001737#:~:text=Incorporating%20intraspecific%20variation%20into%20species,spread%20plant%20species)).  

Mechanistic/trait‐based models offer dramatic illustrations of this effect.  Martín‐Calvo et al. show that in bird invasions, models that account for body size, insulation, and metabolism (and hence thermal tolerance) far outperformed correlative SDMs in predicting which areas would be invaded ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=birds%20introduced%20to%20Europe%2C%20we,escalating%20impacts%20of%20invasive%20species)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L358%20The%20comparatively,models%20are%20more%20readily%20applicable)).  By capturing how birds can buffer temperature fluctuations (a form of plasticity), the mechanistic models accurately mapped “areas at risk”.  In contrast, the correlative models, tied to observed (non‐invaded) niches, mis-predicted many potential invasion sites ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L358%20The%20comparatively,models%20are%20more%20readily%20applicable)).  In other words, plasticity parameterized via physiological traits made the model more **sensitive and accurate**.  

Empirical evidence likewise shows that plasticity can change invasion dynamics.  Thompson and colleagues found that reversible plasticity in seed traits allowed an invasive plant to simultaneously maintain fitness in marginal (dry/wet) habitats and maximize fitness in the range core ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=evidence%20for%20a%20JM%20species,invasiveness%20in%20its%20range%20centre)).  Without that plastic re-allocation, modelled invasion rates were much lower and some regions would not have been colonized at all ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=that%20temporal%20plasticity%20plays%20in,2010)) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=evidence%20for%20a%20JM%20species,invasiveness%20in%20its%20range%20centre)).  In practical terms, risk models for such a species would markedly **under-predict** spread (and thus risk) if they omitted plasticity.  

# Uncertainty and trade‐offs  
In contrast, overestimating plasticity can have the opposite effect: it may **inflate** risk maps, predicting invasiveness in areas where the species cannot truly establish.  Accurate risk assessment thus depends on reliable estimates of plasticity parameters (reaction-norm slopes, tolerance ranges, etc.).  “Plasticity” encompasses multiple traits and environmental responses, so each parameter (e.g. thermal tolerance, moisture-use efficiency) must be measured or inferred.  If data are scarce, plasticity introduces uncertainty.  For example, poorly constrained plasticity could widen confidence intervals of risk predictions.  Sensitivity analyses often show that invasion forecasts vary strongly with assumed plasticity: more plasticity = larger predicted ranges.  

Nevertheless, most studies agree that ignoring realistic plasticity **biases** predictions.  In large reviews, species niche shifts (often due to plasticity or rapid evolution) have been repeatedly linked to SDM failure: when invaders occupy novel climates, native-range models fail “to predict potentially suitable areas” and thus understate risk ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423003992#:~:text=Wood%2C%202014%2C%20Xian%20et%20al,in%20regions%20otherwise%20considered%20unsuitable)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=match%20at%20L358%20The%20comparatively,models%20are%20more%20readily%20applicable)).  By contrast, models incorporating adaptive responses (plastic or genetic) produce estimates of suitable habitat that match observed invasions more closely.  

# Conclusion  
Because phenotypic plasticity effectively expands an invader’s tolerable habitat, its parameterization is crucial for accurate risk forecasting.  Models that include plasticity (via traits, reaction norms or physiological limits) tend to **improve** prediction accuracy, often by enlarging the predicted at-risk area and better matching known invasions ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=evidence%20for%20a%20JM%20species,invasiveness%20in%20its%20range%20centre)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423001737#:~:text=Incorporating%20intraspecific%20variation%20into%20species,spread%20plant%20species)).  Models that omit plasticity can systematically under-predict invasion risk and yield misleadingly narrow forecasts ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=that%20temporal%20plasticity%20plays%20in,2010)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423003992#:~:text=Wood%2C%202014%2C%20Xian%20et%20al,in%20regions%20otherwise%20considered%20unsuitable)).  In practice, invasion‐risk assessments should either use mechanistic or trait‐driven approaches that capture plastic responses, or at least carefully consider plasticity-induced uncertainty.  Properly calibrated plasticity parameters thus sharpen the efficacy of ecological risk models – whereas neglecting them tends to make models less reliable.  

**Key points:** Phenotypic plasticity can greatly broaden an invader’s niche. Ignoring plasticity typically causes underestimation of invasion risk (narrower predicted range) ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=that%20temporal%20plasticity%20plays%20in,2010)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423003992#:~:text=Wood%2C%202014%2C%20Xian%20et%20al,in%20regions%20otherwise%20considered%20unsuitable)). Including realistic plasticity parameters (through trait‐based or mechanistic models) raises sensitivity and overall accuracy of risk maps ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38329-4#:~:text=birds%20introduced%20to%20Europe%2C%20we,escalating%20impacts%20of%20invasive%20species)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423001737#:~:text=Incorporating%20intraspecific%20variation%20into%20species,spread%20plant%20species)). However, plasticity must be well estimated: overstating it can overpredict risk. All else equal, invasive species with high plasticity will have **larger**, more dynamic potential ranges than non-plastic models suggest, so accounting for plasticity is essential for accurate forecasts ([journals.plos.org](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0032323#:~:text=evidence%20for%20a%20JM%20species,invasiveness%20in%20its%20range%20centre)) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30697749/#:~:text=including%20trait%20variation%20over%20geographical,geographical%20gradients%20and%20new%20climates)).
