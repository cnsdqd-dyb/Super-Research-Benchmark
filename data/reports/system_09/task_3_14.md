In a migration‐network Markov model, each region is a state and the weights on edges encode the probability (or rate) of people moving from one region to another.  The long‐run or equilibrium population distribution is simply the chain’s stationary (invariant) distribution – i.e. the (left) eigenvector π of the transition matrix P satisfying π=πP ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjds/s13688-020-00240-z#:~:text=%5C%28P%20%5Cin%20%5Cmathbb%7BR%7D,left%20principal%20eigenvector%20of%20P)).  Crucially, P is determined by the edge weights: changing a weight changes the flow of probability (people) along that link, and hence shifts the stationary distribution. 

Thus **optimizing the edge weights** is essentially how we “tune” the model so that its equilibrium matches reality.  By adjusting weights (subject to whatever constraints or objectives we impose), one can ensure that the Markov chain converges to a desired population distribution.  In fact, recent work on the “inverse” PageRank problem makes this exact point (PageRank is just a Markov equilibrium)!  For example, an algorithm may solve for transition probabilities (derived from edge weights) that make the stationary distribution match a given target vector ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjds/s13688-020-00240-z#:~:text=solving%20for%20the%20reverse%20PageRank,overall%20popularity%20of%20the%20articles)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjds/s13688-020-00240-z#:~:text=In%20this%20work%2C%20we%20take,This)).  In other words, edge‐weight optimization calibrates the migration rates so that, when the chain reaches equilibrium, each region’s share of the total population agrees with the prescribed stationary distribution. 

In summary, edge‐weight optimization is the mechanism by which the network’s flows are balanced to produce the Markov equilibrium.  By fine‐tuning the edge weights (and hence the transition matrix P), one directly shapes the invariant distribution of the chain ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjds/s13688-020-00240-z#:~:text=%5C%28P%20%5Cin%20%5Cmathbb%7BR%7D,left%20principal%20eigenvector%20of%20P)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjds/s13688-020-00240-z#:~:text=solving%20for%20the%20reverse%20PageRank,overall%20popularity%20of%20the%20articles)). This ensures that the modeled regional population shares remain constant over time (in equilibrium) and, if calibrated correctly, match the observed or desired distribution of populations.
