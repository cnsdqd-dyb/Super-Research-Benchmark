The key is to bias the bearing into a fluid‐ or mixed‐lubrication regime and to use materials that “consume” any wear benignly.  In practice this means pairing an extremely hard, smooth surface (e.g. ceramic or a DLC‐coated metal) against a hydrophobic polymer liner (UHMWPE).  The polymer’s low surface energy strongly adsorbs synovial proteins to form a stable boundary film, so the contact runs with a continuous fluid/tribofilm rather than dry sliding ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5090181/#:~:text=rate%20of%20friction%20coefficient%20decreases,This%20result)).  For example, ceramic‐on‐polyethylene interfaces build up much thicker proteinaceous films and run at lower friction than ceramic‐on‐ceramic ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5090181/#:~:text=rate%20of%20friction%20coefficient%20decreases,This%20result)).  DLC or similar coatings on the hard component can also shed a thin carbon-rich transfer layer (“solid lubricant”) that complements the fluid film ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8201056/#:~:text=zirconium,45%2C32)).  In short, the design strategy is to promote film formation (via surface chemistry/coatings and geometry) and thereby avoid direct metal–metal contact.  Any wear that does occur is then mainly on the polymer side, generating ultra-fine polyethylene debris (which is far less osteolytic than metal ions ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3737097/#:~:text=The%20tribological%20performance%20of%20an,Therefore)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8201056/#:~:text=zirconium,45%2C32))).  This deliberate trade‐off – maximizing durable lubricating films while “sacrificing” only benign polymer wear – optimizes both the implant’s longevity and its biocompatibility.
