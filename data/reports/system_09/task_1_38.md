# Balancing Early Diagnosis and False Alarms

Recent Alzheimer’s research and new drugs (like lecanemab and donanemab) mean that identifying the disease as early as possible can improve outcomes. Studies show that treating *mild cognitive impairment* (MCI) or very early Alzheimer’s slows decline: for example, patients on lecanemab were *much more likely to stabilize or even improve* than placebo ([time.com](https://time.com/6328482/alzheimers-lecanemab-early-diagnosis/#:~:text=Eisai%20also%20provided%20more%20detailed,tests%20of%20memory%2C%20orientation%2C%20or)).  Experts note that “the most optimal benefit occurs if people are treated as early as possible” ([time.com](https://time.com/6328482/alzheimers-lecanemab-early-diagnosis/#:~:text=to%20diagnose%20patients%20at%20the,says%20Irizarry)). In practice, this means catching patients *before* substantial brain damage or dementia. A recent analysis found that about one-third of people with MCI progress to full Alzheimer’s dementia within five years, and available treatments can only help *if* used in those early stages ([time.com](https://time.com/6328148/mci-cognitive-decline-under-diagnosed/#:~:text=Alzheimer%27s%20drug%20to%20be%20approved,he%20says)). As one investigator put it: “We have to find these patients early and get them into treatment” ([time.com](https://time.com/6328148/mci-cognitive-decline-under-diagnosed/#:~:text=Alzheimer%27s%20drug%20to%20be%20approved,he%20says)). In short, early diagnosis aims to start therapy while brain function can still be preserved (maintaining independence longer and slowing progression ([time.com](https://time.com/6286157/alzheimers-drug-lecanemab-leqembi-fda/#:~:text=Dr,of%20the%20brain%2C%20called%20amyloid))).

However, unwarranted diagnosis (`false positives`) can do harm.  Broad screening of asymptomatic elders leads to many “positive” results that never cause real disease.  For example, decades of research show that **amyloid plaques alone aren’t destiny** – many healthy older adults harbor abundant amyloid yet remain cognitively normal ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/b31db4ce15caf8b5c9c722b6240b2951#:~:text=Scientists%20don%27t%20know%20exactly%20how,conspirators)).  Even sensitive new blood tests for amyloid or tau can flag people who would never decline.  As one report warned, while amyloid positivity is *necessary* for Alzheimer’s, “not everyone who harbors amyloid plaques necessarily develops the disease” ([time.com](https://time.com/5641602/blood-test-may-be-up-to-94-accurate-in-finding-people-with-alzheimers-brain-changes/#:~:text=Big%20questions%20about%20a%20blood,levels%20would%20be%20a%20start)).  In other words, a solitary positive biomarker test (or a single borderline cognitive score) in someone without symptoms may simply reflect normal aging or a non-Alzheimer’s condition, *not* true Alzheimer’s disease. Mislabeling such people can cause unnecessary anxiety, depression, social stigma and lead to inappropriate treatment and costs.

Given these stakes, clinicians balance benefits and harms by **targeted, stepwise testing** rather than blanket screening. Cognitive assessments (e.g. memory or thinking tests) are used first when there are signs or concerns in a patient, rather than applied to everyone.  Guidelines generally reserve cognitive screening for those with complaints or subtle decline, because unselective testing in a healthy older population would yield mostly false positives ([time.com](https://time.com/36123/families-would-welcome-alzheimers-screening-but-task-force-says-its-too-early/#:~:text=But%20screening%20for%20early%20signs,before%20they%20show%20any%20symptoms)) ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/ad3cffe0bd540accf47e8ee105b439a9#:~:text=The%20tests%20can%20only%20be,don%27t%20yet%20have%20any%20symptoms)).  If a patient *does* show early impairment or MCI on a brief cognitive exam, doctors then use an amyloid biomarker to confirm Alzheimer’s pathology.  For instance, the new FDA-approved blood test for β-amyloid is expressly cleared only for patients aged 55+ *already showing early cognitive symptoms* ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/ad3cffe0bd540accf47e8ee105b439a9#:~:text=The%20tests%20can%20only%20be,don%27t%20yet%20have%20any%20symptoms)).  This two-step approach (screen cognitively → confirm biomarker) maximizes accuracy: a positive cognitive screen with a positive amyloid marker strongly indicates early Alzheimer’s, whereas one test alone would be less reliable. 

Medical experts have even proposed multi-phase strategies to weed out false alarms.  For example, Petersen et al. (2015) suggested having doctors collect baseline risk data (age, family history, vascular factors, minor memory complaints) at age 65, then only if a patient’s status changes – say, a slower walking speed or new memory lapses – advance to genetic/biomarker tests ([time.com](https://time.com/3749130/alzheimers-test-dementia/#:~:text=Petersen%20suggests%20that%20every%20physician,do%20a%20spinal%20tap%2C%20an)).  Only those with both clinical signs *and* positive biomarkers (amyloid) would then be considered to have “MCI due to Alzheimer’s.”  This staged testing avoids expensive or invasive procedures (PET scans, lumbar punctures) in people who are unlikely to truly have Alzheimer’s ([time.com](https://time.com/3749130/alzheimers-test-dementia/#:~:text=Currently%2C%20the%20only%20way%20to,says%20Petersen)).  In practice, specialists are working on refined diagnostic criteria and decision-guidelines so primary‐care doctors can distinguish who really has early AD and who would benefit from therapy ([time.com](https://time.com/6328482/alzheimers-lecanemab-early-diagnosis/#:~:text=if%20people%20are%20treated%20as,to%20make%20it%20easier%20to)) ([time.com](https://time.com/36123/families-would-welcome-alzheimers-screening-but-task-force-says-its-too-early/#:~:text=But%20screening%20for%20early%20signs,before%20they%20show%20any%20symptoms)).

At the same time, the **risks of false positives** are kept in check by the careful use of biomarkers.  For example, a person with memory concerns might get a blood test for amyloid; if it’s negative, Alzheimer’s is deemed unlikely and other causes (e.g. vascular or depression) are considered.  If the blood test is positive, that triggers further evaluation (possibly PET or imaging) and discussion of treatments.  Importantly, labs emphasize that any amyloid test has uncertainty: thresholds for “high” amyloid are still being studied, because interpreting low-level positivity in an asymptomatic person is difficult ([time.com](https://time.com/5641602/blood-test-may-be-up-to-94-accurate-in-finding-people-with-alzheimers-brain-changes/#:~:text=Big%20questions%20about%20a%20blood,levels%20would%20be%20a%20start)).  Doctors must explain that an “amyloid positive” result in an older person simply raises the risk of future decline; it’s not a guaranteed diagnosis by itself.

Finally, the potential harms of treatment underscore the need to avoid false alarms. The anti-amyloid drugs themselves are not benign: clinical trials showed significant side effects.  For example, a panel reviewing lecanemab noted that *some* patients developed brain swelling (amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities, or ARIA) ([time.com](https://time.com/6286157/alzheimers-drug-lecanemab-leqembi-fda/#:~:text=Overall%2C%20the%20panel%20agreed%20that,thinning)).  Likewise, donanemab trials saw cerebral bleeding or microhemorrhages at a higher rate (roughly 2.3% vs 1.9% placebo) because clearing plaques can weaken vessels ([time.com](https://time.com/6987248/alzheimers-drug-donanemab-eli-lilly/#:~:text=The%20biggest%20risk%20associated%20with,those%20receiving%20donanemab%20compared%20to)).  These risks – including rare deaths when combined with blood thinners – are considered acceptable only in patients with true early Alzheimer’s who can potentially benefit ([time.com](https://time.com/6286157/alzheimers-drug-lecanemab-leqembi-fda/#:~:text=Overall%2C%20the%20panel%20agreed%20that,thinning)) ([time.com](https://time.com/6987248/alzheimers-drug-donanemab-eli-lilly/#:~:text=The%20biggest%20risk%20associated%20with,those%20receiving%20donanemab%20compared%20to)).  A false-positive patient on one of these drugs would face risk with no real gain. This reality is why Medicare and experts insist on strict patient selection (confirmatory registry trials, MRI monitoring) to make sure only genuine cases of AD are treated. 

**In summary:**  Early Alzheimer’s diagnosis is pursued because timely therapy can slow the disease and keep patients independent longer ([time.com](https://time.com/6328482/alzheimers-lecanemab-early-diagnosis/#:~:text=Eisai%20also%20provided%20more%20detailed,tests%20of%20memory%2C%20orientation%2C%20or)) ([time.com](https://time.com/6328148/mci-cognitive-decline-under-diagnosed/#:~:text=Alzheimer%27s%20drug%20to%20be%20approved,he%20says)).  But to avoid misdiagnosis, physicians use cognitive tests *in context* and reserve amyloid biomarker tests for those already showing decline ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/ad3cffe0bd540accf47e8ee105b439a9#:~:text=The%20tests%20can%20only%20be,don%27t%20yet%20have%20any%20symptoms)) ([time.com](https://time.com/3749130/alzheimers-test-dementia/#:~:text=Petersen%20suggests%20that%20every%20physician,do%20a%20spinal%20tap%2C%20an)).  By combining tools (clinical exam plus at least one biomarker) and following evidence-based guidelines, they aim to diagnose true early Alzheimer’s while minimizing false positives. This careful approach maximizes patient benefit – catching disease when treatments help – and minimizes harm – sparing healthy individuals from unnecessary anxiety and intervention.

**Key strategies to reduce false positives include:** limiting testing to symptomatic patients, using a stepwise screening (cognitive assessment first, then biomarkers) ([time.com](https://time.com/3749130/alzheimers-test-dementia/#:~:text=Petersen%20suggests%20that%20every%20physician,do%20a%20spinal%20tap%2C%20an)) ([time.com](https://time.com/36123/families-would-welcome-alzheimers-screening-but-task-force-says-its-too-early/#:~:text=But%20screening%20for%20early%20signs,before%20they%20show%20any%20symptoms)), and interpreting results conservatively (e.g. confirming with tau or PET imaging if needed).  In all cases, doctors counsel patients on test limitations.  Clinicians and researchers continue refining the balance so that those who truly have early Alzheimer’s get prompt care, while those without it are not treated needlessly.
