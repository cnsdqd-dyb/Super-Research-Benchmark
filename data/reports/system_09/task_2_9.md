MEMS resonant sensors **can** be engineered to remain extremely stable and accurate over time – but this requires careful design, packaging and calibration.  In practice the raw resonant frequency of a MEMS device does vary with fabrication tolerances (device-to-device “spread”) and drifts under temperature, stress or vibration.  Fortunately, most of this variation is *systematic* (part‐to‐part) and can be calibrated or trimmed out, and modern designs use isolation and compensation to minimize environmental effects.  For example, Q-factor and frequency variations have been shown to be highly repeatable: one study found that the **resonant frequency** of electrostatic MEMS resonators is dominated by systematic (part-to-part) error, with very little random uncertainty ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/9/4/173#:~:text=the%20analysis%20of%20variance%20,for%20a%20small%20electrostatic%20force)).  In other words, once a device is fabricated, its nominal resonant frequency is consistent and can be measured precisely (the same study achieved a “Gage R&R” of only ~0.4% for frequency measurements ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/9/4/173#:~:text=the%20analysis%20of%20variance%20,for%20a%20small%20electrostatic%20force))).  Any fixed offset (due to process deviations) can then be corrected by trimming or calibration (for instance by laser trimming of mass, or by a reference oscillator/PLL and digital offset correction).  In fact, advanced calibration algorithms (e.g. neural-network or linearization techniques) are already used to automatically compensate MEMS sensor variations ([jsss.copernicus.org](https://jsss.copernicus.org/articles/7/245/2018/#:~:text=proposed%20based%20on%20different%20algorithms,for%20the%20reduction%20of%20the)). 

What about **long-term drift and vibration**?  In theory, if left uncompensated a MEMS resonator’s frequency would shift with aging, thermal cycling and external shocks.  However, several recent demonstrations show that with proper design one can achieve *quartz-like* stability.  For instance, Li *et al.* encapsulated a 10–13 MHz silicon disk resonator in a vacuum cavity and observed **±1 ppm** long-term drift (over days), with Allan deviation noise floors of ~26–40 ppb – comparable to AT-cut quartz standards ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0924424718313189#:~:text=Frequency%20stability%20of%203D%20encapsulated,The%20%2013%20between)).  In thermal cycling tests from –40 °C to +85 °C this device’s frequency moved by <1.5 ppm with no hysteresis ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0924424718313189#:~:text=to%20these%20of%20the%20typical,cut%20quartz%20resonator)).  Crucially, when subjected to sinusoidal vibration, its acceleration-sensitivity was **comparable to quartz** (i.e. very low) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0924424718313189#:~:text=to%20these%20of%20the%20typical,cut%20quartz%20resonator)).  In short, a 3D-encapsulated MEMS resonator achieved *stable frequency and accuracy under vibration*, rivaling precision crystal references. 

Even more aggressive compensation schemes exist.  Xu *et al.* demonstrated a silicon MEMS resonator with a two-stage temperature control: first a small oven stabilized the chip, then a nonlinear “amplitude-frequency control” loop (using the Duffing nonlinearity) actively cancelled any remaining drift.  The result was astonishing: **±14 ppb** frequency stability over time ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12460679/#:~:text=compensation%20scheme%20is%20proposed%20with,a%20frequency%20stability%20of%20%C2%B114%E2%80%89ppb)).  In other words, drift was held to a few parts‐in‐10^10.  Such ppb-level performance shows that MEMS can, in principle, achieve the same timing accuracy as the best quartz oscillators. 

In practice, however, one must also guard against vibration‐induced damage or frequency pulling.  Prolonged high-g vibration can induce fatigue or microcracks that slowly change the stiffness of the resonator (and hence its frequency) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024035126#:~:text=drive%20mode%20was%20deduced%20by,the%20result%20of%20modal%20analysis)).  For this reason robust packaging and mechanical design are critical.  Many MEMS inertial sensors use differential or symmetric structures so that uniform vibration is mostly common-mode (ignored) and only true inertial forces are sensed.  Vacuum packaging (or encapsulation) both raises the Q-factor and thermally isolates the element, reducing sensitivity to ambient noise.  As the 3D-encapsulated example above shows, a well-designed resonator can be almost as insensitive to external vibration as an AT-cut quartz. 

**In summary:** with modern techniques it *is* possible for MEMS resonant sensors to maintain excellent long-term frequency stability and accuracy despite process variations and vibration.  The key is to carefully control and compensate those effects – through design (material choices, geometry), packaging (vacuum seals, low-stress adhesives), and electronics (closed-loop PLL/oven controls and digital calibration).  When done right, MEMS resonators can achieve ppm- or even ppb-level stability, on par with crystal standards ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0924424718313189#:~:text=Frequency%20stability%20of%203D%20encapsulated,cut%20quartz%20resonator)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12460679/#:~:text=compensation%20scheme%20is%20proposed%20with,a%20frequency%20stability%20of%20%C2%B114%E2%80%89ppb)), while operating under real-world vibration. 

**References:** Researchers have reported MEMS resonators with *ppm-level stability* and quartz-like vibration tolerance ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0924424718313189#:~:text=Frequency%20stability%20of%203D%20encapsulated,cut%20quartz%20resonator)), and even sub-ppb drift using advanced compensation ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12460679/#:~:text=compensation%20scheme%20is%20proposed%20with,a%20frequency%20stability%20of%20%C2%B114%E2%80%89ppb)).  Device studies confirm that most frequency errors are fixed (part-to-part) and can be calibrated out ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/9/4/173#:~:text=the%20analysis%20of%20variance%20,for%20a%20small%20electrostatic%20force)) ([jsss.copernicus.org](https://jsss.copernicus.org/articles/7/245/2018/#:~:text=proposed%20based%20on%20different%20algorithms,for%20the%20reduction%20of%20the)).
