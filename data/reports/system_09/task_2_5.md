The key to understanding this coupling is that **phase‐transformation hysteresis in SMAs represents the internal energy loss per cycle** and is intimately linked to how much usable work (or heat-pump effect) can be extracted, as well as how much damage the material accumulates.  In a thermomechanical cycle the area of the stress–strain (or temperature–entropy) loop – the hysteresis – is literally the energy dissipated (lost) each cycle ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421115/#:~:text=Hysteresis%20is%20a%20manifestation%20of,transformation%20upon%20heating%20is%20endothermic)).  Thus **a large hysteresis means more heat (or work) is wasted per cycle and lower net conversion efficiency**, whereas a narrow (small) hysteresis loop means more of the latent heat is reversible and the SMA can produce more work for a given heat input ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421115/#:~:text=Hysteresis%20is%20a%20manifestation%20of,transformation%20upon%20heating%20is%20endothermic)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421115/#:~:text=,12)).  

At the same time, hysteresis is tied to microstructural friction and defect creation.  The martensite/austenite interface motion involves frictional and plastic processes (twinning, dislocations, etc.), which appear macroscopically as hysteresis.  High hysteresis implies strong internal friction: each cycle introduces more defects and residual stresses.  This accelerates **functional fatigue** (the loss of recoverable strain) and even **structural fatigue** (crack initiation), shortening the SMA’s usable life.  In contrast, SMAs that exhibit *very low hysteresis* (for example by satisfying special “cofactor” compatibility conditions or through nano/microstructural engineering) undergo almost purely elastic transformations with minimal internal friction.  Such alloys show **remarkably long fatigue lives** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S135964541730397X#:~:text=It%20has%20been%20widely%20shown,such%20as%20the%20migration%20of)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1044580324000172#:~:text=behavior%20%20and%20fatigue%20tests,the%20extreme%20grain%20refinement%20and)).  For instance, Planes et al. report that a specially-designed Ni–Mn–In–Cu SMA with only ~3 K thermal hysteresis retained a **giant 13 K adiabatic temperature change over 10^5 cycles**, with imperceptible drift ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S135964541730397X#:~:text=By%20satisfying%20the%20cofactor%20conditions%2C,These)).  Similarly, Chu et al. showed NiTi micro-pillars processed to have a nanocrystalline structure had an extremely small stress‐hysteresis (~4 MPa) and achieved over 10^6 fatigue cycles under high stress, far exceeding ordinary NiTi behavior ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1044580324000172#:~:text=behavior%20%20and%20fatigue%20tests,the%20extreme%20grain%20refinement%20and)).  In both cases, *small hysteresis* went hand-in-hand with both high conversion (large ∆T or strain output) and ultrahigh cycle life.  

Conversely, if one designs for maximum energy conversion without regard to hysteresis, the fatigue life typically suffers.  For example, a high‐power SMA elastocaloric regenerator ran very efficiently (large temperature span and cooling power), but its fatigue life in tension was reported to be only on the order of 10^3–10^4 cycles ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S135964541830226X#:~:text=temperature%20span%2C%20high%20heating%2Fcooling%20power,cooling%2C%20yet%20on%20the%20other)) – far too low for practical use.  By contrast, NiTi–Cu–Co films with similarly large elastocaloric effect (≈10 K cooling) but intrinsically lower hysteresis have demonstrated endurance beyond 10^7 cycles ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S135964541830226X#:~:text=fatigue%20strain%20limits%20and%20related,and%20adiabatic%20temperature)).  This trade‐off clearly shows the **coupling**: **reducing hysteresis loss not only improves energetic efficiency (less waste heat per cycle) but also slows the degradation that causes fatigue failure**.  

In summary, phase‐transformation hysteresis in SMAs is the microscopic origin of both energy loss and damage under cycling.  Narrow hysteresis loops (i.e. more thermodynamically reversible transformations) yield higher cycle efficiency and dramatically longer fatigue life.  Conversely, wide hysteresis implies more dissipation and faster functional fatigue.  Engineering SMAs for *low hysteresis* (through alloying, precipitate control, texture, etc.) is thus the key strategy to couple long fatigue life with high energy‐conversion efficiency during thermomechanical cycling ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S135964541730397X#:~:text=It%20has%20been%20widely%20shown,such%20as%20the%20migration%20of)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421115/#:~:text=Hysteresis%20is%20a%20manifestation%20of,transformation%20upon%20heating%20is%20endothermic)).
