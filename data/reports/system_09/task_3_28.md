# International cultural frameworks  
International treaties and multilateral bodies set the broad rules protecting culture while enabling creative growth.  Most notably, UNESCO’s cultural conventions explicitly frame heritage as part of development.  For example, the 2003 Convention on Intangible Cultural Heritage declares that living traditions are “a mainspring of cultural diversity and a guarantee of sustainable development,” obliging states to take “safeguarding” measures (documentation, education, transmission, etc.) to ensure the *viability* of those traditions ([ich.unesco.org](https://ich.unesco.org/en/convention#:~:text=Considering%20the%20importance%20of%20the,Table%20of%20Ministers%20of%20Culture)) ([ich.unesco.org](https://ich.unesco.org/en/convention#:~:text=3,education%2C%20as%20well%20as%20the)).  The 2005 Convention on the Diversity of Cultural Expressions similarly recognizes that cultural goods have **both an economic and cultural nature**, mandating policies that protect creators and enable culture-led development ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/creativity/en/articles/unesco-strengthens-protection-cultural-and-creative-industries-digital-environment#:~:text=The%20CCIs%20are%20major%20drivers,governments%20are%20working%20to%20develop)) ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/en/thematicfactsheetculture#:~:text=the%20Diversity%20of%20Cultural%20Expressions,cultural%20sectors%20to%20sustainable%20development)).  UN bodies echo this approach: UNESCO and the World Bank jointly advise cities to build “lasting, enabling environments” for cultural and creative industries that leverage local heritage for economic growth ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-and-world-bank-launch-framework-action-creative-cities-recovery-development-and-economic#:~:text=Guiding%20principles%20and%20recommendations%20are,creative%20industries%20to%20thrive%20in)).  Likewise, the UN 2030 Agenda explicitly links culture to sustainable development (e.g. SDG 11.4 on safeguarding heritage) and even adopted a 2023 resolution to “promote the creative economy for sustainable development” ([unctad.org](https://unctad.org/topic/trade-analysis/creative-economy#:~:text=In%202023%2C%20the%20United%20Nations,creative%20economy%20for%20sustainable%20development)) ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/en/thematicfactsheetculture#:~:text=to%20criteria%20highlighted%20in%20Goal,building%20support%20to%20trade%20negotiators)).  In short, global institutions provide the legal and policy framework – through conventions, resolutions and guidance – that guarantee creative industries must respect heritage while generating value.  

# National laws and policies  
Most countries complement these international commitments with domestic mandates.  Ministries of culture (often coordinating with economic or tourism ministries) craft cultural industries strategies that *embed* heritage preservation.  For instance, Indonesia’s 2017 Cultural Advancement Law explicitly requires the state to “advance the national culture by guaranteeing the freedom of the community in maintaining and developing its cultural values” ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/creativity/en/policy-monitoring-platform/specific-policies-regulate-cultural-and-creative-economy-sector#:~:text=firstly%2C%20the%20advancement%20of%20culture,agenda%20of%20the%20advancement%20of)).  It states that “all cultural heritage shall be maintained within the framework of building a healthy national culture,” characterizing cultural advancement as an integrated process of protecting, developing and utilizing heritage ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/creativity/en/policy-monitoring-platform/specific-policies-regulate-cultural-and-creative-economy-sector#:~:text=between%20the%20agenda%20of%20cultural,the%20advancement%20of%20culture%2C%20and)).  Similarly, many nations enshrine cultural rights in constitutions or laws, set up cultural heritage agencies, designate World Heritage sites, and fund museums or archives – all institutional guarantees that creative enterprises work in concert with heritage conservation.  Even sectoral laws can have such clauses: e.g. film or music laws may require cultural content quotas or royalties.  These domestic legal frameworks ensure long-term creative value chains by mandating that profits flow not just to investors but also support cultural preservation and community interests.  

# Intellectual Property and benefits-sharing mechanisms  
Governments also use intellectual-property (IP) regimes to safeguard heritage and channel economic benefits to creators.  Copyright, trademarks and especially geographical indications (GIs) are often tailored to protect traditional culture.  For example, IP experts note that IP tools “can be leveraged to protect and add value to heritage,” enabling communities to control and profit from traditional knowledge ([gowlingwlg.com](https://gowlingwlg.com/en-ae/insights-resources/articles/2025/cultural-heritage-sustainability-ip-assets#:~:text=In%20recent%20years%2C%20the%20crossover,to%20heritage%20in%20innovative%20ways)) ([gowlingwlg.com](https://gowlingwlg.com/en-ae/insights-resources/articles/2025/cultural-heritage-sustainability-ip-assets#:~:text=,sustainable%20livelihoods%20and%20regional%20development)).  Geographical indication labels – which attach a region’s name to local artisanal products – are a clear case: they link heritage to economic value and “support[] sustainable livelihoods and regional development” ([gowlingwlg.com](https://gowlingwlg.com/en-ae/insights-resources/articles/2025/cultural-heritage-sustainability-ip-assets#:~:text=,sustainable%20livelihoods%20and%20regional%20development)) (think Champagne or Parmigiano-Reggiano).  National laws or sui generis systems also often grant communities collective rights over folklore, handicrafts or local expressions.  By guaranteeing royalties or special IP status (for instance, helping communities copyright or trademark indigenous designs), these legal tools help balance the creative economy: they prevent misappropriation, ensure communities share in the profits, and thus sustain heritage in the value chain ([gowlingwlg.com](https://gowlingwlg.com/en-ae/insights-resources/articles/2025/cultural-heritage-sustainability-ip-assets#:~:text=Understanding%20the%20value%20proposition%20and,why%20heritage%20as%20IP%20matters)) ([gowlingwlg.com](https://gowlingwlg.com/en-ae/insights-resources/articles/2025/cultural-heritage-sustainability-ip-assets#:~:text=,sustainable%20livelihoods%20and%20regional%20development)).  

# Funding and institutional support  
Dedicated public funds and guarantee schemes provide another layer of assurance.  UNESCO’s International Fund for Cultural Diversity (IFCD) is one example: it gives grants to developing countries to “strengthen the economic and social contributions” of their cultural sectors ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/en/thematicfactsheetculture#:~:text=from%20developing%20countries,cultural%20sectors%20to%20sustainable%20development)).  The EU likewise invests heavily – through programs like Creative Europe and Horizon Europe – in projects that **simultaneously** drive cultural tourism, digital innovation and heritage conservation ([research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/research-area/social-sciences-and-humanities/cultural-heritage-and-cultural-and-creative-industries-ccis_en#:~:text=Europe%27s%20cultural%20heritage%20sparks%20creativity%2C,through%20various%C2%A0targeted%C2%A0initiatives%20and%20funding%20programmes)) ([research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/research-area/social-sciences-and-humanities/cultural-heritage-and-cultural-and-creative-industries-ccis_en#:~:text=Boosting%20competitiveness%20and%20a%20fair%2C,the%20cultural%20and%20creative%20industries)).  (An EU factsheet notes that Europe “is committed to safeguarding and enhancing cultural heritage as well as to strengthening…creative industries” using targeted funding ([research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/research-area/social-sciences-and-humanities/cultural-heritage-and-cultural-and-creative-industries-ccis_en#:~:text=Europe%27s%20cultural%20heritage%20sparks%20creativity%2C,through%20various%C2%A0targeted%C2%A0initiatives%20and%20funding%20programmes)).)  Financial instruments also exist: for example, an EU-backed guarantee facility for SMEs in the creative sector helps small art and media companies obtain loans ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/creativity/en/policy-monitoring-platform/cultural-and-creative-sectors-guarantee-facility#:~:text=In%202019%2C%20a%20swedish%20bank,the)), ensuring cultural entrepreneurs survive market fluctuations.  In short, these programs – from UNESCO grants to culture-focused tax incentives and credit schemes – act as institutional guarantees that creative industries can grow economically while reinvesting in cultural heritage.  

# Collaborative networks and city initiatives  
Finally, a range of cooperative institutions and initiatives promote long-term cultural creativity.  UNESCO’s Creative Cities Network and partnerships (e.g. with the World Bank on the *Cities, Culture and Creativity* framework ([www.unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-and-world-bank-launch-framework-action-creative-cities-recovery-development-and-economic#:~:text=Guiding%20principles%20and%20recommendations%20are,creative%20industries%20to%20thrive%20in))) encourage cities to harness local cultural assets for inclusive growth.  Similarly, UNESCO frequently organizes joint programs (such as the “Creative Economy and Intangible Heritage” events ([ich.unesco.org](https://ich.unesco.org/en/activities/creative-economy-and-intangible-cultural-heritage-00663#:~:text=The%20programme%20%E2%80%9CCreative%20Economy%20and,while%20boosting%20local%20creative%20economies))) that bring together communities, designers and policymakers to integrate folk traditions into new art and tourism ventures.  These institutional networks foster “inclusive institutions and regulations” where heritage bearers have a voice in creative development.  By institutionalizing the role of artists, local elders and cultural NGOs in planning – and by monitoring the risks of “decontextualization and over-commercialization” of living heritage ([ich.unesco.org](https://ich.unesco.org/en/news/experts-reflect-on-economic-dimensions-of-intangible-cultural-heritage-safeguarding-13490#:~:text=Given%20the%20relationship%20between%20economic,of%20elements%20of%20living%20heritage)) – these collaborative frameworks help keep creative value chains sustainable and respectful of culture.
