# Mechanisms for satisfaction from expectation–experience gaps

Psychological theories commonly hold that satisfaction (or dissatisfaction) arises from comparing what actually happened to what was expected or planned.  In **expectancy–disconfirmation models**, for example, people mentally compare their *pre-experience expectations* (from planning) to the *post-experience outcome*.  If the outcome **meets or exceeds** expectations, the gap is small or positive and satisfaction is high; if the outcome **falls short**, the gap is negative and satisfaction is low ([grokipedia.com](https://grokipedia.com/page/Customer_satisfaction#:~:text=The%20Expectation,theory%20of%20%2020%20cognitive)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581916301239#:~:text=their%20initial%20expectations,expectations%20results%20in%20lower%20satisfaction)).  In other words, satisfaction is the product of a “disconfirmation” or **comparator mechanism**: the difference between the expected and actual result.  As Oliver (1980) explained, satisfaction emerges from “a cognitive comparison between pre-purchase expectations and post-purchase perceptions” of performance ([grokipedia.com](https://grokipedia.com/page/Customer_satisfaction#:~:text=The%20Expectation,theory%20of%20%2020%20cognitive)). 

Neuroscientifically, this idea maps onto **reward prediction-error** signaling.  Dopamine neurons (and related circuits) encode the difference between expected and received rewards: they signal a positive “prediction error” (phasic burst) if the outcome is better than expected, and a negative error (dip) if worse.  In that way, the brain has an intrinsic **discrepancy detector**: it computes the gap *actual – expected*, and uses that error to update value and generate the feeling of satisfaction.  As Lak et al. (2014) note, “prediction errors represent the difference between predicted and realized outcomes. As such they are an ideal way to learn through everyday experiences” ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3926061/#:~:text=Prediction%20errors%20represent%20the%20difference,based%20%28economic%29%20choices%20between)).  In practice, a larger positive prediction error (outcome > expectation) boosts satisfaction, whereas a negative error (outcome < expectation) reduces it.  Thus both behavioral theory and neural evidence point to an error-comparison mechanism driving satisfaction judgments.

**Contrast vs. Assimilation:**  Cognitive studies also distinguish two ways that expectations affect evaluations. A *contrast* (disconfirmation) process means the satisfaction rating **amplifies the gap** ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581916301239#:~:text=their%20initial%20expectations,expectations%20results%20in%20lower%20satisfaction)) – e.g. you feel extra-satisfied if results exceed hopes, or extra-dissatisfied if they fall short.  In contrast, *assimilation* (a form of cognitive dissonance reduction) means one’s mind may unconsciously adjust expectations or perceptions to reduce the gap ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581916301239#:~:text=Brown%20et%20al,to%20be%20more%20consistent%20with)).  In practice both occur, but the core mechanism remains a comparison: satisfaction is fundamentally a function of the gap between *expected* (planned) and *actual* outcomes ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581916301239#:~:text=their%20initial%20expectations,expectations%20results%20in%20lower%20satisfaction)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3926061/#:~:text=Prediction%20errors%20represent%20the%20difference,based%20%28economic%29%20choices%20between)).

**Primary mechanism:**  In sum, satisfaction judgments appear to be formed by an internal comparator or “prediction‐error” mechanism: the mind (and brain) subtracts the planning‐stage expectation from the experienced outcome and evaluates the sign/magnitude of the discrepancy.  The larger the positive surplus, the greater the satisfaction; the larger the shortfall, the greater the dissatisfaction.  This notion is well captured by disconfirmation theory and reward-prediction-error models ([grokipedia.com](https://grokipedia.com/page/Customer_satisfaction#:~:text=The%20Expectation,adapting%20it%20to%20consumer%20behavior)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3926061/#:~:text=Prediction%20errors%20represent%20the%20difference,based%20%28economic%29%20choices%20between)).  

**Sources:** The expectancy–disconfirmation model of satisfaction (Oliver 1980) and related consumer‐satisfaction theories describe this comparison of expectations versus experience ([grokipedia.com](https://grokipedia.com/page/Customer_satisfaction#:~:text=The%20Expectation,theory%20of%20%2020%20cognitive)).  Experimental neuroscience of reward learning shows that dopamine neurons encode exactly this prediction‐error signal ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3926061/#:~:text=Prediction%20errors%20represent%20the%20difference,based%20%28economic%29%20choices%20between)).  For example, one summary notes: “Contrast theory focuses on the difference between expectations and evaluations. This theory predicts that outcomes above expectations give higher satisfaction, while falling short gives lower satisfaction” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581916301239#:~:text=their%20initial%20expectations,expectations%20results%20in%20lower%20satisfaction)). All these lines of work point to a discrepancy/comparator mechanism as the basis of satisfaction evaluations.
