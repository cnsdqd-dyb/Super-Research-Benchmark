# Fragmentation and Juxtaposition  
Postmodern video collages work by breaking continuous narrative into fragments and reassembling them — much like Derrida’s deconstruction of text.  Artists splice together disparate footage, images or graphics so that meaning emerges from their collision.  In Hirschhorn’s words, even radical pixelated work is still “collages”: **“pasting together at least two existing elements to create something new”** ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/interview-pixel-collage-for-musee-magazine-november-15-2017/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CPixel,giving%20Form%20is%20the%20most)).  This echoes the post-structuralist idea of texts as interwoven quotations: the remix (or video collage) is “a multi-dimensional space” of overlapping references, “a tissue of quotations drawn from the innumerable centres of culture” ([framescinemajournal.com](https://framescinemajournal.com/article/cut-paste-glitch-and-stutter/#:~:text=The%20remix%20fulfils%20the%20promise,structuralists%20as%20our%20guides%2C%20the)).  In effect, digital collage revisits Derrida and Barthes by making narrative **unstable**: each clip or pixel-block can be read against the others, with no single authoritative meaning.  

- **Intertextual Montage:** Postmodern collage often lifts snippets from movies, news and pop culture, layering them nonlinearly.  This foregrounds difference and contradiction (Lyotard’s “incredulity toward metanarratives”), since the viewer must negotiate meaning between clashing images.  The result is anti‐hierarchical – every fragment (every pixelized shape) matters equally – just as deconstruction scatters the center of a text.  

# Pixelation and Visible Grids  
Digital video adds a new tool: the **pixel matrix**.  By foregrounding the image’s tiny units (pixels), the collage literally **deconstructs the medium itself**.  Hirschhorn explains that common uses of pixels (censorship, blur, mosaics) are inherently “authoritarian”: they hide content under the pretext of “protecting” the viewer.  He writes that **“to pixelate is always an authoritarian act”** ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=pixels%20obviously%20always%20comes%20from,Another%20thing)): those black blocks “infantilize or manipulate the viewer.”  Collage artists exploit this by *intentionally* pixelating or gluing cut-outs of pixelated images.  The effect is to **reveal the underlying structure**: the smooth photographic illusion falls apart into a grid.  This makes the medium’s construction visible (the signifier’s materiality), fulfilling deconstruction’s goal of exposing hidden assumptions.  

- **Pixels as Material:** Hirschhorn literally treats pixels as collage material – “magazine cutouts” made of enlarged RGB dots ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=reality,want%20to%20give%20form%20to)).  In his work, “Pixels are a visual bridge between…images of reality” that “make the incommensurable visible” ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=reality,want%20to%20give%20form%20to)).  That is, by juxtaposing hand‐made pixel pieces, he links “the unspeakable with the abstract, the hidden with the known” ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=reality,want%20to%20give%20form%20to)).  In practice, when a collage video suddenly shifts a shot into blocky pixelation, viewers instantly notice the artifice.  The pixel grid shows how the image is built, and any hidden content (what Derrida would call the trace) is suggested rather than fully shown.  This is a direct reconstruction of deconstructionist aesthetics: making the gaps, ambiguities and “non-visible” parts of an image part of the form ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=pixels%20obviously%20always%20comes%20from,Another%20thing)) ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=reality,want%20to%20give%20form%20to)).  

# Glitch and Error as Aesthetic  
Another key technique is **glitching** – deliberately corrupting the video signal or image file so pixels misalign or distort.  Glitch art celebrates the “errors” of digital media, treating them as meaningful fragments.  As one scholar notes, glitch art “recycles digital bits of garbage into high art…and *exposes digital (trace) indices* thereby juxtaposing the analog world with the digital in a beautiful array of spastic pixelation” ([studylib.net](https://studylib.net/doc/18841347/the-glitch-aesthetic---scholarworks-%40-georgia-state-unive...#:~:text=my%20argument%20would%20be%20bland,beautiful%20array%20of%20spastic%20pixelation)).  In other words, the smeared colors, frozen blocks or sweeping pixel sweeps pull back the curtain on the digital apparatus.  A glitchy video frame is literally broken into a new collage of pixels.  

- **Aestheticizing Corruption:**  By using datamoshing, pixel sorting, RGB splits or compression artifacts, the collage amplifies instability.  These techniques make viewers aware that the bright image is only a surface on a coded background.  This is analogous to Derridean tracing: the viewer sees the “play” of differences (here, color and shape glitches) rather than a single, stable scene.  In practice, digital-video collages might overlay glitch loops over documentary footage, or trigger sudden blackout-fragments (freeze-frames).  The upshot is a *visual deconstruction*: the video sequence no longer flows uninterrupted, and meaning must be reassembled from jagged snippets.  As with Hirschhorn’s pixelation, glitch emphasizes that hidden “truth” lies beyond the pixel mask – inviting the audience to engage critically and question the image ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=pixels%20obviously%20always%20comes%20from,Another%20thing)) ([studylib.net](https://studylib.net/doc/18841347/the-glitch-aesthetic---scholarworks-%40-georgia-state-unive...#:~:text=my%20argument%20would%20be%20bland,beautiful%20array%20of%20spastic%20pixelation)).  

# Political Semblance and Truth  
Finally, postmodern digital collage often yields a pointed commentary.  By re-contextualizing media imagery at the pixel level, artists question authority and reality.  Hirschhorn explicitly links pixelation to today’s “post-truth” politics: he observes that pixel-blocks on news images have become symbols of state control.  Yet he also sees a paradox: **“through pixels, abstraction can engage me in today’s world, time and reality”** and point “beyond information” toward truth ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=which%20interests%20me%20is%20the,desire%20of%20truth%2C%20truth%20as)).  In other words, while pixelation is a tool of censorship (“propaganda” ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=pixels%20obviously%20always%20comes%20from,Another%20thing))), in collage it is flipped into an *invitation* to seek meaning.  The viewer must confront both what is shown and what is hidden.  

Thus, postmodern digital collages “reconstruct” deconstructionist aesthetics by taking its ideas – fragmentation, non-hierarchical signifiers, the instability of meaning – and embodying them in pixel form.  The pixel matrix becomes the new canvas: artists cut, paste and glitch this grid in the editing software so that every frame visibly carries its own breakdown.  In doing so, such videos enact Barthes/Derrida’s notion that texts (or images) are built from many interlocking parts with no final center ([framescinemajournal.com](https://framescinemajournal.com/article/cut-paste-glitch-and-stutter/#:~:text=The%20remix%20fulfils%20the%20promise,structuralists%20as%20our%20guides%2C%20the)).  The discrete pixels and their deliberate corruption force us to read the image *against* itself.  Viewers see not a smooth reality, but a handcrafted patchwork where “the unspeakable” meets “the abstract” ([www.thomashirschhorn.com](https://www.thomashirschhorn.com/pixel-collage/#:~:text=reality,want%20to%20give%20form%20to)) – exactly the kind of de-centered, self-reflexive aesthetic deconstruction called for.
