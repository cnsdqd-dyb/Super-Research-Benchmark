In practice, TNM for liver cancer effectively **trades off immediacy against absolute certainty**.  Clinicians stage the *tumor burden* (the “T” component) primarily by imaging (CT or MRI) so that treatment can start without delay, and they defer definitive nodal staging (the “N” component) to the pathology after resection.  In other words, **imaging‐based clinical staging** is used to quickly quantify tumor size/number, while true lymph‐node involvement is only confirmed histologically.  This approach is a built‐in “compromise” in TNM staging – it uses the high specificity of imaging (especially CT) for nodal disease to guide therapy on time, accepting that only surgical/pathologic exam can give 100% certainty.  For example, modern CT staging has very high specificity for identifying lymph node metastases ([journals.lww.com](https://journals.lww.com/10.1002/hep4.1774#:~:text=Sensitivities%20of%20CT%20and%20MRI,respectively%2C%20P%C2%A0%3D%C2%A00.001%29%20reference%20standard)), and is fast to perform, whereas more sensitive tests (like PET) would delay treatment.  Hence, by relying on preoperative imaging (with its high specificity) and treating based on tumor burden, TNM staging balances diagnostic accuracy with timely intervention ([journals.lww.com](https://journals.lww.com/10.1002/hep4.1774#:~:text=Sensitivities%20of%20CT%20and%20MRI,respectively%2C%20P%C2%A0%3D%C2%A00.001%29%20reference%20standard)) ([www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK254197/#:~:text=,low%20for%20sensitivity%20and%20specificity)).
