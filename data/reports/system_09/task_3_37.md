**Minimal throughput change, more variability.** In practice, green‐wave offsets only help when vehicle arrivals come in pulses.  If arrivals are purely random (uniform or Poisson), there are no platoons for the offset to “catch.”  In that case the average cycle capacity (≈ saturation‐flow·green-time) is unchanged by offsetting – you don’t get more green time overall, just shifted in time.  What does change is how the vehicles that do arrive are distributed across cycles.  Some green intervals may be partially empty (if arrivals missed them) while others may clear a few extra cars by chance.  In other words, offsetting under random arrivals tends to **widen** the distribution of vehicles served per cycle – occasional very low‐flow cycles when no cars arrive in the green wave, and occasional higher‐flow cycles if a few happen to cluster – but the *expected* capacity remains about the same.  

Traffic‐engineering sources note exactly this: a “green‐wave” or coordinated timing assumes **impulse-type (platoon) arrivals**, not uniform randomness.  Indeed, coordination is justified only if upstream signals or incoming flows create pulses of vehicles ([www.scirp.org](https://www.scirp.org/html/49805.html#:~:text=factors%20,is%20more%20conducive%20to%20the)).  With truly random (uniform) arrivals, coordination “may provide little benefit” in throughput ([ops.fhwa.dot.gov](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop08024/chapter6.htm#:~:text=identified%20through%20observation%20of%20traffic,simple%20and%20complex%20procedures%20worked)).  The FHWA signal timing manual emphasizes that the *bandwidth* created by an offset is *independent of the actual traffic flow* ([ops.fhwa.dot.gov](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop08024/chapter6.htm#:~:text=is%20a%20parameter%20that%20is,to%20understand%20related%20to%20bandwidth)) – it merely guarantees a window through which a block of vehicles *could* pass, but if few or no vehicles arrive in that window, the green time goes partly unused.  Thus under random arrivals a green-wave offset doesn’t boost the long‐term capacity; it only alters *when* cycles clear vehicles.  The capacity “distribution” (e.g. the pattern of counts per cycle or link) remains governed by the green splits and flow randomness, not by the offset.  In short, phase offsets under random arrivals do not raise average intersection capacity – they only shuffle which cycles carry more flow, increasing variability but leaving the overall capacity essentially unchanged ([ops.fhwa.dot.gov](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop08024/chapter6.htm#:~:text=identified%20through%20observation%20of%20traffic,simple%20and%20complex%20procedures%20worked)) ([ops.fhwa.dot.gov](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop08024/chapter6.htm#:~:text=is%20a%20parameter%20that%20is,to%20understand%20related%20to%20bandwidth)). 

**References:** Coordination literature consistently stresses that green‐wave gains require platooned arrivals ([www.scirp.org](https://www.scirp.org/html/49805.html#:~:text=factors%20,is%20more%20conducive%20to%20the)).  If arrivals are uncorrelated with the offset (i.e. random), then the theoretical progression “bandwidth” often goes unused, yielding negligible throughput gain ([ops.fhwa.dot.gov](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop08024/chapter6.htm#:~:text=identified%20through%20observation%20of%20traffic,simple%20and%20complex%20procedures%20worked)) ([ops.fhwa.dot.gov](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop08024/chapter6.htm#:~:text=is%20a%20parameter%20that%20is,to%20understand%20related%20to%20bandwidth)).
