# Community‐defined authenticity versus tourist accommodation

Folk rituals are living traditions, so the “line” between preserving their authentic form and adapting for outsiders is set by the cultural custodians themselves – typically the community or practitioners who own the tradition.  International heritage guidelines explicitly uphold this principle.  For example, UNESCO’s 2003 Convention on Intangible Cultural Heritage directs that tourism activities **must respect the safeguarding of ICH and the wishes and needs of host communities** ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=The%20UNESCO%202003%20Convention%20for,the%20adoption%20of%20legal%2C%20technical)).  In other words, the local believers or artists decide what can be shared with visitors and what must remain unchanged.  UNESCO even speaks of enabling communities “to represent their ICH on their own terms… selectively providing access when appropriate” ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=This%20toolkit%20aims%20to%20help,selectively%20providing%20access%20when%20appropriate)).  Institutional frameworks thus reinforce that *insider consensus* – not tourist demand – draws the boundary between a genuine ritual and a visitor‐friendly version.

In practice, communities often mark this boundary by creating two “layers” of an event: one curated for public viewing and one reserved as the true ritual.  For instance, Balinese temple dances are a classic case.  Performers have long given condensed, stylized versions of sacred dances to outsiders (with simplified choreography and language) so that tourists can enjoy the spectacle ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=Some%20Balinese%20dances%20performed%20for,as%20well%20as%20financial%20benefits)).  Crucially, the authentic ceremony is kept separate or “out of sight” of the crowd.  The proceeds of the public show are then reinvested in the traditional practice ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=The%20revenues%20are%20invested%20back,26)).  This doublesided approach safeguards ritual integrity (the true rite remains unchanged) while providing a comfortable, engaging performance for visitors. 

- **Local control and norms.**  The community’s own taboos, beliefs and leaders define what is off-limits. Elders or priests may forbid tourists from touching certain objects, entering sacred spaces, or hearing esoteric chants, whereas they might allow a re-interpretation for learning or entertainment.  
- **Heritage guidelines.** International frameworks (like UNESCO’s ICH convention) and ethical tourism codes explicitly say tourism must **heed community wishes** ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=The%20UNESCO%202003%20Convention%20for,the%20adoption%20of%20legal%2C%20technical)).  This means practitioners themselves negotiate or set rules for visitor participation.  
- **Curated “tourist performances.”** Many folk events now have a designated public show or festival segment.  These are arranged so outsiders can engage (e.g. wearing a costume, joining a dance, seeing a ritual sequence) without disrupting the core rite. Such staging often involves abridging or explaining the ritual in simpler terms.  

Ultimately, the boundary is cultural and negotiated – not a physical fence.  It is **established by the tradition‐bearers (often supported by heritage policy)**, who decide how much to adapt rituals for hospitality without diluting their meaning ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=The%20UNESCO%202003%20Convention%20for,the%20adoption%20of%20legal%2C%20technical)) ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=This%20toolkit%20aims%20to%20help,selectively%20providing%20access%20when%20appropriate)).  In short, respect for ritual integrity hinges on *who holds the authority* over the practice. If the hosts (elders, shrine caretakers, folk artists) permit tourists only a sanitized slice of the event, that line – informed by local values and safeguarding rules – protects the authentic rite while allowing visitors a comfortable experience ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=Some%20Balinese%20dances%20performed%20for,as%20well%20as%20financial%20benefits)) ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=The%20revenues%20are%20invested%20back,26)). 

**Sources:** UNESCO guidelines stress that tourism must align with the **wishes of host communities** in safeguarding intangible heritage ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=The%20UNESCO%202003%20Convention%20for,the%20adoption%20of%20legal%2C%20technical)) ([www.ichngoforum.org](https://www.ichngoforum.org/web-dossier-on-intangible-cultural-heritage-and-sustainable-tourism/#:~:text=This%20toolkit%20aims%20to%20help,selectively%20providing%20access%20when%20appropriate)). These practices – underpinned by community norms and heritage principles – draw the line between authentic ritual and visitor-friendly performance.
