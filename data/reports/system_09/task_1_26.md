**Finite-difference vs. semi-analytical methods.**  The Heston model admits a semi-closed (Fourier/characteristic-function) solution for European options ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/1011.6532.html#:~:text=1,not%20matched%20with%20items%20on)). In practice, Fourier‐transform/FFT or Monte Carlo methods are often faster and more accurate for plain-vanilla options than grid solvers.  Finite‐difference (FD) PDE methods are typically used for path-dependent or early‐exercise cases, but they require discretizing both asset price \(S\) and volatility \(v\) plus time.  That means choosing a 2D spatial grid and a time mesh, which introduces a trade-off: **finer meshes reduce discretization error but increase computation time** (operations grow roughly as \(O(N_S N_v N_t)\)).  Implicit or ADI schemes can use larger time steps without instability, but spatial truncation and time-stepping errors still accumulate, especially over long maturities.  

## Convergence speed vs. accuracy.  Higher-order and adaptive schemes can dramatically improve accuracy-per-node.  For example, Chiarella *et al.* (2017) demonstrate a 4th-order in space FD scheme with **local mesh refinement**, focusing nodes at payoff “kinks” (e.g. at the strike) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=following%20advantages%20over%20classical%20spatial,discretisation%20as%20it)).  By adding just a few extra points near singularities, they restore high-order convergence: in one test the error in a European call dropped from ~\(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-7}\) by introducing only 10 extra nodes around the strike ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=match%20at%20L1068%20jump%E2%80%93diffusion%20model,order)).  Similarly, non-uniform grids (e.g. the Bowen–Smith mesh) allow concentration of grid points where the payoff or solution has steep gradients, while using a coarser grid elsewhere ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20propose,classical%20%2051%20as%20it)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=helps%20to%20reduce%20the%20number,the%20use%20of%20complex%20artificial)).  This means you need *far fewer total nodes* to achieve a given accuracy compared to a uniform grid.  In practice this yields both faster solves and smaller errors – for instance, non-uniform FD grids have been shown to “significantly improve efficiency and practicality” by letting far-field boundaries be coarse without complex boundary conditions ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=helps%20to%20reduce%20the%20number,the%20use%20of%20complex%20artificial)). 

- *High-order discretizations.*  High-order FD schemes or spectral methods can attain very low error for a given mesh. For Heston’s PDE these require careful treatment of the mixed derivative, but have been shown to be unconditionally stable and highly accurate ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0377042714002155#:~:text=We%20derive%20high,unconditional%20stability%20of%20the%20scheme)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=helps%20to%20reduce%20the%20number,the%20use%20of%20complex%20artificial)).  Coupled with Rannacher time-stepping or analytic integration of the time operator, one can often use fewer time steps without loss of accuracy.  In short, modern schemes can sometimes “balance” speed and accuracy through algorithmic cleverness, but there is no free lunch: high-order methods often have larger stencils or dense solves, so the implementation can be complex.  

- *Mesh refinement trade-offs.*  Any concentration of grid points in one region necessarily means sparser coverage elsewhere (for fixed total nodes).  As one study notes, “the larger the zoom [around the strike], the smaller the error around K, but on the other hand the error in other parts of the domain increases… There has to be a balance between the error in the area around K and the error in other parts” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0377042714002155#:~:text=match%20at%20L812%20computations,of%20grid%20points%20in%20other)).  In other words, focusing resolution on-the-money levels reduces local error but can inflate error in far-out-of-the-money or high-volatility regions.  This is especially important for **long-dated options**, where the tail of the distribution becomes more important.  If the grid does not extend far enough in \(S\) or \(v\), or is too coarse, pricing errors may grow over a long horizon.  

## Long-maturity and grid errors.  For options with long time-to-maturity, discretization errors can compound.  A coarse time grid forces large \(\Delta t\), which may smear out the solution (even if stable) and reduce accuracy.  Likewise, long maturities often mean the underlying distribution can move farther in \(S\) and \(v\)-space, demanding a larger domain or finer meshes to capture outlier scenarios.  Empirically, many authors report that fine grids are needed to “capture the long-term dynamics” of stochastic volatility – otherwise significant bias appears.  For example, using a too-small or too-coarse grid can underestimate the option’s value, because the effective volatility has more time to vary under Heston.  In practice, one typically checks convergence by refining the grid: if moving from, say, 200×100 to 400×200 grid points materially changes the price for a long-dated option, the coarser grid was insufficient.  

In summary, **the grid density critically affects accuracy**.  A well-designed non-uniform mesh or high-order scheme can achieve both speed and precision ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=In%20this%20paper%2C%20we%20propose,classical%20%2051%20as%20it)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S089812211730295X#:~:text=helps%20to%20reduce%20the%20number,the%20use%20of%20complex%20artificial)), but naive choices incur errors.  If one simply coarsens the FD grid to speed up computation, option prices (especially for long maturities or far strikes) can be significantly off.  As noted in one Heston-PDE study, non-uniform grids “do of course perform better in general,” but improper grid spacing can yield unpredictable errors – sometimes even giving worse results than a simple uniform grid ([www.acenumerics.com](https://www.acenumerics.com/miscellaneous/old-fashioned-heston-pricer-vs-the-state-of-the-art#:~:text=QLib%20by%20default%20focuses%20the,uniform%20grids%20need%20more)). 

**Bottom line:**  Careful grid design and advanced numerical techniques can mitigate the speed–accuracy trade-off, but finite-difference pricing always involves a compromise.  For risk-neutral European options, practitioners often prefer the FFT/characteristic-function method for its high accuracy and speed ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/1011.6532.html#:~:text=1,not%20matched%20with%20items%20on)).  If using FD, one should use sufficiently fine (or adaptive) grids and convergence tests – otherwise long-dated option prices may be biased by discretization error.
