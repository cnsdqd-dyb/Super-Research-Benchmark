**City scale (local vs. regional/global):**  Empirical studies show that mobility-distance distributions are not invariant across scales.  At national or multi-city scales (e.g. the U.S. bank-note data or combined multi-city cellphone/GPS datasets), displacements follow a Lévy-flight–like (truncated power-law) form with a heavy tail exponent on the order of 1–1.5 ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362592/#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20confirm%20the,datasets%2C%20we%20note%20that%20the)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5375979/#:~:text=Supplementary%20Table%20S1%20%29,21%29%2C%20and%20the)).  For example, Brockmann et al. and González et al. found exponents ≈1.5–1.8 in large-scale human travel data, and a recent global Foursquare study finds an overall travel-displacement exponent ∼1.5 ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362592/#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20confirm%20the,datasets%2C%20we%20note%20that%20the)).  In contrast, *within a single city* the distribution is dominated by short trips and cuts off sharply at distances comparable to the city’s size. Noulas et al. show that intra-city jump PDF is nearly flat at very short ranges and then decays abruptly beyond ≈10–20 km, so a pure power law is a poor fit ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362592/#:~:text=of%20intracity%20displacements%20can%20not,not%20captured%20by%20such%20process)).  In practice, fitted exponents in urban OD networks tend to be larger (steeper) than the global value.  For instance, Saberi et al. report truncated power-law fits to Chicago and Melbourne trip lengths with exponents b≈1.03 and 1.29, respectively ([paperzz.com](https://paperzz.com/doc/8466056/a-complex-network-perspective-for-characterizing-urban-tr...#:~:text=P%C3%B0l%C3%9E%20%C2%BC%20%C3%B0l%20%C3%BE%20l0,exponents%20observed%20in%20earlier%20studies)).  (Chicago’s smaller effective exponent means a heavier tail.)  In general, as one aggregates over larger geographic scales, very long trips become possible and the empirical tail exponent drops toward ~1–1.5.  In small or constrained networks (small city, no long links), the tail effectively steepens (exponent grows) and often shows an exponential cutoff once city-sized limits are reached ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362592/#:~:text=of%20intracity%20displacements%20can%20not,not%20captured%20by%20such%20process)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362592/#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20confirm%20the,datasets%2C%20we%20note%20that%20the)).  (Zhao et al. similarly report that intra-city GPS flights in Beijing/Geneva fit truncated power laws with α≈1.57 and 1.39 ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5375979/#:~:text=Supplementary%20Table%20S1%20%29,21%29%2C%20and%20the)), whereas the same data truncated within one city would have an even larger effective slope.)  

**Network topology:**  The connectivity and geometry of the transport network strongly modulate the power-law tail.  In very regular, lattice-like networks (e.g. idealized Manhattan grids), simulated traffic or trip distributions show no clear heavy tail, whereas irregular or “random” street layouts produce robust power laws ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42001-018-0028-7#:~:text=behavior%20appeared%20robustly%20with%20or,network%2C%20unlike%20the%20grid%20network)).  For example, Mori and Hasegawa simulated traffic on Kobe’s organic road network and found a power-law segment-flow distribution (exponent ≈–1.1), but on a Manhattan grid the distribution lacked a power-law regime ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42001-018-0028-7#:~:text=behavior%20appeared%20robustly%20with%20or,network%2C%20unlike%20the%20grid%20network)).  In real cities, Zhao et al. found that Beijing’s road segment lengths follow a very steep power law (exponent ≈3.4), much larger than the ~1.4–1.6 found for actual human trip lengths ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5375979/#:~:text=distribution,be%20much%20larger%20than%20those)).  In other words, a simple tree- or grid-like road network strongly constrains travel (producing a short-tailed, steep exponent), whereas adding long-range links (express highways, subways, etc.) allows many more long trips and lightens the tail.  In practice, cities with more hierarchical or *scale-free* connectivity (long arterials, ring roads) tend to exhibit heavier-tailed travel distributions.  Thus, *as network topology becomes more connected or heterogeneous, the fitted power-law exponent for trip lengths tends to decrease (heavier tail)*; conversely, in sparse or regular networks the exponent grows (steeper drop-off) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42001-018-0028-7#:~:text=behavior%20appeared%20robustly%20with%20or,network%2C%20unlike%20the%20grid%20network)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5375979/#:~:text=distribution,be%20much%20larger%20than%20those)).

**In summary:**  Human trip-length distributions are scale–dependent and topology–dependent.  At large (interurban/national) scales one often sees a robust Lévy-like tail (exponent ~1–1.5) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362592/#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20confirm%20the,datasets%2C%20we%20note%20that%20the)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5375979/#:~:text=Supplementary%20Table%20S1%20%29,21%29%2C%20and%20the)).  At small (intra-urban) scales, most travel is short-range and the effective exponent is much larger, with a clear cut-off at city-scale distances ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3362592/#:~:text=of%20intracity%20displacements%20can%20not,not%20captured%20by%20such%20process)).  Network structure modulates these effects: random/organic networks induce heavier tails (lower exponents) while uniform/grid networks yield shallower tails or cutoffs ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42001-018-0028-7#:~:text=behavior%20appeared%20robustly%20with%20or,network%2C%20unlike%20the%20grid%20network)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5375979/#:~:text=distribution,be%20much%20larger%20than%20those)).
