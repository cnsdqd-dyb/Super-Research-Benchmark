**Cognitive accessibility** refers to our mind’s ability to conceive or simulate alternative scenarios.  Human beings routinely imagine how things “could have been different” – we judge that things could have been otherwise than they actually are ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2017/entries/modality-epistemology/#:~:text=impossible,we%20are%20motivated%20to%20make)).  In modal epistemology, conceiving a scenario is often taken to suggest it’s *possible*, though philosophers caution that mere imaginability doesn’t prove reality (one might conceive impossible scenarios due to ignorance) ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2017/entries/modality-epistemology/#:~:text=conceive%20of%20a%20situation%20in,in%20which%20water%20does%20not)).  In any case, our cognitive toolkit allows us to form the concept of **parallel worlds** or “what-if” alternatives.  This is evidenced by fiction, thought experiments and even modal logic: e.g. in discussing quantum mechanics one often talks of many parallel outcomes.  

**Determinism** traditionally holds that, given a complete state of the world at some time *t*, the laws of nature *rigidly fix* what happens at all future times.  Roughly: *“the way things go thereafter is fixed as a matter of natural law”* once the state at *t* is specified ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/determinism-causal/#:~:text=,a%20matter%20of%20natural%20law)).  In a classic deterministic worldview (e.g. Newtonian physics or Laplace’s demon thought-experiment), perfect knowledge of the present would allow perfect prediction of the future.  Deterministic *knowledge* thus presumes a single, fixed future given present conditions.

The idea of **parallel worlds** complicates this picture.  In philosophy and physics one influential model is the Everettian Many-Worlds Interpretation (MWI) of quantum mechanics.  MWI posits that when a quantum event with multiple possible outcomes occurs, *all* outcomes actually happen, but in different “branches” of reality.  As one popular account puts it, there are “myriads of worlds” in which *every* possible measurement outcome is realized ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=The%20Many,The%20MWI%20provides%20a)).  Crucially, in MWI the underlying physics remains fully deterministic: the universal wave-function evolves by the Schrödinger equation without “collapse” ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=state,be%20indeterministic%20for%20human%20observers)).  In the words of one recent exposition, **“the MWI is a deterministic theory for a physical Universe”**, with apparent randomness for observers arising only because each observer becomes correlated with one branch ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=state,be%20indeterministic%20for%20human%20observers)).  In other words, the entire branching structure of the multiverse is *uniquely determined* by the past state of the universe ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02314-y#:~:text=of%20nature%20are%20deterministic,will%20occur%20in%20some%20branch)).  Every branch’s state follows deterministically, even though from the point of view of an embedded observer the outcome seems random (since you only experience one branch).  Thus, from a physical perspective, “nature need not play dice” – determinism is preserved, but at the cost of proliferating worlds.

**Epistemological implications:**  If we grant that parallel/compossible worlds are cognitively imaginable or even physically real (as in MWI), what does this do to knowledge founded on determinism?  On one hand, the mere *conceivability* of multiple outcomes doesn’t automatically refute a deterministic rule of nature – indeed, MWI shows they can coexist.  Deterministic *laws* can still hold (the whole system evolves predictably), but our **knowledge** is about one branch.  In practice, because any measurement leads to branching, an observer cannot know in advance which outcome *they* will observe; all they can do is assign probabilities (or “branch weights”) to different outcomes.  This means that even if the world is deterministic in principle, predictions for an individual (ignorant of their future branch) remain probabilistic or uncertain.  The “foundation” of determinism – that the present fixes the future – is thus epistemically weakened: one cannot determine *which* future branch will be realized in one’s experience without additional information. 

On the other hand, nothing logically forces us to accept that parallel worlds physically exist just because we can conceive them.  Cognitive accessibility of an idea (like many worlds) can prompt us to question assumptions, but it doesn’t itself constitute empirical disproof.  Philosophers note that imaging alternative worlds is natural, but it must be checked against actual evidence or deeper theory ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2017/entries/modality-epistemology/#:~:text=impossible,we%20are%20motivated%20to%20make)) ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2017/entries/modality-epistemology/#:~:text=conceive%20of%20a%20situation%20in,in%20which%20water%20does%20not)).  If, for example, the world is fundamentally single-world classical physics, then talking about “parallel worlds” is just a convenient way to think about hypothetical outcomes, not a statement of reality.  In that case our epistemological foundation for determinism (grounded in physical law and observation) remains intact despite our imaginative freedom.

**Conclusion:**  In sum, being able to **think** about parallel worlds highlights that our knowledge of future events can never be absolutely certain in practice – since we may cognitively entertain multiple outcomes – but it does *not* in itself overthrow the idea that nature follows deterministic laws.  Many-worlds physics shows that one can fully retain determinism even in a multiverse scenario ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=state,be%20indeterministic%20for%20human%20observers)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02314-y#:~:text=of%20nature%20are%20deterministic,will%20occur%20in%20some%20branch)).  What changes is the interpretation of knowledge claims: rather than saying “future *will* be X (period) given present conditions”, we must say “future branches will include X (and Y, etc.), and this observer will find themselves in one branch”.  Thus cognitive accessibility of alternate worlds invites us to refine the epistemology of deterministic theories – distinguishing what the *laws* fix from what a single observer *knows* or experiences.  It challenges naive ideas of a uniquely knowable future, but it need not negate determinism itself; it mainly underscores that knowledge is perspectival (branch-specific) in a multiverse view ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=state,be%20indeterministic%20for%20human%20observers)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02314-y#:~:text=of%20nature%20are%20deterministic,will%20occur%20in%20some%20branch)).

**Key references:** The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Many-Worlds notes that under Everett’s picture *“the MWI is a deterministic theory”* and that each world’s future branches into many future worlds ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=state,be%20indeterministic%20for%20human%20observers)).  It also defines a “world” as a maximal set of objects in definite classical states, highlighting that our usual notion of a single future is replaced by many ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=The%20Many,The%20MWI%20provides%20a)) ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/#:~:text=The%20concept%20of%20a%20world,a%20time%20in%20the%20future)).  Philosophers of modality similarly observe that humans instinctively imagine alternate possible scenarios ([plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2017/entries/modality-epistemology/#:~:text=impossible,we%20are%20motivated%20to%20make)) – but caution that this imaginability does not by itself settle what is metaphysically determined.  Together, these sources suggest that conceiving parallel worlds refines but does not destroy the epistemological basis of determinism: the laws may remain fixed, even as our knowledge (anchored in one branch) becomes necessarily limited.
