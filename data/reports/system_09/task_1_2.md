In real HEAs the “random” FCC lattice actually contains significant short- and medium-range order (element clustering).  This local chemical ordering creates a highly non-uniform energy landscape: stacking‐fault (and twin‐fault) energies fluctuate strongly from place to place.  Atomistic simulations show that LCO **“roughens”** the glide plane energy – raising activation barriers and lattice friction for dislocation glide ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=principal,In%20contrast)).  In practice this means dislocations move in a more jerky, wavy fashion rather than straight: some segments cut through low-resistance regions easily, while others are pinned by high‐order clusters.  As a result, when a dislocation approaches a Σ3 twin boundary it cannot constrict uniformly; **different parts of the line arrive under different stresses**.  Molecular‐dynamics work on a CrMnFeCoNi HEA confirmed that screw dislocations become “wave-shaped” due to local chemistry fluctuations, preventing fully synchronous constriction at the twin plane ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927025622003652#:~:text=Different%20from%20the%20pure%20Ni%2C,dislocation%20at%20the%20twin%20boundary)).  In other words, the usual “cut-through” or “absorb” modes at a twin boundary are altered: the pinned segments delay or hinder the transmission of the dislocation across the twin boundary, effectively increasing the required stress for twin penetration.  

- **Roughened slip paths.**  LCO causes large spatial variations in stacking‐fault (SF) energy.  Each partial dislocation must break local order to glide, so the unstable SF energy contains a substantial chemical component ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=significant%20fraction%20of%20SFE%20is,the%20RSS%20extreme%20shows%20a)).  Practically, dislocations become **segmented**: they advance via nanoscale “detrapping” as each segment escapes a pinning site ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=principal,In%20contrast)).  The net effect is higher intrinsic friction — dislocations require more stress to move and take a serpentine path.  

- **Asynchronous twin‐boundary interaction.**  A wavy dislocation line striking a twin plane cannot reach the boundary all at once.  Some portions intersect regions of low LCO and bow out first, while other portions in strongly ordered domains lag.  This desynchronized interaction means the twin‐boundary constriction occurs in steps, not in one smooth motion ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927025622003652#:~:text=Different%20from%20the%20pure%20Ni%2C,dislocation%20at%20the%20twin%20boundary)).  In contrast to homogeneous alloys (where a screw dislocation might synchronously cross the twin or be absorbed in one event), the HEA’s LCO enforces a more complex, stress‐enhanced mechanism.  

- **Elevated and non-uniform twin boundary energy.**  Because each new twin layer must disrupt a different local composition, the twin boundary energy (γ_twin) in an HEA is not constant.  Li *et al.* (2019) showed that for FCC HEAs the effective twin-boundary energy **grows with twin thickness**, roughly linearly, instead of saturating as it does in pure metals ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=However%2C%20samples%20with%20LCOs%20show,as%20the%20thickening%20always%20incurs)).  In practical terms, every extra atomic layer of twin incurs an extra energy penalty to break up the underlying chemical order.  This “unconventional” twin energy makes twin nucleation and growth harder: twins stay very narrow, and twin‐thickening requires additional stress.  

- **Combined effect on dislocation–twin coupling.**  The roughened slip and raised twin‐plane energies work together to strengthen the HEA.  Dislocations are more strongly pinned by LCO and cannot easily shear through or thicken a twin.  Experimentally and by simulation, it is observed that LCO therefore *raises* the stress for twin formation and motion, and alters the mode of slip-twin interaction ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=principal,In%20contrast)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927025622003652#:~:text=Different%20from%20the%20pure%20Ni%2C,dislocation%20at%20the%20twin%20boundary)) ([www.nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11464-7#:~:text=However%2C%20samples%20with%20LCOs%20show,as%20the%20thickening%20always%20incurs)).  In summary, local chemical order in HEAs adds a solute-like strengthening to both ordinary slip and to twinning: dislocations encounter higher barriers and twin boundaries become effectively “harder,” changing the plastic deformation mechanism accordingly.

These effects explain why LCO raises the critical stress for twin‐mediated plasticity in HEAs.
