Enzyme induction is inherently time‐dependent, because new CYP protein must be synthesized.  In practice this means a perpetrator must be given for many days (often a week or more) to reach near‐maximal induction ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7603454/#:~:text=usually%20days%20to%20even%20weeks,to%20the%20patient%E2%80%99s%20established%20drug)).  For example, clinical induction trials typically dose a known inducer (e.g. rifampicin) for ~10–14 days before measuring the effect on a CYP substrate.  Importantly, the **impact** of that induction on drug levels depends on how much the CYP enzyme contributes to the drug’s clearance.  Regulatory guidances use the “fraction metabolized” threshold to judge significance – roughly speaking, if a CYP accounts for ≥25% of a drug’s clearance, induction of that CYP can cause a meaningful drop in exposure ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2691466/#:~:text=Understanding%20which%20P450%20enzyme%20is,a%20number%20of%20different%20enzymes)).  Conversely, if the enzyme plays only a minor role, even strong induction has little effect.  This is why in designing DDI studies one “matches” a long enough inducer exposure (to allow enzyme up‐regulation) with substrates where the CYP is a major clearance pathway.  As a practical illustration, St. John’s Wort (a CYP3A4 inducer) cut midazolam’s AUC by ~79% ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2751387/#:~:text=the%20interaction%20between%20midazolam%20,significant%20pharmacological%20consequences%20because%20increased)) – a dramatic effect because CYP3A4 mediates essentially all midazolam clearance.  By contrast, a drug that is only 10% cleared by CYP3A4 would show only a modest change.  In summary, inducer treatment is extended until enzyme levels stabilize (days–weeks) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7603454/#:~:text=usually%20days%20to%20even%20weeks,to%20the%20patient%E2%80%99s%20established%20drug)), and the resulting DDI effect is weighted by the enzyme’s **fraction of clearance** (high f_m → large AUC drop ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2691466/#:~:text=Understanding%20which%20P450%20enzyme%20is,a%20number%20of%20different%20enzymes)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2751387/#:~:text=the%20interaction%20between%20midazolam%20,significant%20pharmacological%20consequences%20because%20increased)), low f_m → small change). 

**Sources:** Regulatory guidance and reviews specify that induction requires repeating dosing over several days to weeks ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7603454/#:~:text=usually%20days%20to%20even%20weeks,to%20the%20patient%E2%80%99s%20established%20drug)), and that only enzymes contributing substantially to clearance (often cited as ≳25%) yield clinically significant induction interactions ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2691466/#:~:text=Understanding%20which%20P450%20enzyme%20is,a%20number%20of%20different%20enzymes)).  Observed examples (midazolam AUC ↓79% with St John’s Wort) confirm that high‐f_m pathways show the biggest effects ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2751387/#:~:text=the%20interaction%20between%20midazolam%20,significant%20pharmacological%20consequences%20because%20increased)).
