# Scenario design in climate/environmental stress tests

In climate and environmental stress tests, **scenario design** means defining the future “what-if” pathways for key drivers of climate risk (e.g. temperature rise, carbon prices, extreme weather events, policy measures, energy transition).  Scenarios typically span _both_ transition risks (policy/technology changes, carbon taxes, emissions paths etc.) and physical risks (frequency/severity of floods, droughts, heatwaves), and map these to macroeconomic and financial variables (GDP, unemployment, asset values) ([www.ecb.europa.eu](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2022/html/ecb.blog221118~e416e71aba.bg.html#:~:text=The%20framework%20explores%20a%20set,transition%20fails%20to%20limit%20physical)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Each%20climate%20change%20scenario%20typically,However)).  For example, the NGFS climate scenarios range from **“orderly”** (early, coordinated policy action) to **“disorderly”** (delayed or uncoordinated action) to **“hot-house world”** (insufficient action, severe physical impacts) ([www.ecb.europa.eu](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2022/html/ecb.blog221118~e416e71aba.bg.html#:~:text=The%20framework%20explores%20a%20set,transition%20fails%20to%20limit%20physical)).  Scenario design choices – such as the length of the horizon (often 20–50 years), the severity of shocks (e.g. sharp carbon tax vs flat trajectory), and the interactions between climate and economy – determine the stress-test shock and thus the loss outcomes.  

Key scenario-design factors include: 

- **Risk drivers and narrative**: What assumptions about policy (carbon pricing, subsidies), technology (electric vehicles, renewables) and physical events (flood frequency, temperature increase) are made. For instance, an “orderly” scenario assumes early emissions cuts, whereas a “disorderly” one assumes abrupt late action ([www.ecb.europa.eu](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2022/html/ecb.blog221118~e416e71aba.bg.html#:~:text=The%20framework%20explores%20a%20set,transition%20fails%20to%20limit%20physical)).  
- **Time horizon**: Environmental risks unfold over decades. Many climate stress scenarios use horizons of 20–50 years ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=attempts%20to%20measure%20outcomes%20over,While)), far beyond typical economic stress tests (e.g. 3–4 years).  
- **Granularity**: Scenarios may be global or regional, and should ideally be **sector-specific**.  The impact on banks depends heavily on sectoral emissions and technology (e.g. steel vs services) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=For%20climate%20change%20stress%20testing%2C,group%20of%20borrowers%20in%20a)).  Without detailed sector/borrower assumptions, loss projections can vary widely.  
- **Mapping to finance**: Climate scenarios must be translated into macro-financial shocks (GDP decline, credit spreads, property price falls, etc.).  This mapping is difficult due to lack of historical analogues ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=likely%20to%20be%20less%20reliable,While)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Each%20climate%20change%20scenario%20typically,However)).  

Thus, scenario design in environmental stress testing is both broader and more uncertain than traditional macro scenarios. It requires adding new climate-specific drivers (carbon prices, physical stress indices) and making long-term projections.  As one analysis notes, “climate stress scenarios include both physical and transition variables…then mapped into unemployment, GDP, housing, yields etc.  However, because the economic consequences of climate change are new, there are currently no data that calibrate the interactions” ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Each%20climate%20change%20scenario%20typically,However)).  These design choices directly shape the stress-test output (losses, profit impact) to which capital adequacy responses are tuned.  

## Impact on projected losses and capital needs

The chosen scenario path determines **the severity and timing of losses** in the stress test.  A more severe scenario (e.g. sudden high carbon price or extreme physical events) will produce larger loan defaults, asset devaluations and revenue hits than a mild scenario.  These losses erode banks’ capital and indicate how much buffer is needed to absorb them.  For example, under an accelerated transition scenario, one study projected climate-related losses equal to ~0.6% of euro-area banks’ risk-weighted assets ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  In that application the regulators then **calibrated an extra buffer** (a systemic climate buffer) roughly one-to-one with those losses, covering ~€52 bn of losses with ~€51 bn in additional capital ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  In other words, the scenario’s severity directly determined the capital volume set aside to ensure resilience.  

Conversely, if scenario design is mild (e.g. low climate policy costs), projected losses may appear small and trigger little extra capital.  Thus **scenario design feeds directly into capital calibration**.  Regulatory practice and proposals often use scenario outputs to set risk buffers.  For instance, the referenced study “proposed a calibration methodology based on granular data and a state-of-the-art climate stress test” so that buffer requirements are **“based on a severe, but realistic, climate risk scenario”** ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20paper%20contributes%20to%20the,response%20based%20on%20a%20severe)).  By contrast, an overly benign scenario would understate risk and lead to insufficient capital.  

- *Example:* The Basel/US CCAR stress-capital buffer rules explicitly **link scenario losses to added capital buffers**, making stress-test design a driver of capital sensitivity ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=The%20focus%20on%20regulatory%20capital,which%20continue%20to%20be%20back)).  In climate stress testing, a similar logic applies: scenario losses suggest how much extra capital (e.g. Pillar 2 add-ons or system-wide buffers) banks should hold.  One working paper uses projected transition losses from the ECB’s climate stress test to calibrate a **climate systemic buffer**, effectively translating stress losses (in bps of RWAs) into additional CET1 requirements ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20paper%20contributes%20to%20the,response%20based%20on%20a%20severe)) ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  

### How scenario design factors influence capital calibration

- **Horizon length:**  Longer horizons (decades) mean losses may appear gradually.  Capital calibration must decide whether to target long-term accumulations or only near-term impacts.  The study above, for example, applied a short (3-year) horizon for transition losses and calibrated buffers for that period; longer scenarios could require higher buffers.  
- **Shock magnitude:**  Initial shocks (e.g. hit from a jump in carbon price) need calibration.  Research shows that using statistical outlier detection to calibrate shock sizes can change the stress horizon and magnitude significantly ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0264999324001007#:~:text=The%20shock%20scenarios%20used%20in,for)).  Larger initial shocks in scenario design imply higher peak losses and thus higher capital buffers.  
- **Multiple scenarios:**  Climate analysts emphasize using multiple scenarios (orderly, disorderly, hot house) to capture uncertainty.  Capital calibration could be based on the worst-case or a prudent blend.  Some proposals suggest calibrating to the “severest credible” scenario, or assigning capital add-ons reflecting each scenario type.  
- **Sector exposure:**  If a bank has concentrated exposure to high-emitting industries, a transition scenario will hit it harder.  Good scenario design will highlight these sectoral shocks, and capital calibration may vary bank-by-bank (higher CET1 for carbon-intensive lenders).  The SUERF study indeed buckets banks by their scenario loss exposure and assigns buffer rates accordingly ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  

In sum, **scenario design determines the stress-test “inputs”**, and capital adequacy is then calibrated to the resulting “output” (losses/capital shortfall).  Tighter design (severe, abrupt transition) yields larger stress losses and calls for a stronger capital response, whereas looser design yields lower losses and correspondingly lighter capital needs.  As one policy review notes, “higher calibration factors (harsher scenario) will increase banks’ loss absorption capacity…higher capital requirements” ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=banks%20and%20time%20horizon%2C%20as,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)). 

## Regulatory context and practical examples

Regulators worldwide are beginning to incorporate climate scenarios into their stress frameworks.  The ECB, EBA, Bank of England, and others have issued draft guidance on ESG scenario analysis linked to ICAAP and Pillar 2 processes.  For example, the ECB’s 2025 EU-wide stress test will add climate risk to its standard scenarios ([www.ecb.europa.eu](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/financial-stability-publications/macroprudential-bulletin/html/ecb.mpbu202511_04.ro.html#:~:text=As%20authorities%20across%20the%20euro,incorporating%20both%20transition%20and%20acute)).  These frameworks will use scenario outputs to refine capital buffers or governance expectations.  Financial firms, in turn, use climate stress outcomes to inform their own internal capital planning (ICAAP) or risk-weighted model parameters.  

**Example – Calibrating a climate buffer:**  In the EU context, researchers have shown how capital buffers can be set from scenario outputs.  The ECB-commissioned study used results of the euro-area climate stress test (an NGFS-aligned **“accelerated transition”** scenario) to derive a new systemic risk buffer.  By mapping each bank’s projected losses to a bucket, the authors assigned buffer rates (e.g. 50–100+ bps of CET1) to offset those losses ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  On aggregate, this buffer almost fully “offsets the impact of a transition shock on capital positions” ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  This illustrates how scenario design (here, an aggressive transition path) directly set the magnitude of capital add-ons.  

**Another perspective:**  Some prudential frameworks suggest using scenario results qualitatively.  The Basel Committee (BCBS) has set out principles encouraging banks to use climate scenario analysis to gauge resilience, but so far stops short of mandating capital charges ([www.bis.org](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/publ/d572.htm#:~:text=In%202022%2C%20the%20Principles%20for,on%20their%20overall%20risk%20profile)).  In the U.S., Fed officials have acknowledged developing climate stress tests under the CCAR framework, which by design adjust the Stress Capital Buffer based on the severity of assigned scenarios.  Even building insurers model climate through their economic capital models, linking scenario outputs to required capital under IFRS S2 disclosure rules.  

## Challenges and uncertainties

While scenario design is critical, it is also fraught with uncertainty.  The Bank Policy Institute cautions that **“climate stress testing…has chosen a much longer time horizon”** and is based on **“limited…expert judgment,”** making its results far more uncertain than traditional stress tests ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=likely%20to%20be%20less%20reliable,While)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Climate%20related%20risks%20are%20significant%2C,scenarios%20to%20the%20macroeconomic%20stress)).  In fact, the BPI argues it is *“premature to add new climate change scenarios to the macroeconomic stress tests”* for capital purposes given current data gaps and modeling challenges ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Climate%20related%20risks%20are%20significant%2C,scenarios%20to%20the%20macroeconomic%20stress)).  

Key challenges include:

- **Lack of historical data:**  There is scant calibration data for climate variables vs. credit losses, so banks must make speculative assumptions ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=likely%20to%20be%20less%20reliable,While)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=For%20climate%20change%20stress%20testing%2C,group%20of%20borrowers%20in%20a)).  This makes stress outputs highly sensitive to scenario choices.  
- **Long, static assumptions:**  Many scenario designs assume fixed bank portfolios over decades, which is unrealistic.  Any capital calibration based on that can be overly conservative (or else irrelevant by the time losses materialize) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=attempts%20to%20measure%20outcomes%20over,While)).  
- **Second-round effects:**  Climate shocks may reset markets and policies globally. Modeling these feedbacks is extremely difficult yet can greatly amplify or dampen losses ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Consequently%2C%20allowing%20for%20the%20modeling,9)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Each%20climate%20change%20scenario%20typically,However)).  
- **Model uncertainty:**  Different models translating climate drivers to losses yield very different outcomes. To account for this, regulators may apply **conservatism factors**.  For instance, the SUERF study noted that lowering the calibration factor (e.g. to 0.5 or 0.25) would reduce the capital added and mitigate model uncertainty ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Bn%2C%20compared%20to%20transition%20losses,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  

Despite these caveats, scenario design remains the linchpin for capital calibration.  Sound scenario construction (covering multiple pathways, credible shocks, and transmission channels) is necessary for any reliable capital adjustment.  As one implementation guide puts it, properly designed climate stress tests “illuminate the costs of delay” and inform policymakers and banks, even if they do not yet translate one-to-one into capital ratios ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Climate%20related%20risks%20are%20significant%2C,scenarios%20to%20the%20macroeconomic%20stress)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=financial%20system%2C%20climate%20change%2C%20and,environmental%20policies)).  

**In practice**, supervisors are likely to use environmental scenarios qualitatively at first, then gradually modify capital requirements as confidence in scenario design grows.  For now, climate scenario outputs may inform Pillar 2 discussions or systemic buffers rather than hard Pillar 1 charges ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20paper%20contributes%20to%20the,response%20based%20on%20a%20severe)) ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  Over time, as scenarios become standardized (e.g. NGFS sets) and models improve, the interplay between scenario design and capital calibration will become more direct and quantitative.

**Summary:**  In environmental risk stress tests, the design of the scenario – its assumptions, severity, and time path – **directly drives the estimated losses and therefore the level of capital buffers deemed necessary**.  A harsher, more abrupt climate scenario produces greater stress losses and leads to higher calibrated capital requirements, whereas a mild scenario produces smaller losses and lower capital needs (all else equal).  Regulators and banks use these scenario-based loss projections to fine-tune capital buffers (e.g. Pillar 2 add-ons or systemic buffers) so that banks remain solvent under plausible environmental risks ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20paper%20contributes%20to%20the,response%20based%20on%20a%20severe)) ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  However, given the novel and long-term nature of climate risks, scenario design is necessarily uncertain, so prudence and cross-disciplinary expertise are paramount in linking scenarios to actual capital adequacy figures ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Climate%20related%20risks%20are%20significant%2C,scenarios%20to%20the%20macroeconomic%20stress)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Each%20climate%20change%20scenario%20typically,However)).  

**Key citations:** For example, Suerf (ECB) finds that a severe “net-zero transition” scenario projects ~0.6% RWAs in losses, and calibrates capital buffers almost one-for-one to absorb that ([www.suerf.org](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/designing-a-macroprudential-capital-buffer-for-climate-related-risks/#:~:text=Our%20baseline%20approach%20leads%20to,as%20under%20more%20adverse%20scenarios)).  By contrast, the Bank Policy Inst. notes that climate stress tests (with decades-long static scenarios) are so uncertain that it may be too soon to use them for formal capital calibration ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=Climate%20related%20risks%20are%20significant%2C,scenarios%20to%20the%20macroeconomic%20stress)) ([bpi.com](https://bpi.com/challenges-in-stress-testing-and-climate-change/#:~:text=likely%20to%20be%20less%20reliable,While)).  Together, these sources illustrate how scenario design shapes – and currently complicates – the calibration of capital adequacy under environmental risk.
