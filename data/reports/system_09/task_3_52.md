# Atmospheric Blocking and Precipitation Extremes  
Atmospheric *blocking* refers to large, quasi-stationary high-pressure systems (or equivalent troughs) that disrupt the normal west‐to‐east flow.  Such blocks often **persist** for days or weeks, causing unusually prolonged weather patterns (e.g. drought, heatwaves or stalled storm systems).  Because blocking can “trap” moisture or steer storms slowly, it tends to cluster precipitation and amplify heavy-rain events in affected regions.  In statistical terms, this persistence breaks the usual assumption of independent daily weather: blocks introduce strong temporal correlation (low *extremal index*) in precipitation.  

In practice, models that ignore blocking durations often under-estimate tail risk.  By explicitly modeling block duration (for example by treating “blocked” vs “unblocked” states as regimes, or by including a blocking-index covariate in an extreme-value model), the model implicitly allows multiple heavy-rain days in a row.  This typically **raises the predicted probability of extreme precipitation**.  In other words, the tail of the rainfall distribution becomes heavier once block-induced persistence is included.  

## Evidence from Statistical Extreme-Value Models  
Recent studies quantify this effect.  For instance, Fauer and Rust (2023) fitted a non-stationary GEV model for precipitation in Germany that includes time, NAO, temperature and a blocking-index as predictors ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-023-02515-z#:~:text=model%20performs%20better%20than%20a,they%20are%20negatively%20correlated%20with)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-023-02515-z#:~:text=the%20effect%20of%20the%20NAO,precipitation%20should%20be%20acknowledged%20more)).  They found that:  
- **Improved fit and higher tail probabilities.**  The “large-scale” model with the blocking covariate significantly outperforms a stationary model without it ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-023-02515-z#:~:text=model%20performs%20better%20than%20a,they%20are%20negatively%20correlated%20with)).  In particular, extreme precipitation probabilities *increase* in association with blocking: high probabilities of extreme rain are **positively correlated** with block presence in summer ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-023-02515-z#:~:text=model%20performs%20better%20than%20a,they%20are%20negatively%20correlated%20with)).  
- **Magnitude of change.**  The study shows that including the blocking index can change the estimated exceedance probability *by a factor of two or more* ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-023-02515-z#:~:text=the%20effect%20of%20the%20NAO,precipitation%20should%20be%20acknowledged%20more)).  Concretely, they report that the blocking index (along with NAO and temperature) occasionally *doubles* the probability of a very heavy rain event compared to the baseline model.  This means a “100‐year event” in a stationary model might be closer to a 50‐year event when blocking is accounted for.  

These results agree with the physical expectation that long‐lived blocks allow greater moisture accumulation or persistent uplift.  In summer, for example, a Scandinavian or Greenland blocking ridge can steer slow-moving convective storms or tap subtropical moisture, so that daily rains pile up over the same area.  The statistical model captures this by effectively stretching the tails of the precipitation distribution under blocked conditions.  

## Implications for Probability Forecasts  
In summary, **statistical models that include blocking duration predict higher extreme-rainfall probabilities** than models that do not.  Accounting for block persistence introduces a form of intra-cluster dependence, which raises extremal probabilities.  As a result, return levels for rare events become larger (or equivalently, predicted return periods shorten).  This has practical importance: ignoring blocking can lead to under-preparation for extreme floods or prolonged downpours, whereas models with blocking give more realistic risk estimates.  

**Key takeaway:**  Incorporating atmospheric blocking as a statistical covariate or regime leads to significantly higher forecast probabilities for extreme precipitation.  For example, a GEV analysis in Central Europe showed that adding a blocking index improved model skill and roughly **doubled** the predicted chance of very heavy rains when a block is present ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-023-02515-z#:~:text=model%20performs%20better%20than%20a,they%20are%20negatively%20correlated%20with)) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-023-02515-z#:~:text=the%20effect%20of%20the%20NAO,precipitation%20should%20be%20acknowledged%20more)).
