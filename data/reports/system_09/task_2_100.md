# Autonomy-Preserving Design Minimizes Privacy Intrusion  

Under an autonomy-centered framework, brain‐computer interfaces (BCIs) are designed to operate only under the explicit control of the user.  In practice this often means the device remains “off” by default and only reads or transmits brain signals when the user actively enables it. For example, speech-BCIs can implement an **executory control** (a user‐triggered “on” switch) so that the neural decoder is only engaged when the user intends to communicate ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11240287/#:~:text=could%20constitute%20control%20over%20whether,also%20covers%20the%20choice%20for)).  This ensures the BCI does not passively harvest or externalize any neural data beyond what the user explicitly authorizes.  In effect, the BCI acts as an auxiliary tool **subordinate to human autonomy** – it only amplifies segments of action the user has chosen, and the ultimate goals and commands remain governed by the user ([www.cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-responsible-artificial-intelligence/responsible-ai-healthcare-and-neurotechnology-governance/C061FD150FC65B4F673E23F49A22DD5A#:~:text=brain%E2%80%93computer%20interfaces%20as%20auxiliary%20devices,remains%20governed%20by%20human%20agency)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11240287/#:~:text=could%20constitute%20control%20over%20whether,also%20covers%20the%20choice%20for)).  By tying BCI operation strictly to user intention, the system by design minimizes any unauthorized intrusion into the user’s mental states and preserves “mental privacy.”  

## Rights-Based Standards and Data Minimization  

This autonomy emphasis is reinforced by emerging ethical and legal standards.  UNESCO’s neuroethics guidelines explicitly enshrine the inviolability of the human mind and uphold cognitive liberty, treating neural data as a unique privacy frontier ([www.theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2025/nov/06/unesco-adopts-global-standards-on-wild-west-field-of-neurotechnology#:~:text=%E2%80%9CNeurotechnology%20has%20the%20potential%20to,the%20human%20mind%E2%80%9D%2C%20she%20said)) ([www.theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2025/nov/06/unesco-adopts-global-standards-on-wild-west-field-of-neurotechnology#:~:text=Advocates%20for%20neurotech%20regulation%20emphasise,privacy%E2%80%9D%20and%20%E2%80%9Cfreedom%20of%20thought%E2%80%9D)).  In these standards, concepts like **mental privacy** and freedom of thought are highlighted as fundamental rights.  In practice, that means any BCI must obtain informed consent before accessing brain data, and must process only the minimal neurodata needed for the permitted task.  For instance, data-protection principles (akin to “privacy by design” or GDPR’s data-minimization rule) are applied: neural signals are recorded, held and transmitted only with the user’s permission, and only the smallest necessary signal features are used.  By treating the BCI strictly as a user‐controlled prosthesis and insisting on explicit opt-in for any data collection or decoding, the autonomy framework enforces that privacy interventions are kept to an absolute minimum ([www.theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2025/nov/06/unesco-adopts-global-standards-on-wild-west-field-of-neurotechnology#:~:text=%E2%80%9CNeurotechnology%20has%20the%20potential%20to,the%20human%20mind%E2%80%9D%2C%20she%20said)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11951885/#:~:text=issues%20ranging%20from%20accountability%20to,further%20constituted%20by%20the%20UNESCO)).
