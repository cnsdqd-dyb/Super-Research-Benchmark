In matrix‐models of migration, countries (or regions) are nodes and migration flows are encoded by a nonnegative matrix $A$.  The **spectral radius** $\rho(A)$ (its largest eigenvalue) sets the long‐run growth/connectivity of the system.  In fact, if $X_n$ is the population vector at time $n$, then one step of the model is $X_{n+1}=A\,X_n$, so after $k$ steps $X_k=A^k X_0$.  For large $k$, $X_k\approx \rho(A)^k\,v$, where $v$ is the Perron (dominant) eigenvector ([math.libretexts.org](https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Coastline_College/Math_C285%3A_Linear_Algebra_and_Diffrential_Equations_%28Tran%29/07%3A_Spectral_Theory/7.03%3A_Applications_of_Spectral_Theory#:~:text=,summarized%20in%20the%20following%20theorem)).  Thus $\rho(A)$ governs the asymptotic behavior of repeated migration.

When the model is truly **stochastic** (i.e.\ each column of $A$ sums to 1, so $A$ is a Markov transition matrix), the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees $1$ is an eigenvalue ([math.libretexts.org](https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Coastline_College/Math_C285%3A_Linear_Algebra_and_Diffrential_Equations_%28Tran%29/07%3A_Spectral_Theory/7.03%3A_Applications_of_Spectral_Theory#:~:text=Let%20%5C%28A%3D%5Cleft%20%5B%20a_,A)).  In fact for any column-stochastic migration matrix the largest eigenvalue is exactly 1 (so the spectral radius $\rho(A)=1$).  Hence **any small policy change that preserves the stochastic normalization (reallocating flows but keeping column sums =1)** will *not* change $\rho(A)$ from 1 – it merely redistributes the eigenvector (the steady-state) but the dominant eigenvalue stays 1 ([math.libretexts.org](https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Coastline_College/Math_C285%3A_Linear_Algebra_and_Diffrential_Equations_%28Tran%29/07%3A_Spectral_Theory/7.03%3A_Applications_of_Spectral_Theory#:~:text=Let%20%5C%28A%3D%5Cleft%20%5B%20a_,A)).  Only if a perturbation breaks the stochastic constraint (e.g.\ permits net growth or shrinkage so column sums change) will $\rho(A)$ move away from 1.

In a more general (non‐normalized) flow matrix, perturbing policy parameters changes the entries of $A$, and $\rho(A)$ shifts continuously.  By standard matrix perturbation theory (e.g. Rayleigh quotient arguments), a small change $\Delta A$ produces a first-order change in the Perron root given by 
$$\Delta \rho\;\approx\; y^T (\Delta A)\,x,$$ 
where $x,y$ are the right and left Perron eigenvectors of $A$, normalized so that $y^T x=1$.  In other words, the sensitivity of the dominant eigenvalue to an entry $a_{ij}$ is roughly $y_i x_j$.  Concretely, if a policy parameter increases migration from region $j$ to $i$ (increasing $a_{ij}$), then $\rho(A)$ will increase by about $y_i x_j\;\Delta a_{ij}$.  Since $x$ and $y$ are positive for an irreducible matrix (Perron–Frobenius), this shows **increasing any positive flow entry raises the spectral radius**, and decreasing an entry lowers it.  In fact one can show monotonically that for irreducible nonnegative $A$, $\rho(A)$ is a strictly increasing function of any entry.  

In practical terms, policy changes on “high‐centrality” routes have larger effect: opening a major migration corridor (where the involved eigenvector components $x_j,y_i$ are large) yields a proportionally larger jump in $\rho$, while altering low‐traffic links has smaller impact.  Intuitively, if migration is thought to amplify population (or connectivity) each period, the spectral radius measures that amplification factor.  Thus **perturbing policy parameters that boost overall migrant flows tends to increase $\rho$, whereas tightening restrictions (reducing flows) decreases $\rho$**.  In a stochastic (normalized) model $\rho$ remains pinned at 1 (no net amplification), but in any model where total flow magnitude can change, $\rho$ will move up or down in direct response to the policy‐driven matrix perturbations.

**References:**  By Perron–Frobenius, a (column) stochastic migration matrix $A$ always has eigenvalue 1 ([math.libretexts.org](https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Coastline_College/Math_C285%3A_Linear_Algebra_and_Diffrential_Equations_%28Tran%29/07%3A_Spectral_Theory/7.03%3A_Applications_of_Spectral_Theory#:~:text=Let%20%5C%28A%3D%5Cleft%20%5B%20a_,A)), so $\rho(A)=1$.  Repeated application gives $X_{n+1}=AX_n$, hence $X_k=A^kX_0$ ([math.libretexts.org](https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Coastline_College/Math_C285%3A_Linear_Algebra_and_Diffrential_Equations_%28Tran%29/07%3A_Spectral_Theory/7.03%3A_Applications_of_Spectral_Theory#:~:text=,summarized%20in%20the%20following%20theorem)), whose growth is set by $\rho(A)$.  First‐order eigen‐perturbation formulas (e.g.\ in standard texts on matrix perturbation theory) imply $\Delta\rho\approx y^T(\Delta A)x$, so $\rho$ shifts proportionally to eigenvector‐weighted changes in $A$.
