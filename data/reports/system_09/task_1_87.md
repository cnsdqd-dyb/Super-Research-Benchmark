In practice there is no magical “granularity knob” – the depth and detail of product categories are set by the taxonomy design and business rules, not by the products themselves.  In real e‐commerce systems the taxonomy architects decide how deep to make the category tree.  For example, Alibaba notes that multi‐level product catalogs are usually kept at a *coarse* granularity – if you try to split categories too finely, the leaf categories constantly shift as products change (e.g. if you have a leaf category “Silk Dresses”, every time a dress changes material you’d have to re‐assign it, whereas a single “Dresses” category is stable) ([patents.justia.com](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20140143094#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20preserve%20the,architecture%20is%20bound%20to%20be)) ([patents.justia.com](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20140143094#:~:text=For%20example%2C%20assume%20the%20product,information%20from%20the%20leaf%20category)).  In short, very fine splits lead to instability and an unwieldy, oversized hierarchy, so designers often stop subdivision when further splits would hurt stability or usefulness ([patents.justia.com](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20140143094#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20preserve%20the,architecture%20is%20bound%20to%20be)) ([patents.justia.com](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20140143094#:~:text=For%20example%2C%20assume%20the%20product,information%20from%20the%20leaf%20category)).

More generally, taxonomy best practices impose the granularity boundary.  Usability guides typically recommend limiting depth (often 3–5 levels) and keeping top‐level counts manageable ([medium.com](https://medium.com/scalia/the-dos-and-don-ts-of-product-taxonomy-e8b452c5dc78#:~:text=In%20our%20opinion%20a%20robust,selling%20and%20your%20product%20normalization)).  Categories are usually driven by core product functions or use‐cases, browsing behavior and supply considerations (for example, products sharing an attribute “match” a category or else fall into the closest match) ([key-g.com](https://key-g.com/uk/blog/classification-of-products-a-practical-guide-to-product-categorization/#:~:text=Start%20with%20a%20proper%2C%20data,factors%20to%20separate%20edge%20cases)) ([key-g.com](https://key-g.com/uk/blog/classification-of-products-a-practical-guide-to-product-categorization/#:~:text=Between%20categories%2C%20set%20thresholds%20that,to%20only%20the%20closest%20match)).  In other words, each cut‐off is set by simple rules or thresholds: if a potential sub‐category would contain too few products, or if splitting it doesn’t yield a clearly stronger category match, the system just stops at the parent level.  This keeps the tree navigable and easy to maintain.  Ultimately, the granularity boundary is controlled by design choices – balancing detail against stability, manageability and user experience ([key-g.com](https://key-g.com/uk/blog/classification-of-products-a-practical-guide-to-product-categorization/#:~:text=Center%20the%20thinking%20on%20consumers%2C,supply%20and%20respond%20to%20demand)) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/scalia/the-dos-and-don-ts-of-product-taxonomy-e8b452c5dc78#:~:text=In%20our%20opinion%20a%20robust,selling%20and%20your%20product%20normalization)).  In automated systems this often translates into confidence thresholds or stopping criteria (so that a classifier won’t descend into a child category if it isn’t very sure), but fundamentally it comes down to human‐defined taxonomy rules and usability needs, not an inherent algorithmic limit ([key-g.com](https://key-g.com/uk/blog/classification-of-products-a-practical-guide-to-product-categorization/#:~:text=Start%20with%20a%20proper%2C%20data,factors%20to%20separate%20edge%20cases)) ([patents.justia.com](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20140143094#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20preserve%20the,architecture%20is%20bound%20to%20be)).

**Answer:** The depth of the category tree is set by the taxonomy’s design rules and practical constraints (stability, maintainability, usability) – e.g. stable core categories, minimal product thresholds for splits, browsing‐behavior signals, etc. These determine where the hierarchy stops, rather than any fixed algorithmic parameter. For instance, Alibaba explicitly keeps a coarse granularity to keep categories stable ([patents.justia.com](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20140143094#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20preserve%20the,architecture%20is%20bound%20to%20be)), and industry guides advise limiting depth (typically ~3–5 levels) so categories remain meaningful ([medium.com](https://medium.com/scalia/the-dos-and-don-ts-of-product-taxonomy-e8b452c5dc78#:~:text=In%20our%20opinion%20a%20robust,selling%20and%20your%20product%20normalization)). Thus the “boundary” is controlled by business/design decisions (and in automated systems by confidence thresholds), not by the products themselves ([patents.justia.com](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20140143094#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20preserve%20the,architecture%20is%20bound%20to%20be)) ([key-g.com](https://key-g.com/uk/blog/classification-of-products-a-practical-guide-to-product-categorization/#:~:text=Between%20categories%2C%20set%20thresholds%20that,to%20only%20the%20closest%20match)).
