# Species–Area Patterns and Reserve Size  
Protected‐area planners know from island biogeography and the species–area relationship (SAR) that larger habitat patches hold more species.  In general **species richness grows as a power of area** (S = c A^z), so big reserves tend to capture **greater habitat diversity and more species** ([bio.libretexts.org](https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Ecology/Conservation_Biology_in_Sub-Saharan_Africa_%28Wilson_and_Primack%29/13%3A_The_Importance_of_Protected_Areas/13.05%3A_Designing_Protected_Areas#:~:text=Specifically%2C%20because%20large%20protected%20areas,and%20live%20at%20low%20densities)).  In practice this means a single large park normally **supports a larger variety of ecosystems and larger wildlife populations** than an equal total area in many tiny fragments ([bio.libretexts.org](https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Ecology/Conservation_Biology_in_Sub-Saharan_Africa_%28Wilson_and_Primack%29/13%3A_The_Importance_of_Protected_Areas/13.05%3A_Designing_Protected_Areas#:~:text=Specifically%2C%20because%20large%20protected%20areas,and%20live%20at%20low%20densities)).  Because of this scaling law, small isolated reserves quickly lose species: as area shrinks, diversity collapses.  

# Critical Patch Thresholds  
Metapopulation theory shows that **each species has a minimum habitat threshold** below which it cannot persist.  Hanski et al. (2013) summarize that empirical and modeling studies have found an **“extinction threshold”** in fragmented landscapes: if fragment sizes (or total habitat amount) drop below a certain critical value (species‐specific), then local extinctions outrun recolonization ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=landscapes,analogous%20to%20the%20eradication%20threshold)).  In other words, **below that threshold the colonization rate cannot compensate for extinctions** ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=landscapes,analogous%20to%20the%20eradication%20threshold)).  For many forest birds and mammals, for example, remaining habitat in heavily cleared regions (e.g. <10% forest cover in the Atlantic Forest) lies below these critical levels, so populations collapse ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=landscapes%20with%2010,the%20conventional%20SAR%20at%20the)).  Metapopulation models formalize this: the landscape’s “metapopulation capacity” (λ) – a measure of how well patches sustain populations – **rises with total area but falls sharply with fragmentation** (because isolation cuts connectivity) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=%2830%20%2C%20%2033%29,34%20Materials)).  In short, patches smaller than the critical size (or too isolated) cannot hold viable populations, no matter how many species they initially contained.  

## Implications for Reserve Design  
- **Reserve size vs. number (SLOSS debate):**  The SAR suggests large reserves are safer for retaining species.  Indeed, many planners favor one single large protected area over many tiny ones of equal total area, to ensure populations remain above extinction thresholds ([bio.libretexts.org](https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Ecology/Conservation_Biology_in_Sub-Saharan_Africa_%28Wilson_and_Primack%29/13%3A_The_Importance_of_Protected_Areas/13.05%3A_Designing_Protected_Areas#:~:text=Specifically%2C%20because%20large%20protected%20areas,and%20live%20at%20low%20densities)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=landscapes,analogous%20to%20the%20eradication%20threshold)).  (Large patches maintain more interior habitat and self-sustaining populations.)  Interestingly, recent syntheses find that **many small patches can cumulatively include more species than one large patch** of the same area ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37021385/#:~:text=habitat%20existing%20as%20a%20few,patches%20than%20the%20small%20patches)), since separate fragments may each support different species assemblages.  However, while “several small” can boost raw species count, those small patches often fail to sustain wide-ranging or area-sensitive species long-term ([bio.libretexts.org](https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Ecology/Conservation_Biology_in_Sub-Saharan_Africa_%28Wilson_and_Primack%29/13%3A_The_Importance_of_Protected_Areas/13.05%3A_Designing_Protected_Areas#:~:text=Specifically%2C%20because%20large%20protected%20areas,and%20live%20at%20low%20densities)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=landscapes,analogous%20to%20the%20eradication%20threshold)).  In practice, protected-area design usually prioritizes **patches above the critical size needed for target species** (or connects them) to ensure viability.  

- **Connectivity and fragmentation:**  Because fragmentation effectively reduces patch size, designers often link reserves with corridors or buffer zones.  Connected reserves behave like one larger area (increasing λ), whereas isolated fragments behave like many mini‐islands (each below critical size).  Hanski et al. show that conventional SAR (ignoring fragmentation) grossly **overestimates species persistence** in highly fragmented systems ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=total%20landscape%20area%20in%20this,several%20sets%20of%20dissimilarly%20fragmented)).  The “species–fragmented-area relationship” (SFAR) model improves on SAR by including connectivity: it predicts far fewer survivors if fragmentation is severe ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=model%20and%20with%20empirical%20data,the%20number%20of%20extinctions%20for)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=total%20landscape%20area%20in%20this,several%20sets%20of%20dissimilarly%20fragmented)).  In other words, for a given total protected area, splitting it into many isolated reserves will support **fewer species** (especially fragmentation-intolerant ones) than keeping it intact ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=total%20landscape%20area%20in%20this,several%20sets%20of%20dissimilarly%20fragmented)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=model%20and%20with%20empirical%20data,the%20number%20of%20extinctions%20for)).  Thus design must minimize fragmentation or compensate with corridors to keep most patches effectively above the critical size.  

- **Population viability (area requirements):**  Many species have large home ranges or need core habitat; these impose hard “minimum area requirements.”  If reserves are too small, such species will be extirpated regardless of overall species count.  Protected-area guidelines often use these minimum patch sizes as constraints.  For example, planners may *exclude* candidate patches below a given size or combine small patches to form core areas.  In effect, the critical-fragment‐size concept **sets a lower bound** on what fragments can be considered viable reserves.  Any design that includes patches below that bound risks losing species.  

- **“Resiliency” and redundancy:**  Because smaller patches are more vulnerable to disturbance and edge effects, large areas also contribute resilience.  Extended contiguous habitat (above critical sizes) can buffer climate change, fire, and other catastrophes that might wipe out entire small reserves otherwise.  Planners therefore often balance representation (capturing all ecosystems) with **resiliency** (ensuring sufficiently large and connected reserves).  

**Summary:**  In protected-area planning, the species–area law and the concept of a critical patch size place strong constraints on design.  Simply put, to **protect species effectively**, reserves must either be large enough (or well-connected) to exceed the species’ area-thresholds.  Breaking up habitat into too many small fragments will inevitably leave many species without sufficient area or connectivity to survive ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=landscapes,analogous%20to%20the%20eradication%20threshold)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=%2830%20%2C%20%2033%29,34%20Materials)).  This drives the common recommendation that a reserve network should include substantial contiguous blocks of habitat (often supplemented by corridors) rather than only small isolated patches ([bio.libretexts.org](https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Ecology/Conservation_Biology_in_Sub-Saharan_Africa_%28Wilson_and_Primack%29/13%3A_The_Importance_of_Protected_Areas/13.05%3A_Designing_Protected_Areas#:~:text=Specifically%2C%20because%20large%20protected%20areas,and%20live%20at%20low%20densities)) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3732936/#:~:text=model%20and%20with%20empirical%20data,the%20number%20of%20extinctions%20for)).
