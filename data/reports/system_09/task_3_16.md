In AI ethics and fairness research, a **responsibility-attribution function** is often used to assign “blame” or “credit” scores to an agent or outcome.  For example, Hiller *et al*. explain that a *formalized and quantifiable responsibility score* is a crucial component in multi‐agent decision systems and can be based on probabilities of outcomes ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.06711#:~:text=A%20formalized%20and%20quantifiable%20responsibility,the%20deterministic%20causal%20views%20employed)).  Intuitively, one can think of this score \(R(x)\) as a continuous function over the AI’s decision space (the set of possible actions or outcomes \(x\)), where higher values mean more moral responsibility (blame). 

Mathematically, saying \(R\) is **convex** means that for any two outcomes \(x_1, x_2\) and any weight \(0\le\lambda\le 1\),  
\[ R(\lambda x_1 + (1-\lambda)x_2) \;\le\; \lambda R(x_1) + (1-\lambda) R(x_2). \]  
In plain language, the responsibility of a “mixed” outcome can’t exceed the weighted average of its components.  Geometrically, the graph of a convex function is “cup”-shaped ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_function#:~:text=In%20mathematics%20%2C%20a%20,cap)), and importantly its sublevel sets \(\{x:R(x)\le c\}\) are convex regions.  Equivalently, any straight-line combination of two actions that are both below a blame-threshold also stays below that threshold.  This means if two decisions are judged morally acceptable, any probabilistic blend of them is also acceptable.  

This convexity has a direct impact on the **stability boundary** of moral judgments.  Imagine we set a responsibility threshold (e.g. \(R(x)=\tau\)) that separates “permissible” actions from “blameworthy” ones.  Because \(R\) is convex, the set of all permissible actions \( \{R(x)\le\tau\}\) is itself a convex (and typically one-piece) region in the decision space.  The boundary \(R(x)=\tau\) will be a smooth convex hypersurface (often analogous to a linear decision boundary in a transformed space).  In other words, moral judgments behave like a stable classifier: small changes in the AI’s decision won’t flip the verdict unless those changes move the outcome across this convex threshold.  In machine learning, for example, convex decision functions (like linear SVMs) produce simple, robust boundaries ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.05385#:~:text=that%20the%20last%20weight%20layer,The%20experiment%20codes%20are)).  Li *et al*. showed that deep networks often converge to a linear (hence convex) decision boundary on their last layer – a situation where the “acceptable” and “unacceptable” regions are cleanly separated by a flat hyperplane ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.05385#:~:text=that%20the%20last%20weight%20layer,The%20experiment%20codes%20are)).  By analogy, a convex responsibility function ensures the moral “line” between good and bad actions is similarly well-behaved.  

In contrast, if the responsibility function were **non-convex**, the moral boundary could be highly irregular or fragmented.  A non-convex \(R\) might produce multiple disjoint acceptable regions or sharp folds, meaning tiny changes in context could unpredictably swing the moral judgment from one class to another.  But with convexity, the stability boundary is essentially the level-set \(R(x)=\tau\), which by convexity is smooth and monotonic.  This makes the AI’s moral evaluations *stable* in the sense that as you move continuously through decision space, the moral verdict changes only once when you cross this single, convex threshold surface.  In sum, convex responsibility functions yield convex “safe” regions and therefore simple, stable moral decision boundaries ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_function#:~:text=In%20mathematics%20%2C%20a%20,cap)) ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.05385#:~:text=that%20the%20last%20weight%20layer,The%20experiment%20codes%20are)). 

**Sources:** The notion of a formal responsibility score in AI ethics is discussed by Hiller *et al.* ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.06711#:~:text=A%20formalized%20and%20quantifiable%20responsibility,the%20deterministic%20causal%20views%20employed)).  Convex functions and their properties (including convex level sets) are standard in optimization theory ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_function#:~:text=In%20mathematics%20%2C%20a%20,cap)).  Convexity in learning models leads to linear or convex decision boundaries (as in linear SVMs) that are empirically well-behaved ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.05385#:~:text=that%20the%20last%20weight%20layer,The%20experiment%20codes%20are)).  (By analogy, a convex moral-responsibility function imposes a similarly robust, single-piece boundary on moral judgments in the decision space.)
