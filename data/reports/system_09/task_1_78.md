# Activation‐Energy Barriers in Polymer Degradation  
Recycling a plastic chemically (e.g. via pyrolysis or depolymerization) requires cleaving its polymer chains, which demands overcoming very large activation‐energy barriers.  In a typical polyolefin (like PE or PP), for instance, homolytic backbone scission requires on the order of 150–250 kJ/mol ([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/gc/d2gc02588d#:~:text=Peterson%20et%20al.,186)).  Such high barriers mean that at moderate processing conditions only the weakest (or most labile) bonds break. The result is that **only a fraction of the polymers** reacts at any given temperature/time: bonds in short chains or at defect sites (which have slightly lower effective barriers) will cleave first, while the longest, most stable chains remain intact under those same conditions.  Conversely, driving the reaction hard enough to crack the heaviest chains inevitably over‐cracks whatever fragments were already cleaved, producing a mélange of small molecules. In short, because C–C bond cleavage is kinetically demanding, one **cannot partially depolymerize a broad mixture without affecting all fractions**; the kinetics bind the fate of the different molecular‐weight segments.  

## Effect of Molecular‐Weight Distribution  
Commercial plastics are polydisperse – they contain a range of chain lengths and molecular weights.  Each length class can have somewhat different reactivity or decomposition pathway.  For example, experiments show that the *pyrolysis product slate* depends on both polymer size and severity. At low pyrolysis temperatures the products are dominated by high‐molecular‐weight oils or waxes, whereas at higher temperatures/light‐cracking conditions the output shifts entirely to light oils and gases ([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/gc/d2gc02588d#:~:text=product%20molecular%20weights%20as%20reaction,decompose%20into%20a%20distribution%20of)). Crucially, *polymer molecular weight itself* alters the decomposition chemistry.  Chae and Choi (2025) report that as PE’s weight‐average molecular weight increases, the tendency to form alkadienes (unsaturated C=C compounds) grows while alkane yields fall ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4360/17/5/576#:~:text=abundances%20of%20alkanes%20tended%20to,than%20those%20at%20764%20%C2%B0C)).  In other words, **high‐M polymers favor unsaturated fragments** and give larger “molecular” products at a given condition, whereas low‐M polymers crack more easily into lighter, saturated fragments.  This chain‐length dependence arises because longer, more entangled chains require extra energy (or longer times) to unzip, while shorter chains break more randomly.  

Because of this broad MWD, a single set of reaction conditions never converts the feed uniformly.  At a given temperature, only those portions of the MWD with activation barriers below that energy will largely react.  The remaining high‐Mw tail simply remains.  If one raises temperature to convert the leftovers, the fraction already cleaved now degrades further (for example, producing even smaller molecules or undesired side‐products).  The result is a *continuously varying* product distribution rather than a sharp “purified” output.  In practice, pyrolysis of mixed plastics always yields a range of chain fragments: heavy oils, light oils, and gases coexist, and their proportions slide with temperature/treatment time ([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/gc/d2gc02588d#:~:text=product%20molecular%20weights%20as%20reaction,decompose%20into%20a%20distribution%20of)).  

## Implications for Recycling Purity  
The net effect is that activation‐energy barriers impose a fundamental limit on purity control.  One cannot fine‐tune conditions to yield exclusively one molecular species because of the MWD.  For instance, trying to depolymerize all chains down to monomer will require very aggressive conditions (high T or strong catalyst), but under those conditions even short oligomers will be fully cracked and even start to degrade (often to undesired byproducts). Conversely, milder conditions will preserve larger chains but leave some fraction of the polymer unreacted (so the output still contains high‐Mw residues).  

Even advanced catalytic or solvent‐mediated methods only partially alleviate this.  For example, using a hydrogen‐donor solvent (supercritical methanol) can dramatically lower the barrier to PS depolymerization (≈ 117 kJ/mol vs ≈ 224 kJ/mol in plain pyrolysis) ([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/gc/d2gc02588d#:~:text=degrading%20PS%20in%20supercritical%20methanol,Besides)). This makes the process faster and somewhat more selective toward monomeric products. But it **does not change the fact** that a distribution of chain lengths will yield a mix of products – in this case, hydrogenated (saturated) versus unsaturated aromatics – depending on which bonds react first. In other words, even when the overall activation hurdle is reduced, the broad MWD still translates to a broad “energy‐window” of scission events, so purity control must rely on downstream separation rather than reaction selectivity.  

In summary, the high and distributed activation energies for polymer bond cleavage mean that a polydisperse plastic feed cannot be cleanly converted into a single pure product. The conversion profile is inherently broad: low‐energy pathways consume the easiest bonds first while high‐energy pathways (needed for long chains) kick in later, invariably producing a spectrum of fragments. This kinetic reality – borne out by pyrolysis studies of PE, PP, PET, etc. – fundamentally constrains how narrowly one can control the molecular composition of recycled plastic⁤ ([pubs.rsc.org](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/gc/d2gc02588d#:~:text=Peterson%20et%20al.,186)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4360/17/5/576#:~:text=abundances%20of%20alkanes%20tended%20to,than%20those%20at%20764%20%C2%B0C)). Ultimately, **purity in recycling must be managed by separation and purification, not by “tweaking” reaction conditions alone**, because the activation barriers tie all chain lengths together in the reaction dynamics.

All of these kinetic facts underscore the difficulty of obtaining “pure” monomer or polymer from a broad molecular-weight feed.
