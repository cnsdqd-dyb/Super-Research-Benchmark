# Heterogeneous CPU–GPU Scheduling for Throughput vs. Energy

A good strategy is to schedule tasks to maximize *performance per watt*.  In practice one assigns work to the device (CPU or GPU) that gives the highest throughput for the least energy, effectively equalizing marginal performance/watt.  In fact, Chen and Marculescu (2017) proved this principle: they derive an **analytical optimal scheduler** (called **CAB**) for a two-resource CPU+GPU system ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=theory,for%20any%20task%20size%20distribution)).  CAB uses queueing-theoretic analysis to allocate tasks so as to maximize overall throughput under energy constraints.  In experiments it significantly beat simple load-balancing or round-robin schemes – achieving roughly 1.1×–2.2× more throughput *and* up to 2.3× better energy efficiency ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=theory,for%20any%20task%20size%20distribution)) ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=real%20platform%20to%20show%20the,9.07x%20better%20performance%20in%20experiments)).

- **CAB (Chen et al. 2017)**: The “Completion-Aware” optimal policy for a CPU+GPU pair.  CAB analytically computes steady-state service rates and dispatches each task to the processor (CPU or GPU) that maximizes the marginal throughput per watt ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=theory,for%20any%20task%20size%20distribution)).  This formal scheduler simultaneously optimizes for both performance and energy.  Compared to naïve CPU/GPU load-splitting, CAB showed ~2× improvements in throughput-per-watt ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=real%20platform%20to%20show%20the,9.07x%20better%20performance%20in%20experiments)).

- **Heuristic/ML Co-scheduling**: Modern methods use predictive models to make similar trade-offs dynamically.  For example, Saba et al. (2024) treat co-scheduling, resource partitioning, and power capping as one joint optimization and solve it with machine learning ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.03831#:~:text=%281%29%20co,distributes%20power%20budgets%20across%20components)).  Their scheduler learns how to co-locate programs on CPU/GPU and set power budgets to maximize total throughput under a power cap.  In practice it saw ~67% higher throughput vs. a naïve round-robin+static-power scheme ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.03831#:~:text=%281%29%20co,distributes%20power%20budgets%20across%20components)).  These learned policies effectively aim for the same throughput-per-watt balance as CAB, but adapt at runtime to workload characteristics.

In summary, the state-of-the-art is to use a **performance-per-watt scheduling policy**.  The analytically-derived CAB algorithm is provably optimal for a single CPU+GPU system ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=theory,for%20any%20task%20size%20distribution)), and thus “best balances” throughput with energy.  In real systems one can also use DVFS or ML-based co-schedulers (as above) to achieve nearly the same energy-vs.-performance trade-off. 

**Answer:** In short, the queueing‐theory based “CAB” scheduler (Chen & Marculescu 2017) is optimal for a CPU+GPU pair, maximizing throughput per energy ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=theory,for%20any%20task%20size%20distribution)) ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03209#:~:text=real%20platform%20to%20show%20the,9.07x%20better%20performance%20in%20experiments)).  (Modern ML-based co-schedulers also follow the same principle, dynamically maximizing perf/watt ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.03831#:~:text=%281%29%20co,distributes%20power%20budgets%20across%20components)).)
