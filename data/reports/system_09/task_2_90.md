In hierarchical‐configuration (multi‐level) models, the “right” threshold for treating a node as a warehouse (versus leaving it as a simple transit node) is **not arbitrary** – it emerges from the network’s structure and cost/demand trade‐offs.  In practice the optimal threshold is set so that opening a warehouse node (or treating a node as a hub) **just balances the costs of added connectivity against the benefits of distribution**.  Concretely, this balance depends on:

- **Connectivity and degree‐distribution**.  The key determinant is how many **inter‐community links** (or inter‐warehouse edges) each node has.  In a configuration‐model random network, a giant connected component appears once the average branching factor exceeds one.  Equivalently, the percolation threshold is reached when 
  \[
     \nu \;=\; \frac{E[D(D\!-\!1)]}{E[D]} \;\approx\;1,
  \] 
  where \(D\) is the inter‐warehouse degree.  Thus the optimal threshold roughly corresponds to the point where each warehouse (community) has on average one “surplus” connection, i.e. \(E[D(D-1)]/E[D] =1\).  In hierarchical models the same principle holds on the *community level*: one chooses the threshold on the number (or fraction) of inter‐community connections so that the overall network is just at critical connectivity.  In effect, the first- and second-moments of the inter‐warehouse degree distribution determine the threshold; large variance raises the critical threshold.  

- **Community (warehouse) scale**.  The **sizes of the communities (warehouses)** are crucial.  Van der Hofstad et al. show that when communities (warehouses) are large – on the order of \(N^{2/3}\) or more in an \(N\)-node network – they begin to dominate connectivity.  Below that scale, the network behaves like an ordinary configuration model; above it, the community hubs (warehouses) *dictate* the phase‐transition.  In other words, the threshold shifts depending on whether each warehouse can itself become a “bottleneck” for connectivity.  As Hofstad et al. note, if communities are larger than \(N^{2/3}\), they “start to alter the critical structures” and can *entirely change the macroscopic picture* ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304414918300218#:~:text=critical%20graph%20behavior,entirely%20change%20the%20macroscopic%20picture)).  Thus the optimal threshold must account for community size – for smaller communities you use the usual critical point, but for very large communities you may need a higher threshold (more connections) to connect them.  

- **Cost–benefit tradeoff (service vs. cost)**.  In real supply‐chain terms, each warehouse node has fixed costs and variable distribution costs.  The threshold effectively balances **demand coverage** against **inventory/transport costs**.  For example, when apportioning inventory across many candidate warehouses, systems typically weigh the projected customer demand (by region), shipping distance/costs, and warehouse capacities ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11775931B2/en#:~:text=,expected%20customer%20demand%20is%20lower)).  Sending more volume to a given warehouse increases service level in its region but also ties up more inventory there.  Accordingly, the “optimal” threshold of stock to commit at a warehouse is where putting an additional unit in inventory yields diminishing returns in service relative to its holding+shipping cost.  In hierarchical models this is implicitly captured by the network’s link / node costs: a higher threshold (opening more hubs) spreads inventory out (better coverage) but incurs higher overhead. 

- **Supply/demand distribution**.  The spatial or topological distribution of demand influences the threshold.  If customer demand is highly concentrated, a lower threshold (fewer hubs) may suffice.  If demand is very dispersed, more warehouses (higher threshold) are needed to keep delivery distances (or times) low.  In terms of the hierarchical graph, this means the threshold depends on how demand “loads” the network – effectively on the degree to which different warehouse nodes need to serve far‐flung subtrees.  

- **Performance constraints**.  Often there are service‐level requirements (e.g. a certain percentile delivery time) that force a minimum number of warehouse nodes.  The threshold is then set by those requirements.  For instance, if a model requires 95% of customers be served within two hops (warehouses), the threshold must be high enough that the giant component (of “covered” customers) meets that criterion.   

In summary, **no single magic number** fixes the threshold; it is determined by the interplay of network connectivity and practical costs.  Analytically, in a random (hierarchical) graph the threshold coincides with the critical point for a giant component – i.e. when the expected number of second‐generation neighbors per warehouse is about one ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304414918300218#:~:text=critical%20graph%20behavior,entirely%20change%20the%20macroscopic%20picture)).  Equivalently, one tunes the threshold so that the *effective inter‐warehouse degree* just reaches the percolation threshold.  In real supply-chain hierarchies, this corresponds to choosing a threshold such that incremental distribution costs equal incremental service benefits (demand coverage, speed, etc.).  In practice, managers or algorithms compute this by balancing average shipping cost versus delivery time – effectively using demand forecasts, storage costs, and transport costs to place the break‐even point.  As a rule of thumb, the threshold is higher (more hubs) when communities or demand dispersal are larger, and lower when they are smaller.

In applied logistics, similar thresholds are set by comparing customer demand distribution and inventory costs ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11775931B2/en#:~:text=,expected%20customer%20demand%20is%20lower)).
