The dominant effect of a CTE‐mismatch in a metal‐matrix composite (MMC) is the build‐up of large cyclic thermal stresses at the particle/matrix interface.  Each heating/cooling cycle produces incompatible strains between the phases, so that, for example, a stiff particle (low CTE) embedded in a softer metal (high CTE) “pushes” or “pulls” on the matrix.  Analytical models show that this mismatch generates a strong local stress field around the inclusion: the highest shear and tangential stresses occur at the particle–matrix boundary and decay to near zero within only a few particle radii  ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=In%20Figure%206%2C%20the%20dependence,p)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=Let%20us%20determine%20the%20maximum,decreases%20and%20becomes%20vanishingly%20small)).  In fact the peak thermal stress is roughly proportional to Δα·ΔT·E (where Δα is the CTE difference), so even moderate temperature swings produce stresses comparable to or exceeding the matrix yield strength ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=Let%20us%20determine%20the%20maximum,decreases%20and%20becomes%20vanishingly%20small)).  Matvienko *et al.* explicitly note that mismatch stresses “may be higher than the yield strength of the matrix” and are sufficient to induce debonding and crack propagation from the particle surface ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)).  Likewise, full‐field strain measurements on model composites confirm that large CTE differences concentrate strain at inclusions, exactly where cracks later develop.

Because these thermal stresses are concentrated at the interface, they directly drive interface cracking under thermal cycling.  Experimentally it is observed that fatigue cracks almost invariably nucleate and grow along the reinforcement/matrix boundary in MMCs subjected to thermal cycles  ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=interface%20of%20particle%20and%20through,Distributions%20of%20the)).  For example, in a SiC‐particle/Aluminium alloy composite cycled from RT to ~250 °C, cracks did not cut through the particles but propagated along the particle–matrix interface ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=interface%20of%20particle%20and%20through,Distributions%20of%20the)).  The authors report that micro‐cracks formed repeatedly at the particle–matrix interface during cycling, and these coalesced with the main crack front to accelerate crack growth ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=interface%20of%20particle%20and%20through,Distributions%20of%20the)).  Similarly, Rist *et al.* (2017) note that large CTE differences (~5× between SiC and Al ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=The%20Table%203%20%20shows,between%20the%20matrix%20and%20reinforced))) produce residual stresses so high that “thermal cracks… appear easily in the interface” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)).  In other words, the mismatch itself taints the interface with microcracks within just tens or hundreds of cycles.  Scanning‐electron studies of Al/SiC MMCs after thermal fatigue show extensive interfacial debonding and voiding, leading to a clear “interface degradation” and loss of composite strength ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0266353896001728#:~:text=of%20residual%20stress%2C%20and%20consequently%2C,via%20melt%20infiltration%20with%20the)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)).  In short, a CTE mismatch is _the_ mechanism by which thermal cycles create and propagate cracks at the interface.

The stress‐concentration field from CTE mismatches also evolves with cycling.  On each cycle the elastic/plastic mismatch strains produce residual plasticity (“ratcheting”) around the particles, gradually raising local dislocation density and stress concentrations ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=The%20thermal%20stresses%20may%20be,investigations%20of%20the%20elastic%20and)).  Thermal modelling shows that the stress amplitude scales with temperature range and CTE difference ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=An%20analysis%20of%20the%20effect,stresses%20is%20proportional%20to%20%CE%94T)), so high‐Δα systems (e.g. Al/SiC, Al/Al2O3) accumulate much larger interfacial stress than low‐Δα pairs.  Over many cycles this leads to shakedown and residual crack growth: after each cycle the interfacial crack extends slightly, then the next cycle propagates it further.  Several authors emphasize that thermal mismatch stresses create effectively an “oversized” particle in terms of stress‐field influence ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=case%20of%20thermal%20deformation%20of,Orowan%20mechanisms%20are%20much%20lower)) – the particle seems larger because the high‐stress zone around it causes plasticity beyond its geometric size.  This magnifies stress concentration at the interface and thus the driving force for crack growth. 

In summary, the CTE‐mismatch drives the bulk of the thermal‐fatigue damage in MMCs.  The stress fields it produces at the interface are by far the dominant source of cyclic strain energy.  Where the phases have very different CTE’s, almost all early thermal fatigue cracking originates at the interface ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=interface%20of%20particle%20and%20through,Distributions%20of%20the)).  If the mismatch is small, thermal cracking is much less severe, but in most metal‐matrix composites (e.g. Al or Mg matrices with ceramic reinforcements) the difference is large and the resulting interfacial stresses routinely exceed material strength.  Thus mismatch‐induced thermal stress is the primary driver of progressive interface cracking and stress concentration under thermal cycling ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)).  Any attempt to predict or mitigate thermal‐fatigue life must account for these mismatch fields – they essentially set the pace of crack initiation and growth in the composite. 

**Key References:** Continuum and microscopy studies all confirm that CTE mismatch creates local stresses at the interface that exceed yield and cause cracks ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)).  For example, Matvienko *et al.* (2021) show analytically that high‐Δα particles generate a localized stress field (shear peaking at the interface) sufficient to debond or crack the matrix ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=thermally%20induced%20stresses%20in%20addition,32%2C33)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/11/2/279#:~:text=Let%20us%20determine%20the%20maximum,decreases%20and%20becomes%20vanishingly%20small)).  Likewise, Rist *et al.* (2017) observe experimentally that in SiC/Al composites a 5× CTE difference induces thermal microcracks right at the particle–matrix interfaces ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=The%20Table%203%20%20shows,between%20the%20matrix%20and%20reinforced)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211379717301006#:~:text=structure%20,induced%20residual%20stresses)).  These and other studies indicate that the thermal expansion mismatch is essentially the main cause of interface cracking and the resulting stress concentrations during thermal cycling.
