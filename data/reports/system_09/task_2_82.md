# Local lane-change disturbances and cascading bottlenecks

When a vehicle changes lanes it creates a **local perturbation** (a sudden speed/gap change) in the flow.  Trailing vehicles must brake or adjust, causing a brief slowdown around the lane-change.  In dense traffic these disturbances do not disappear – they form *oscillations* or shockwaves.  Researchers have shown that “a large number of lane changes lead to oscillations in traffic flow and a capacity drop, making the segment a bottleneck” ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856423000721#:~:text=successfully%2C%20which%20in%20turn%20cause,as%20optimizing%20lane%20markings%20and)).  In other words, each lane‐change acts like a small bottleneck, reducing the local throughput.

These small oscillations **propagate and amplify upstream**.  As vehicles pile up, the slowdown spreads backward through the flow.  Once an oscillation forms, it “can amplify upstream and ultimately result in [a] capacity-drop” at that location ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856423000721#:~:text=trigger%20oscillations%20in%20traffic%20flow,cannot%20be%20observed%20at%20the)).  In practice this means the flow through that point remains permanently lower – the bottleneck is activated and throughput falls.  Thus a single lane-change shock can trigger a *persistent* reduction in capacity.

Under heavy traffic, drivers react to these slowdowns by changing lanes themselves.  This often leads to a *chain reaction* of lane changes: one driver’s lane change forces another to change lanes or brake, and so on.  Studies of real merge bottlenecks observed this back-to-back lane-changing behavior (a “lane changing chain reaction”) causing “rapid breakdown at merge bottlenecks” ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1288366#:~:text=with%20ILC%2C%20the%20LCCR%20pose,models%20to%20describe%20LCCR%20behavior)).  In effect, each perturbation spawns more perturbations.  As a result, multiple localized bottlenecks form in succession – a cascading effect of reduced capacity spots along the highway.

- **Local perturbation:** A lane change forces nearby cars to adjust speed or gaps.  This creates an oscillation in traffic density/flow around the changer.  
- **Propagation:** These oscillations travel backward through the stream (i.e. upstream) and grow in congested conditions ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856423000721#:~:text=trigger%20oscillations%20in%20traffic%20flow,cannot%20be%20observed%20at%20the)).  Downstream flow may remain high, but upstream flow drops – a capacity drop occurs.  
- **Cascade:** Each new slowdown triggers further lane changes or braking by other drivers.  Empirical studies show such chain-reaction lane changing causes rapid breakdowns at merges ([trid.trb.org](https://trid.trb.org/View/1288366#:~:text=with%20ILC%2C%20the%20LCCR%20pose,models%20to%20describe%20LCCR%20behavior)).  Many small slowdowns accumulate into successive bottlenecks, amplifying delays.  

In summary, *each lane change is a small “disturbance” that can seed a bottleneck*.  When traffic is near capacity, even minor lane-change perturbations spawn upstream shockwaves that reduce throughput.  Multiple interacting lane changes then propagate these bottlenecks backward, causing a cascade of reduced-capacity spots along the highway ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856423000721#:~:text=successfully%2C%20which%20in%20turn%20cause,as%20optimizing%20lane%20markings%20and)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856423000721#:~:text=trigger%20oscillations%20in%20traffic%20flow,cannot%20be%20observed%20at%20the)).
