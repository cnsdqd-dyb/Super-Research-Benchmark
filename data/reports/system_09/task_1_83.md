The “time‐decay” or **temporal discount** applied to past reviews is not universal but set by design and data‐driven factors.  In practice, most systems *do* down‐weight older ratings, typically via a continuous decay (e.g. exponential) or by using a cutoff window.  The exact shape (how fast weights fall off) is chosen based on both algorithmic design and domain needs.  For example, many recommender systems weight each review by a function like \(e^{-t/\tau}\) (an exponential decay with a chosen half‐life \(τ\)) or a logistic/damping function, so that very old ratings contribute almost zero ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2011005157A1/en#:~:text=,interval%20after%20which%20the%20distance)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=The%20weighting%20functions%20penalized%20the,105)).  In one patent formulation, each rating’s value “decays with a predetermined half-life … to reflect the change of a user’s preferences over time” ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2011005157A1/en#:~:text=,interval%20after%20which%20the%20distance)).  In other approaches, systems simply ignore reviews older than a cutoff (a fixed “window”) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=With%20the%20window,the%20algorithm%20with%20the%20lowest)). 

Several factors drive how that discount function is set:

- **Product/item dynamics (concept drift).**  If the product or service changes quickly (e.g. software updates, fast fashion, or raging trends), old reviews become stale sooner.  A fast (short) half‐life is chosen so older ratings fade quickly.  For stable items (e.g. classic books), the decay can be much slower.  In effect, the chosen “forgetting rate” is tuned to match the actual rate of change in user preferences or item quality ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2011005157A1/en#:~:text=,interval%20after%20which%20the%20distance)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=The%20weighting%20functions%20penalized%20the,105)).  Recommender literature notes that selecting an appropriate decay rate is critical: if chosen too large, the system “will be less responsive to changes,” but if too small it gives undue weight to outdated feedback ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=approach%20is%20the%20choice%20of,each%20time%20the%20window%20moves)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=The%20weighting%20functions%20penalized%20the,105)).

- **User behavior & recency effects.**  Empirical studies show users treat recent reviews as more relevant to near-term decisions.  For example, recent reviews tend to influence immediate purchase choices more strongly than old reviews ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435914000311#:~:text=Conventional%20wisdom%20suggests%20that%20the,shifting%20consumer%20preferences%20increases%20when)).  A system may reflect this by giving newer ratings more weight.  (Conversely, some research finds old reviews can shape long‐term perceptions, so the exact discounting can even be task‐dependent.)  In any case, the discount function is partly determined by how quickly one believes consumers “forget” a review’s impact.

- **Rating volume and noise.**  Systems often balance new vs. historical data to smooth out noise. A rapid stream of new reviews might justify heavier emphasis on recency (to track trends), while sparse ratings mean retaining older contributions longer.  Weighting older reviews too much can make the aggregate rating slow to reflect improvement or decline; too little weight can make it volatile.  The decay curve is typically calibrated (manually or via learning) to achieve the right balance.

- **Algorithmic implementation.**  Some platforms (as one Amazon forum post notes) use complex machine‐learning models to combine review scores and time, rather than a fixed formula.  Others hard‐code a decay.  Commonly used mathematical functions include exponential decay, logistic curves, or simple linear/interpolated weights across time slices ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=The%20weighting%20functions%20penalized%20the,105)).  For instance, a simple heuristic might split reviews into “recent” and “old” groups and weight them (as in one example, giving  “newest” reviews ~30% weight, oldest only ~20%) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/%40furkannakdagg/rating-product-sorting-reviews-in-amazon-e25a0fbbc7df#:~:text=,Based%20Weighted%20Average%3A%20%25.4f)).

- **Business/policy considerations.**  Finally, system designers may enforce recency by policy (e.g. “expire” very old reviews or automatically reduce their visibility) if they want ratings to reflect current product state.  Some also de-weight extreme old reviews to mitigate the impact of outdated promotions or one-time flukes. 

In summary, there is no single universal function – instead, the temporal discount is a design choice informed by how quickly past ratings lose relevance.  Most often one uses a decaying weight (exponential or similar) with a tunable time constant or half‐life that reflects the expected “shelf‐life” of a review ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2011005157A1/en#:~:text=,interval%20after%20which%20the%20distance)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=The%20weighting%20functions%20penalized%20the,105)).  This constant is set by considering factors like item stability, user expectation of freshness, and the underlying prediction model (window vs. decay).

In practice, designers choose the decay rate so that old reviews gradually “forget” in line with observed concept drift ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/7/2204/xml#:~:text=The%20weighting%20functions%20penalized%20the,105)) ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2011005157A1/en#:~:text=,interval%20after%20which%20the%20distance)).
