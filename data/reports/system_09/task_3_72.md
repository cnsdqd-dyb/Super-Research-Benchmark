# Reward Gradients in Point-Based Incentives 

Point-based reward systems typically map accumulated points to payouts via a chosen “gradient” or conversion curve.  In practice, designers use several common shapes for this gradient.  A **piece-rate (linear)** scheme gives a fixed rate of reward per point (e.g. 5 points per task, with every 10 points redeemable for \$1).  This yields a flat, uniform gradient – each additional point is worth the same reward ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=defines%20piece,be%20redeemed%20for%201%20Yuan)).  By contrast, a **goal-gradient (convex)** design makes points increasingly valuable as one nears a threshold.  In such schemes the more points a person has, the larger the reward each new point earns ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=tournament%20point%20rewards%2C%20piece,be%20rewarded%20to%20the%20top)).  (This leverages the “goal-gradient” effect: subjects become more motivated as they approach a target ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=tournament%20point%20rewards%2C%20piece,be%20rewarded%20to%20the%20top)).)  An **exponential/tournament** scheme is even steeper or discontinuous: only the highest point-earners get big prizes (for example, paying \$500/\$300/\$100 to the top 3 scorers) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=%28e.g.%2C%20Refs.%20,to%20the%20number%20of%20tasks)).  In effect, this creates a highly skewed gradient—little or nothing for average performers, and large jumps for the top.

These gradient shapes have predictable effects.  A flat (uniform) gradient maximizes participation but yields little per-person effort.  For example, one simulation found a constant reward-per-task rule gave the *highest* participation rate (“review ratio”) yet drove individual effort nearly to zero: agents all did the minimum since the reward was the same regardless of quality ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=The%20uniform%20mechanism%20displayed%20the,than%20writing%20a%20quality%20review)).  A linear (proportional) gradient demands only moderate effort to earn most of the reward ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=This%20is%20because%20with%20the,by%20investing%20moderate%20amount%20of)), so agents tend to settle at middling effort.  By contrast, a convex/exponential gradient discourages low-effort participation—many will drop out early—but those who remain invest much more.  In the same simulation, switching to an exponential reward rule caused participation to fall (review ratio dropped from ~91% to ~65%) even while the average effort of remaining participants rose ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=Compared%20to%20the%20proportional%20mechanism%E2%80%99s,a%20significant%20drop%20to%2065)). 

In summary, point-incentive schemes are designed by choosing the reward-vs-points curve.  Flat (uniform) or piece-rate curves give constant marginal reward per point, boosting entry but not effort ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=defines%20piece,be%20redeemed%20for%201%20Yuan)) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=The%20uniform%20mechanism%20displayed%20the,than%20writing%20a%20quality%20review)).  Proportional (linear) curves strike a middle ground (reward grows steadily with points) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=This%20is%20because%20with%20the,by%20investing%20moderate%20amount%20of)).  Steeper or “goal-gradient” curves make each additional point worth more as one approaches a goal, strongly motivating high performers ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=tournament%20point%20rewards%2C%20piece,be%20rewarded%20to%20the%20top)).  Tournament‐style or sharply convex curves put large rewards at the top end only ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=%28e.g.%2C%20Refs.%20,to%20the%20number%20of%20tasks)).  The chosen gradient thus depends on whether the designer wants broad participation (favoring flat/linear) or high-quality/high-effort output (favoring rising/steep gradients) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=The%20uniform%20mechanism%20displayed%20the,than%20writing%20a%20quality%20review)) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=Compared%20to%20the%20proportional%20mechanism%E2%80%99s,a%20significant%20drop%20to%2065)).

**Sources:**  Studies of loyalty‐point and crowdsourcing incentives describe these types of schemes.  For instance, Sun *et al.*  (2023) define “goal-gradient” point rewards (larger rewards as points accumulate) versus “piece-rate” (fixed points per action) and tournament payoffs ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=tournament%20point%20rewards%2C%20piece,to%20the%20number%20of%20tasks)) ([www.sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038012123003002#:~:text=defines%20piece,be%20redeemed%20for%201%20Yuan)).  Simulations confirm that flat versus exponential gradients produce the expected trade-offs in participation versus effort ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=The%20uniform%20mechanism%20displayed%20the,than%20writing%20a%20quality%20review)) ([medium.com](https://medium.com/decon-lab/simulation-of-incentive-design-6ac9d08c6728#:~:text=Compared%20to%20the%20proportional%20mechanism%E2%80%99s,a%20significant%20drop%20to%2065)).
