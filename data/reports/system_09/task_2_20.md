# WDM for on‐chip interconnects

Wavelength‐division multiplexing (WDM) on silicon photonic links can **multiply data throughput** roughly in proportion to the number of wavelengths used. In principle one waveguide can carry \(N\) independent channels, giving ~\(N\)× the single‐wavelength bandwidth ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=scale%20integration%2C%20the%20single,chip%20networks)).  In practice, modern silicon photonic WDM demonstrators have already shown multi‐Gb/s per wavelength. For example, an 8‐channel WDM link at 10 Gb/s per channel (80 Gb/s aggregate) was realized in a hybrid 130 nm/40 nm integrated module ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=prototype%20supported%20eight%20tunable%20WDM,solid%20state%20photonic%20modules)). Models and scaled projections suggest much higher rates: by carefully choosing channel count, spacing and data rate one can approach bandwidth densities of order 700 Gb/s per mm² and total throughputs in the **10+ Tb/s** range on a single multi‐chip module ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=faster,type%20of%20MCM%20transceiver%20module)). In short, WDM can increase aggregate link bandwidth by one to two orders of magnitude over a single channel. 

Similarly, WDM can improve **energy efficiency** (bits/J) by amortizing overhead (e.g. laser and driver costs) across many parallel streams.  Demonstrated WDM transceivers already achieve energies on the order of 1–a few pJ/bit per channel ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=prototype%20supported%20eight%20tunable%20WDM,solid%20state%20photonic%20modules)). (In the cited 8×10 Gb/s demonstrator, on‐chip energy was ≈1.3 pJ/bit per channel excluding the laser power ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=prototype%20supported%20eight%20tunable%20WDM,solid%20state%20photonic%20modules)).)  In principle continued device innovation (high-Q microcavities, zero‐loss waveguides, etc.) could push on-chip interconnect energy toward the **fJ/bit** regime ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=low%20latency%20according%20to%20some,OSNR%29%20attenuation%2C%20and%20network)), far below what dense electrical wires can achieve.  

## Crosstalk limits on scaling

The ultimate gains from WDM are **bounded by crosstalk**. Every additional wavelength introduces optical leakage and nonlinear mixing, which erode the signal‐to‐noise ratio.  In silicon photonics, key crosstalk sources include imperfect ring‐resonator filters (finite isolation) and four-wave‐mixing (FWM) between channels.  For example, studies report that as rings’ bandwidth (i.e. lower Q) is increased to support higher data rates or tighter channel packing, **optical crosstalk grows substantially** ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=In%20order%20to%20scale%20up,of%20wavelengths%20can%20result%20in)).  Likewise, analytic models and simulations of WDM NoCs show that accumulated linear and FWM crosstalk “produces a prodigious proportion of nonlinear noise” ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=low%20latency%20according%20to%20some,OSNR%29%20attenuation%2C%20and%20network)) that **degrades OSNR and ultimately limits network scalability**.  One analysis found that in a 4×4 on‐chip torus network, crosstalk noise could drive the OSNR below workable thresholds and cause signal distortion, thereby capping performance ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=match%20at%20L159%20evaluate%20communication,network%20is%204%20%C3%97%204)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=low%20latency%20according%20to%20some,OSNR%29%20attenuation%2C%20and%20network)). In short, when too many wavelengths are packed closely, the error rate and loss from crosstalk climb steeply, so the incremental bandwidth per extra channel falls off.

In engineering practice, designers mitigate crosstalk with spacing and filtering, but this imposes trade‐offs.  For instance, a carefully engineered 8-wavelength silicon link used relatively coarse spacing and high-isolation resonators to keep inter‐channel crosstalk penalties under ~0.5 dB ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=Both%20electrical%20and%20optical%20crosstalk,15%5D.%20To%20minimize%20the)).  In that design each neighboring channel was ≥18 dB down, causing only about a 0.15 dB error penalty ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=Both%20electrical%20and%20optical%20crosstalk,15%5D.%20To%20minimize%20the)).  This shows that *for small channel counts*, WDM can be made nearly transparent. However, the larger the WDM mux, the harder it gets to maintain such isolation without sacrificing throughput.  Eventually the noise floor dictates an optimal number of wavelengths.  
 
## Net impact

In summary, WDM *can* dramatically raise on-chip interconnect bandwidth (roughly ×N for N wavelengths) and thus the overall bits/J efficiency by paralleling many data streams.  State-of-the-art demonstrations (∼8–16 channels at 10–25 Gb/s each) already achieve aggregate throughputs in the 10^1–10^2 Gb/s per link range with on-chip energies of order 1–10 pJ/bit ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=prototype%20supported%20eight%20tunable%20WDM,solid%20state%20photonic%20modules)).  Scaling beyond that (to hundreds of Gb/s or Tb/s per link) is theoretically possible ([opg.optica.org](https://opg.optica.org/oe/viewmedia.cfm?html=true&seq=0&uri=oe-23-10-13172#:~:text=faster,type%20of%20MCM%20transceiver%20module)), but only up to the point where inter‐channel crosstalk becomes intolerable.  In practice, channel counts will be chosen to balance bandwidth vs. signal integrity.  With careful design, WDM‐enabled networks can be one or two orders of magnitude higher‐bandwidth (and comparably more energy‐efficient) than electrical NoCs, but **crosstalk noise sets a ceiling** on how many wavelengths can be practically used ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=low%20latency%20according%20to%20some,OSNR%29%20attenuation%2C%20and%20network)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=match%20at%20L159%20evaluate%20communication,network%20is%204%20%C3%97%204)). 

**Answer in brief:** WDM can in principle multiply on-chip link bandwidth by the number of wavelengths and slash energy‐per‐bit far below electrical limits, but in silicon photonics the need for adequate inter-channel isolation means only a moderate number of wavelengths (typically ≲10–20) is feasible with low error.  Rigorous analyses show that beyond that range crosstalk noise (linear and nonlinear) causes OSNR collapse ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=low%20latency%20according%20to%20some,OSNR%29%20attenuation%2C%20and%20network)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-666X/11/11/985#:~:text=match%20at%20L159%20evaluate%20communication,network%20is%204%20%C3%97%204)).  Thus WDM boosts bandwidth and bits/J by orders of magnitude **up to** the point set by crosstalk, after which additional channels give diminishing returns.
