Yes.  In practice, as a supply network gains more nodes, tiers and interconnections, it becomes **rapidly harder** to place inventory in a way that guards against all disruptions.  Each new warehouse or distribution channel adds exponentially many new demand/supply scenarios and coupling effects.  In other words, the system’s uncertainty and state‐space explode combinatorially, so that each additional link or echelon creates **more than linear** fragility.  Numerous authors have noted that modern supply chains face “growing challenges due to … increased network complexity and inter-dependency” ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11213#:~:text=Today%27s%20global%20supply%20chains%20face,Furthermore)).  In fact, recent research decomposes the cost of complexity into an intrinsic penalty: complex networks incur a built-in performance cost (“intrinsic cost of structural complexity”) plus extra costs if that complexity is ignored ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01585#:~:text=process,impact%20of%20those%20approaches%20is)).  In short, complexity adds hidden penalties that can quickly overwhelm the benefits of added inventory buffers.  

- **Combinatorial scenario growth:**  Every new link/node introduces new demand and disruption paths.  This rapidly multiplies the number of worst-case scenarios an inventory plan must cover.  In turn, robust policies must hedge against many more joint failures, so service levels deteriorate faster than linearly.  (This is akin to the “curse of dimensionality” in multi-echelon inventory problems.)  
- **Amplified risk propagation:**  Complex networks tend to amplify demand variability (a well-known “bullwhip” effect).  For example, a small demand shock at the end customer can cascade upstream through many nodes.  The more tiers you have, the larger the amplification – which is typically nonlinear.  In practice, simply adding distribution points without addressing these dynamics can **increase** stockouts or overages disproportionately.  
- **Coordination/overhead costs:**  More interdependencies also mean more coordination lag and forecast mismatch.  These introduce new failure points (late orders, stockouts, inflated safety stocks), which compound rather than add.  Indeed, studies show that without careful redesign, “network restructuring efforts” in a complex supply chain often fail to reduce total costs ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01585#:~:text=evaluated%20through%20extensive%20numerical%20experiments,impact%20on%20the%20companys%20profitability)) – implying complexity itself drags on performance.  

Taken together, these effects imply that inventory-positioning robustness **does decline nonlinearly** as network complexity grows.  Initial increases in complexity (e.g. adding a second warehouse) might only slightly hurt performance, but beyond a threshold each extra node or route tends to introduce disproportionately large uncertainty and cost.  This is why leading researchers model complexity-driven costs explicitly ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01585#:~:text=process,impact%20of%20those%20approaches%20is)) and why advanced supply-chain optimizers (e.g. robust or stochastic models ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11213#:~:text=Today%27s%20global%20supply%20chains%20face,Furthermore))) are needed even in moderately sized networks.  In summary, the decline in robustness with complexity is generally convex/accelerating: the larger and more interlinked the network, the steeper the drop in performance and robustness ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11213#:~:text=Today%27s%20global%20supply%20chains%20face,Furthermore)) ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01585#:~:text=process,impact%20of%20those%20approaches%20is)).  

**Answer:** Yes – in complex supply networks, adding links or tiers generally makes robust inventory planning much harder in a nonlinear way. Each extra layer magnifies risks and hidden costs, so robustness typically falls off faster than just linearly as complexity grows ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11213#:~:text=Today%27s%20global%20supply%20chains%20face,Furthermore)) ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01585#:~:text=process,impact%20of%20those%20approaches%20is)).
