# Balancing Sensitivity, Specificity, and Cost-Effectiveness in Heat Stroke Warning Models: A Comprehensive Analysis of Meteorological Thresholds and Emergency Visit Data

Heat stroke warning models represent a critical public health tool that must navigate inherent trade-offs between operational efficiency and health protection. The fundamental challenge lies in designing systems that maximize the detection of genuine heat-related health threats while minimizing false alarms and associated costs, all while grounding these decisions in meteorological data that can be reliably forecasted days in advance. This report examines how contemporary heat stroke warning models achieve this balance through sophisticated integration of sensitivity and specificity metrics with cost-effectiveness frameworks, leveraging both emergency department surveillance data and advanced meteorological indicators to inform evidence-based threshold selection across diverse geographic and demographic contexts.

## Machine Learning Approaches to Heatstroke Prediction with Performance Metrics

Machine learning has emerged as a powerful methodology for improving heatstroke prediction accuracy, particularly through models that integrate multiple clinical and environmental variables while explicitly managing the tension between false positive and false negative rates. Recent research demonstrates that gradient boosting machine (GBM) models significantly outperform traditional approaches, achieving area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values of 0.971 in training datasets and 0.836 in testing datasets.[1] These performance levels reflect substantially improved discrimination compared to traditional acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE)-II scoring systems, suggesting that machine learning approaches can achieve clinically meaningful improvements in heatstroke risk stratification. The predictive superiority of machine learning models becomes particularly apparent when examining area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR) metrics, where developed models demonstrated significantly higher performance than reference standards.[24]

The selection of optimal machine learning architectures involves careful consideration of multiple competing performance metrics that reflect different operational priorities. When comparing nine distinct machine learning algorithms applied to identical heatstroke datasets, the gradient boosting machine approach demonstrated the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.971 in training data, substantially exceeding k-nearest neighbors (KNN) at 0.867, neural networks (NN) at 0.710, multilayer perceptrons (MLP) at 0.772, support vector machines (SVM) at 0.672, Gaussian processes (GP) at 0.702, naive Bayes (NB) at 0.770, extreme gradient boosting (XGB) at 0.789, and logistic regression (LR) at 0.594.[1] The calibration characteristics of these models prove equally important for clinical utility, with the GBM model exhibiting a calibration intercept of 0.15 (95% CI: -0.17 to 0.48) and calibration slope of 1.23 (95% CI: 0.86–1.60), indicating good concordance between observed and predicted probabilities. Importantly, these performance metrics translate into decision curve analysis demonstrating substantial net benefits for clinical decision-making, confirming that superior discrimination translates into practical clinical utility when models are applied within heat-health early warning systems.

The identification of specific biomarkers as key predictors reveals important insights into the physiological mechanisms underlying heatstroke mortality and guides the prioritization of clinical measurements for rapid risk assessment. Through SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis applied to the best-performing gradient boosting machine model, creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) emerged as the most impactful variable, with other essential predictors including mean arterial pressure (MAP), d-dimer, lactate, thrombin time (TT), and core temperature.[1] These findings align with emerging understanding that heatstroke pathophysiology involves multiple organ dysfunction, with coagulation abnormalities (reflected in d-dimer and thrombin time), muscle injury (CK-MB), renal dysfunction (creatinine), and metabolic derangement (lactate) all contributing substantially to mortality risk. The identification of these key predictors enables clinical teams to focus measurement efforts on variables with the highest predictive impact, potentially supporting more rapid risk assessment in resource-constrained settings.

Model performance validation across independent test datasets confirms the generalizability of machine learning approaches, with external validation demonstrating maintained discrimination while revealing the importance of calibration assessment. A separate heatstroke mortality prediction model based on Fisher discriminant analysis achieved diagnostic sensitivity of 95%, specificity of 83%, and AUROC of 0.937 on internal validation, with external validation results showing 85.71% accuracy for predicting survival and 88.89% accuracy for predicting mortality.[4] These validation results suggest that appropriately designed machine learning models can maintain strong predictive performance when deployed on new datasets, though periodic recalibration may be necessary to maintain optimal clinical performance over time as population characteristics and heatstroke epidemiology evolve.

## Decision Curve Analysis and Net Benefit Framework

The translation of discrimination metrics into clinical utility requires moving beyond traditional accuracy measures to explicitly evaluate how different operating points on the receiver operating characteristic curve affect the balance between benefits and harms for populations exposed to heat warning systems. Decision curve analysis provides a framework for this evaluation by calculating net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, defined as the minimum probability of heat-related illness at which further intervention would be warranted.[19][22] The fundamental insight underlying decision curve analysis is that the optimal classification threshold depends on the consequences of false positive and false negative classifications, which inherently reflects clinical context and stakeholder values. Net benefit is calculated as the number of true positives minus the number of false positives multiplied by the odds at the threshold probability, expressed mathematically as net benefit = sensitivity × prevalence – (1 – specificity) × (1 – prevalence) × w, where w represents the odds at the threshold probability.[19] This formulation explicitly quantifies the tradeoff that societies must make between detecting genuine heat-related health threats (sensitivity) and generating potentially costly false alarms (false positives).

Practical application of decision curve analysis to heat-related illness prediction reveals that different warning system designs achieve optimal performance across different threshold ranges, with implications for calibrating alert criteria. An XGBoost model incorporating class rebalancing through the Random Over-Sampling Examples (ROSE) algorithm achieved comparable area under the curve (0.895) to unbalanced approaches while substantially improving sensitivity (0.771 compared to 0.681), reflecting a deliberate choice to prioritize detection of true heat-related illness cases even at the cost of increased false positives.[9][32] The decision curve for this model revealed consistent superiority across a broad range of high-risk thresholds (0.1–0.5) compared with "treat-all" or "treat-none" strategies, confirming that the proposed model yields a superior tradeoff between true-positive detection and false-positive costs. This example illustrates a central principle of cost-effective heat warning system design: in many public health contexts, the costs of missing a potentially severe heat-related illness substantially exceed the costs of generating a false alarm, justifying operating points that prioritize sensitivity over specificity.

The practical implementation of decision curve analysis requires careful specification of the threshold probabilities that define clinically meaningful operating points and agreement on the implicit costs and benefits of different types of errors. Investigators often identify multiple candidate operating points, such as the Youden index (which maximizes sensitivity plus specificity minus one), the Euclidean distance (which identifies the point on the ROC curve closest to perfect sensitivity and specificity), or application-specific thresholds derived from clinical judgment or regulatory guidance.[16][18][22] For heat-related illness prediction, a sensitivity-prioritized threshold ensuring recall ≥ 0.75 represents one reasonable operating point where the system deliberately accepts increased false positives to minimize missed heat-related illness cases.[9] Alternatively, when computational or response resources are limited, higher specificity thresholds might be chosen to focus interventions on cases with highest predicted probability of serious heat-related illness. The flexibility of decision curve analysis allows stakeholders to evaluate system performance across this range and make explicit tradeoffs reflecting their context-specific priorities.

## Threshold Selection Using Epidemiological Methods and Emergency Visit Data

The selection of appropriate temperature thresholds for heat warning systems represents a fundamental challenge that bridges meteorology and epidemiology, requiring systematic methods that translate temperature exposure into predicted health outcomes. Traditional epidemiological approaches identify heat alert thresholds by examining the relationship between daily temperature (or other meteorological indices) and health outcomes such as emergency department visits, hospital admissions, or mortality, searching for the temperature point at which health impacts show a statistically significant and substantially elevated increase above background levels.[2][7][15][42][57] These approaches differ fundamentally from heat-health relationships, which describe the overall statistical relationship between temperature and health outcomes across the entire temperature range; heat warning thresholds instead identify the specific temperature at which public health authorities should initiate alert and response procedures. A threshold selected too low will result in frequent alerts that may cause public and political fatigue, rendering warnings ineffective, while a threshold selected too high will miss genuine heat health threats and delay protective responses.[7][28][57]

The modified generalized additive model (GAM) approach with risk ratio increment (RRI) comparison provides a systematic methodology for threshold selection that explicitly considers the magnitude of health risk enhancement at different temperature candidates. This approach begins by confirming linear patterns of health outcomes above certain cutpoints of heat indicators using standard generalized additive models incorporating Poisson distribution for count data and flexible smooth functions for temperature-health relationships.[2][7][15][57] Once linear relationships are confirmed above specific temperature thresholds, the method compares relative risk (RR) values at different threshold candidates against a reference temperature (typically the lowest candidate, such as 30°C), calculating risk ratio increments as [e^βθ - e^βa] × 100% where βθ represents the damage coefficient at threshold candidate θ and βa represents the reference coefficient.[7][15][57] The threshold with the maximum risk ratio increment is identified as potentially optimal, provided that the corresponding model estimate achieves statistical significance and that the resulting warning frequency is operationally feasible. This systematic approach has identified wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) at 33.0°C as an appropriate threshold for Taiwan, with maximum risk ratio increments of 242% for heat-related emergency visits, 73% for heat-related hospital admissions, and 9% for all-cause mortality.[42][57]

The consideration of lag effects in threshold selection reflects the temporal dynamics of heat-health associations, where health impacts may manifest over multiple days following heat exposure rather than on the day of maximum temperature. Distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNM) have become increasingly important for characterizing these temporal patterns, demonstrating that heat-related emergency department visits typically exhibit maximal association with same-day and previous-day temperature (lag 0–1 structure), while all-cause mortality shows stronger associations with cumulative temperature exposure over longer periods (lag 0–25 days).[31][50] For heat warning systems, understanding these lag structures is critical because it determines how far in advance predictions must be made to enable effective public health response. When heat-related illness shows a maximum lag of 0–1 days, meteorological forecasts must accurately predict temperatures 1–2 days in advance, which is typically feasible with modern weather forecasting. In contrast, when health effects show longer lags, either forecasts must extend further into the future (with correspondingly increased uncertainty) or the warning system must operate on less predictable signals.

Emergency department surveillance data from national syndromic surveillance systems provides near-real-time information on heat-related illness that can be leveraged for both threshold validation and adaptive adjustment of warning systems based on observed health impacts. The National Syndromic Surveillance Program (NSSP), which captures emergency department data from hundreds of facilities across the United States, has documented that during the 2023 warm season, daily heat-related illness emergency department visit rates peaked in several regions and remained elevated for prolonged durations.[3][6][21][55] The rate of heat-related illness emergency department visits during May–September 2023 averaged 97 per 100,000 all-cause emergency department visits across months May, June, and September, but increased dramatically to 303 per 100,000 in July and August.[3][21] This substantial seasonal variation underscores the importance of seasonal adjustment in threshold selection; temperature thresholds appropriate for July may be inappropriately high for May or September, when population acclimatization to heat is incomplete and susceptibility remains elevated.[28][50] Some advanced heat warning systems now employ monthly variations in temperature thresholds to account for these seasonal changes in heat-health relationships, improving sensitivity in early and late heat season while reducing alert frequency during peak adaptation periods.

## Meteorological Indicators and Their Comparative Effectiveness

The choice of meteorological indicator substantially influences both the discrimination ability of heat warning systems and the threshold values that define alert criteria, requiring deliberate consideration of the tradeoffs between different measurement approaches. The three primary meteorological indicators used in modern heat warning systems include the heat index (also called apparent temperature), which combines temperature and relative humidity to estimate how hot the air feels to the human body in shaded conditions; wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), which incorporates temperature, humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation to estimate heat stress on the body in direct sunlight conditions; and the universal thermal climate index, which attempts to incorporate additional environmental factors including radiation and urban geometry.[5][8][28] Each indicator has distinct strengths and limitations that make it suitable for different applications and audiences. The heat index represents the National Weather Service's flagship heat stress indicator and is generally easy for the public to understand, being calculated for shady areas and familiar to most people through long-term media usage.[8] However, the heat index does not account for solar radiation, direct sunlight exposure, or wind speed, potentially underestimating the stress experienced by outdoor workers, athletes, or individuals in hot vehicles.

Wet-bulb globe temperature provides improved heat stress assessment by explicitly incorporating solar radiation and accounting for conditions in direct sunlight, making it particularly valuable for assessing occupational heat stress and outdoor activity safety.[8][11] The WBGT index is widely used by military agencies, OSHA, many universities, and numerous nations as a guide to managing workload in direct sunlight and preventing occupational heat-related illness.[8] Importantly, research comparing WBGT against both temperature and apparent temperature has demonstrated that WBGT shows the most obvious increasing trends for heat-related health outcomes (emergency visits, hospital admissions, and mortality) in population studies, suggesting superior predictive validity for population-level heat-health relationships.[2][7][42][57] For threshold selection specifically, WBGT has demonstrated maximum risk ratio increments of 242% for heat-related emergency visits compared to more modest increments for temperature or apparent temperature, suggesting that WBGT-based thresholds may enable more sensitive detection of impending heat-related illness surges. However, WBGT values are typically lower than heat index values for identical conditions, which may cause values to be perceived as indicating lower risk than comparable heat index numbers, potentially leading to confusion if WBGT values are provided to general public audiences without context and local guidance.

The geographic variation in appropriate heat warning thresholds reflects regional differences in population acclimatization, infrastructure, health system capacity, and socioeconomic vulnerability, necessitating locally calibrated alert criteria rather than universal national thresholds. Research comparing threshold selection approaches across multiple cities found that disagreement between methods was poorest for Chicago and Madrid, where no date appeared in the top 20 ranking of every approach, but better agreement was observed for London and Montreal, with six dates common to all approaches.[36] This geographic variation in threshold determination emphasizes the importance of developing locally specific thresholds based on community-specific heat-health relationships, population characteristics, and infrastructure capabilities. The National Weather Service HeatRisk system addresses this regional variation by calculating threat level color scales based on WBGT climatological regions, accounting for geographic variations in heat exposure and acclimatization such that the same WBGT value may trigger different alert levels in different regions.[8][12] Similarly, research examining heat-related mortality across 107 US communities found substantial heterogeneity in temperature-mortality relationships, with heat effects larger in colder communities and cold effects larger in warmer communities, consistent with the hypothesis that populations develop acclimatization to their typical climate conditions.[53] These findings have led to recommendations that heat warning thresholds be calibrated based on community-specific meteorological and health data, recognizing that a 95°F heat index in Phoenix, Arizona has fundamentally different health implications than the same temperature in Minneapolis, Minnesota due to differences in population acclimatization, air conditioning prevalence, and infrastructure design.

## Cost-Effectiveness Framework and Resource Allocation

Establishing effective heat warning systems requires moving beyond epidemiological considerations of sensitivity and specificity to explicitly model the costs and benefits of different alert strategies, recognizing that the optimal balance between alert frequency and accuracy depends on available resources and societal willingness to tolerate false alarms. The cost-effectiveness of heat early warning systems depends fundamentally on several parameters: the operational costs of maintaining the warning system and coordinating response activities; the social and economic costs of false alarms, including both direct costs (such as mobilization of emergency services) and indirect costs (such as erosion of public trust); and the societal and economic savings achieved through timely protective action that averts heat-related illness and death.[10][14][39][56] A benefit-cost analysis applied to Madrid, Spain estimated benefit-cost ratios ranging from 12 to 3700 depending on the valuation methodology applied to avoided deaths, with the choice of valuation scheme (value of statistical life versus value of life-year lost) having the largest influence on cost-effectiveness calculations.[56] These results underscore that the apparent cost-effectiveness of heat warning systems depends substantially on methodological choices regarding how to value health outcomes, which reflects deeper societal judgments about the appropriate tradeoff between lives saved and resources expended.

The operational cost of heat early warning systems must be weighed against the frequency and consequences of warnings triggered to determine whether resources would be more effectively deployed on alternative interventions. In regions where extreme heat occurs infrequently, the ongoing costs of maintaining a heat early warning system may exceed the expected savings from preventing occasional heat-related deaths, suggesting that resources might be better spent on permanent infrastructure improvements such as increasing access to air conditioning, greening/cooling urban infrastructure, or improving building insulation.[10][14][39] Conversely, in regions with frequent heat waves and large vulnerable populations, an early warning system coupled with rapid response procedures can avert a substantial number of deaths at reasonable cost. Metro Vancouver's experience with extreme heat events illustrates these dynamics: the 2009 heat wave resulted in 130 excess deaths, and subsequent analysis suggested that the costs of developing and maintaining a heat early warning system capable of preventing approximately 50% of these deaths (65 deaths prevented) would be justified by the health benefits even under conservative cost-effectiveness assumptions.[10][39] However, the same analysis noted that the costs of a warning system itself may be high relative to the benefit if that system is used solely for infrequent events, emphasizing the importance of geographic targeting and baseline risk assessment when deciding to implement heat warning systems.

Public health evaluation of heat warning system cost-effectiveness must incorporate effectiveness measures beyond simple threshold exceedance, focusing instead on actual behavioral response and health outcome reduction. The true effectiveness measure of a heat early warning system is not simply its accuracy in predicting above-threshold temperatures, but rather the degree to which issued warnings mobilize protective responses that reduce heat-related health impacts.[10][14][28][39] A warning system can achieve perfect temperature prediction accuracy yet produce minimal health benefit if the community fails to implement adequate cooling infrastructure, if vulnerable populations do not receive and act on warnings, or if healthcare systems lack surge capacity to manage increased heat-related illness. Conversely, a less accurate warning system coupled with strong community mobilization and infrastructure support might achieve greater health benefits per dollar spent. These considerations have led to recommendations that heat warning systems always be embedded within comprehensive heat action plans that include cooling centers, outreach to vulnerable populations, healthcare system preparedness, utility affordability measures, and long-term infrastructure improvements addressing the root causes of heat vulnerability.[27][28][60]

## Regional Heat Emergency Department Surveillance and Adaptation Strategies

The 2023 United States heat season provided unprecedented opportunity to study geographic variation in heat warning system performance and population vulnerability through analysis of heat-related emergency department visit rates across different Health and Human Services (HHS) regions. During May–September 2023, heat-related illness emergency department visit rates showed dramatic geographic variation, with the lowest average warm-season rate of 51 per 100,000 emergency department visits recorded by HHS Region 2 (New Jersey and New York), while the highest rate of 483 per 100,000 was reported by Region 6 (Arkansas, Louisiana, New Mexico, Oklahoma, and Texas).[3][21] Compared to Region 2 as the referent, the heat-related illness emergency department visit risks for Regions 4, 6, 7, and 9 were 1.5–2.5 times higher during 2023 than during 2018–2022, suggesting that either heat exposure exceeded typical patterns in these regions or population vulnerability increased relative to prior years.[3][21] Furthermore, in Region 6 alone, more than one-third (37%; 56 days) of the days during the warm season of 2023 had daily heat-related illness emergency department visit rates exceeding the 95th percentile of historical variation, indicating unprecedented heat-related illness burden for that region.[3][6][21]

The effectiveness of existing warning systems in predicting these observed emergency department visit surges determines whether systems require recalibration to maintain current performance levels under changing climate patterns. National Weather Service heat alert criteria in place during 2023 were designed based on historical temperature distributions, such that the same alert thresholds applied uniformly across time periods. However, the pattern of 2023 heat-related emergency department visits exceeding historical 95th percentile rates on more than one-third of summer days in Region 6 suggests that population vulnerability or heat exposure (or both) has increased substantially relative to the historical baseline used to calibrate alert thresholds. This situation raises important questions about whether fixed temperature-based thresholds remain appropriate as climate and population characteristics evolve, or whether warning systems should implement dynamic thresholds that adjust based on recent years' experience. Some researchers have advocated for periodic re-evaluation and recalibration of heat warning thresholds, such as every five years, to capture changes in population demographics, heat infrastructure, acclimatization, and baseline health status.[28][52] Implementing such adaptive threshold adjustment procedures could help heat warning systems maintain appropriate sensitivity and specificity as the underlying heat-health relationships change over time.

Regional differences in rates of heat-related emergency department visits likely reflect differential acclimatization, behavioral responses, and adaptation strategies across the country, with important implications for targeted public health intervention design. Populations in warmer regions like Texas and the Southwest have greater exposure to extreme heat over their lifetimes and may develop physiological acclimatization and behavioral adaptations (such as routinely using air conditioning) that reduce heat vulnerability relative to populations in cooler regions.[3][6] Correspondingly, the absolute temperature at which similar rates of heat-related illness occur varies systematically by region, with emergency department visit rates showing higher sensitivity to temperature increases in cooler regions. The New England Regional Heat Collaborative recognized this regional variation by lowering heat advisory thresholds for Northern New England and portions of New York, moving from traditional criteria based on heat index values of 95–99°F for 2 or more consecutive days to new criteria requiring heat index values of 95–104°F for 2 or more consecutive hours.[5] This adjustment acknowledged that residents of the Northeast are uniquely vulnerable to extreme heat because they are less physiologically adapted to extreme heat, buildings and infrastructure are not designed to counteract extreme heat (particularly with lower air conditioning prevalence than in other parts of the country), populations are older than in other parts of the country with higher rates of chronic diseases, and the health system may not have developed surge capacity for heat-related illness responses.[5][28]

## Integration of Wearable Technology and Real-Time Physiological Monitoring

Emerging wearable sensor technology offers novel opportunities to complement meteorological threshold-based warning systems by enabling real-time individual physiological monitoring and early detection of heat stress before it progresses to clinical heat-related illness requiring emergency care. Smart wristbands and sensor patches can continuously monitor heart rate, skin temperature, and estimated hydration status, identifying patterns that may precede heat illness such as combinations of rising heart rate, elevated skin temperature, and decreased activity levels.[38][41] By collecting multiple biometric data points simultaneously, wearable systems can create a holistic picture of an individual's physiological state and detect subtle deviations that suggest emerging heat stress before they reach the threshold for clinical heat-related illness requiring emergency intervention. Industrial trials of wearable heat monitoring systems in occupational settings have demonstrated that providing actionable advice ("drink X ounces of water now") was substantially more effective than simply beeping alarms, suggesting that individual physiological monitoring systems can achieve greater effectiveness when coupled with clear behavioral guidance rather than abstract risk notifications.[38]

The practical implementation of wearable-based heat monitoring systems at large events such as music festivals reveals both the potential and challenges of this technology for population-level heat health protection. When a wearable device detects threshold exceedances—for example, heart rate exceeding 140 beats per minute for over 5 minutes with simultaneously elevated temperature—the system generates real-time alerts that can be triaged by severity, with mild alerts sent to the individual suggesting self-protective behaviors while more serious alerts immediately notify medical staff with the individual's location.[38] However, the utility of such systems depends critically on solving several technical and operational challenges: building robust, low-latency networks capable of handling real-time biometric data across the entire event venue; developing alert thresholds calibrated for the specific event context (accounting for factors such as intense physical activity, alcohol consumption, and age-stratified differences in heat tolerance); managing alert fatigue through careful threshold calibration to minimize false positives that cause users to ignore warnings; and training response teams to appropriately interpret alerts and provide effective on-site intervention.[38] These examples illustrate that the most effective heat health protection systems likely combine meteorological warning systems (which operate at population and geographic scales) with individual physiological monitoring (which operates at individual and real-time scales), with each system contributing distinct and complementary information to support decision-making.

## Sensitivity-Specificity Tradeoffs in Clinical Decision-Making

The fundamental challenge of designing heat-related illness prediction models that balance sensitivity and specificity emerges clearly when examining the consequences of different classification thresholds on clinical outcomes and resource utilization. When prioritizing sensitivity to minimize missed cases of heat-related illness, machine learning models achieve recall (sensitivity) ≥ 0.75 at the cost of reduced specificity, generating more false positive alerts that may trigger unnecessary protective interventions or healthcare resource activation.[9][32] Conversely, when prioritizing specificity to minimize resource waste from false alarms, models reduce sensitivity and consequently risk missing genuine heat-related illness cases that could benefit from early intervention. The implicit cost-benefit tradeoff differs substantially depending on the specific clinical or public health context: in emergency medicine settings where the consequences of missing a true heat stroke case could be severe (including death or permanent organ damage), achieving sensitivity ≥ 0.75 may be justified even with specificity as low as 0.70–0.80. In contrast, for large-scale population warnings (where the consequences of a false alarm are primarily public inconvenience and potential erosion of trust), higher specificity thresholds that minimize alert frequency might be preferred to maintain public engagement with the warning system over time.

The relationship between prediction model discrimination (measured by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) and clinical utility (measured by decision curve net benefit) reveals that models with superior discrimination metrics do not necessarily provide greater clinical utility across all relevant operating points. Decision curve analysis directly addresses this phenomenon by plotting net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, revealing that the model achieving highest area under the curve at one threshold may be inferior to alternative models at other thresholds reflecting different implicit cost-benefit assumptions.[19][22][23] For heat-related illness prediction specifically, a model optimized for area under the curve (AUC = 0.895) with relatively balanced sensitivity (0.771) and specificity (0.892) consistently provided higher net benefit across high-risk thresholds (0.1–0.5) compared with "treat-all" or "treat-none" strategies, confirming that the model's improved discrimination translated into genuine clinical utility.[9][32] However, at very low-risk thresholds (< 0.1), treating all individuals or a "treat-none" strategy might achieve comparable net benefit, suggesting that operating point selection is critical for realizing the potential benefits of prediction models.

The practical challenge of translating decision curve analysis insights into specific alert thresholds for heat warning systems requires engagement between meteorologists, epidemiologists, public health practitioners, and community stakeholders to reach consensus on acceptable tradeoffs between sensitivity and specificity. Different communities may reasonably reach different conclusions about appropriate operating points based on their specific circumstances: a city with strong air conditioning penetration, well-developed cooling infrastructure, and robust healthcare surge capacity might accept lower alert sensitivity (higher threshold probability for issuing warnings) to minimize alert frequency and maintain public trust. In contrast, a city with substantial vulnerable populations, limited cooling resources, and constrained healthcare capacity might prioritize alert sensitivity, accepting higher false alarm rates to ensure that genuine heat threats are detected and response resources are mobilized early. Guidance recommending periodic validation and recalibration of thresholds approximately every five years reflects recognition that the appropriate operating point may shift over time as population characteristics, infrastructure, and health system capacity change.[28][52]

## Synchronization of Meteorological and Health Data for Impact-Based Warning Systems

The most effective heat warning systems achieve superior sensitivity and specificity by integrating meteorological forecasts with near-real-time health surveillance data, enabling rapid detection of when forecast temperature increases are translating into actual health impacts and allowing dynamic adjustment of response escalation. Traditional threshold-based warning systems issue predetermined alert levels once meteorological forecasts show that temperature will exceed predefined values, without confirming whether those temperature increases are actually producing elevated rates of heat-related health impacts in the community. In contrast, impact-based warning systems first predict health impacts based on meteorological forecasts, then verify those predictions by monitoring near-real-time emergency department visit data from the National Syndromic Surveillance Program to confirm whether forecast heat impacts are materializing as predicted.[3][6][55] This approach enables more efficient allocation of healthcare resources by confirming alert necessity before full emergency preparedness escalation, and provides earlier signals of unexpected heat impacts (such as unusual geographic distribution of illness or disproportionate effect on particular age groups) that might indicate unanticipated population vulnerability or environmental factors.

The CDC's Heat & Health Tracker, launched in 2021, exemplifies this integrated meteorological-epidemiological approach by providing near-real-time emergency department visit rate data for heat-related illness displayed alongside weather forecasts and heat risk classifications.[55] The tracker displays heat-related illness emergency department visit rates per 100,000 total emergency department visits by Health and Human Services region for selected days or weeks, with these data drawn from the National Syndromic Surveillance Program representing near real-time reporting from participating emergency departments. Communities using this dashboard can simultaneously view forecasted heat stress (through heat index, WBGT, or HeatRisk metrics) and observed emergency department response to current/recent heat exposure, enabling verification that forecast heat impacts are indeed producing observable health effects and identifying situations where observed illness rates exceed those typically associated with forecast temperatures (suggesting either unanticipated vulnerability or possible data quality issues). This feedback mechanism strengthens the evidence base for alert decisions and enables more efficient resource allocation by confirming the genuine health impacts of predicted heat before committing to full response activation.

## Threshold Validation and Accuracy Assessment Through Calibration Metrics

The calibration of predicted probabilities from machine learning models represents a critical step often overlooked in favor of discrimination metrics such as area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, yet calibration proves essential for the reliable operation of threshold-based alert systems. A well-calibrated model produces probability estimates where a prediction of event probability p is correct approximately 100p% of the time; for example, a model predicting 30% probability of heat-related illness should observe actual heat illness in approximately 30 of 100 cases with that prediction.[47] Poor calibration—where models predict higher or lower probabilities than observed frequencies indicate—undermines the reliability of threshold-based decision-making and can lead to inappropriate alert decisions. The Brier score, which measures the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, provides a quantitative assessment of calibration, with values closer to 0 indicating better calibration.[1][4][47] A recent machine learning model for heat-related illness prediction achieved a Brier score of 0.1261, indicating good calibration between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes and supporting its suitability for operational use in early warning systems.[9] Calibration plots that display predicted probabilities on the x-axis and observed frequencies on the y-axis provide a visual representation of model calibration, with perfectly calibrated models falling along a 45-degree diagonal line and miscalibrated models deviating systematically from this line.

Several post-hoc calibration techniques can improve model probability estimates if initial calibration proves inadequate, though these techniques must be applied carefully to avoid overfitting. Platt scaling, which applies a sigmoid transformation to raw model probabilities, provides a simple one-parameter calibration approach suitable for moderate sample sizes.[47] Isotonic regression, which applies a non-parametric isotonic (order-preserving) transformation to predicted probabilities, offers greater flexibility for more complex miscalibration patterns but risks overfitting when applied to small datasets.[47] These calibration approaches have been successfully applied to heat-related illness prediction and other medical applications, with studies demonstrating that appropriate calibration improves clinical utility even when discrimination metrics remain unchanged. The integration of calibration assessment and potential recalibration into model development pipelines represents an important step often overlooked but essential for ensuring that threshold-based warning systems operate with appropriate sensitivity and specificity characteristics when deployed in real-world settings.

## Seasonal Adjustment and Acclimatization-Based Threshold Variation

Recognition that the temperature-health relationship varies substantially by season has led to development of multi-threshold heat warning systems that adjust alert criteria based on acclimatization status and seasonal context rather than applying uniform thresholds throughout the warm season. A heat-health watch and warning system developed for Greater Montreal demonstrates this approach by obtaining distinct temperature thresholds for maximum and minimum temperature combinations that vary from (23°C and 12°C) in April to (32°C and 21°C) in July and back to (25°C and 13°C) in October.[17] This monthly variation reflects understanding that populations become progressively more acclimatized to heat as the season progresses, such that the same temperature occurring in late May produces greater health impacts than the same temperature in late July when physiological acclimatization and behavioral adaptation have developed. The system achieved excellent sensitivity (100%) with minimal false alerts (less than one per year) through this approach of matching temperature thresholds to expected seasonal acclimatization patterns.

The physiological and behavioral mechanisms underlying seasonal variation in heat-health relationships include thermoregulatory acclimatization (improvements in sweating response and cardiovascular stability with repeated heat exposure), behavioral adaptation (increased use of air conditioning, modified activity schedules, altered clothing), and changes in population baseline vulnerability as summer progresses.[28][50] Early in the heat season (May–June in the Northern Hemisphere), populations have not yet developed full acclimatization from the winter period, and some individuals may not have activated air conditioning systems or prepared protective equipment. Consequently, the same temperature occurring in early May produces higher mortality and emergency department visit rates than the same temperature in July.[28][50][53] Later in the season (September–October), as heat exposure becomes less common and populations anticipate winter, acclimatization may partially reverse and protective infrastructure (such as air conditioning) may be scaled back, again increasing vulnerability to elevated temperatures. These seasonal patterns have led researchers to recommend that public health messaging explicitly address the increased vulnerability during early season heat exposure, noting that for example 25°C (77°F) in May can be as harmful to health as 29°C (84°F) in July, and that populations should not be notified that only heat in the middle of the season poses health risks.[28][50]

## Recommendations for Future Heat Warning System Development

The scientific literature examining heat warning system design, validation, and performance has identified several key recommendations that should guide development and improvement of systems worldwide, reflecting accumulated evidence on best practices for balancing sensitivity, specificity, and cost-effectiveness. First, heat warning thresholds should be based on analysis of local population-specific heat-health relationships using epidemiological methods applied to community-specific meteorological and health outcome data, rather than adopting generic thresholds developed in other geographic regions.[28][42][57][59] Different populations, infrastructure, healthcare systems, and climate zones experience different heat-health relationships even at identical temperatures, necessitating locally calibrated thresholds. This recommendation has led many jurisdictions to invest in epidemiological capacity to analyze heat-health relationships within their regions and engage in periodic recalibration (approximately every five years) as population and infrastructure characteristics change.[28][52]

Second, alert thresholds should be based on multiple health outcome indicators rather than mortality alone, because morbidity outcomes (emergency department visits, hospital admissions) occur at lower temperatures than mortality and are thus more sensitive early warning indicators.[20][59] The use of multiple health outcomes also enables assessment of how threshold selection affects different populations; for example, a threshold that provides excellent warning of heat-related emergency department visits in elderly populations might miss heat illness in younger outdoor workers with different physiological responses to heat stress.

Third, heat warning systems should integrate meteorological forecasting with near-real-time health surveillance to confirm that forecast heat impacts are translating into observed health effects, enabling more efficient resource allocation and earlier detection of unanticipated impacts.[3][55][59] This integration is now technically feasible through systems like the National Syndromic Surveillance Program and CDC Heat & Health Tracker that provide near-real-time emergency department data.

Fourth, heat warning systems should employ multiple meteorological indicators (heat index, WBGT, HeatRisk) tailored to specific audience needs rather than relying on a single index, recognizing that outdoor workers need WBGT-based guidance, general public audiences benefit from heat index information, and decision-makers benefit from impact-based HeatRisk assessments.[8][12] Different indicators provide complementary information and are suitable for different decision-making contexts.

Fifth, decision curve analysis should be applied during heat warning system development to explicitly evaluate the clinical or public health utility of different threshold choices, moving beyond discrimination metrics to assess the actual net benefits of different alert strategies within specific contexts.[9][19][22] This analysis should engage with community stakeholders to reach consensus on acceptable tradeoffs between sensitivity and specificity reflecting local circumstances.

## Conclusion

Heat stroke warning models that successfully balance sensitivity, specificity, and cost-effectiveness must integrate multiple complementary methodologies spanning machine learning for risk prediction, epidemiological threshold identification, cost-effectiveness analysis, and near-real-time health surveillance. Contemporary evidence demonstrates that machine learning approaches substantially outperform traditional scoring systems in predicting heatstroke mortality and risk stratification when appropriately validated and calibrated. The translation of superior discrimination into genuine clinical utility requires explicit application of decision curve analysis to evaluate net benefits across ranges of threshold probabilities, recognizing that the optimal threshold depends on local context and stakeholder values. Selection of meteorological thresholds for population-level heat warning systems should be grounded in systematic epidemiological analysis of local heat-health relationships using emergency department visit data, with explicit consideration of seasonal acclimatization patterns and geographic variation in population vulnerability. The integration of meteorological forecasts with near-real-time health surveillance enables dynamic confirmation that forecast heat impacts are producing expected health effects, supporting more efficient resource allocation and earlier detection of unanticipated population impacts. Regular revalidation and recalibration of warning thresholds at appropriate intervals (approximately every five years) maintains system performance as population demographics, infrastructure capabilities, and heat-health relationships evolve over time. Ultimately, the most effective heat warning systems recognize that no single threshold or alert strategy is universally optimal, but rather that context-dependent, evidence-based threshold selection coupled with comprehensive heat action plans encompassing infrastructure, healthcare preparedness, and community outreach represents the path forward for effective adaptation to increasing heat risks in a warming world.
