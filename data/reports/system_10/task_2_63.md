# Balancing Immune Reconstruction and Infection Risk in GVHD Monitoring: A Histopathology-Based Approach

The management of graft-versus-host disease represents one of the most challenging clinical scenarios in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, requiring careful navigation between the need to suppress donor immune cell-mediated tissue damage while simultaneously maintaining sufficient immune function to prevent life-threatening infections and control underlying malignancy. When adjusting immunosuppressants based on histopathological findings, clinicians must integrate multiple layers of information including tissue-based evidence of GVHD activity, markers of immune reconstitution, infectious complications profile, and prognostic indicators specific to individual patients. This comprehensive report examines the evidence-based strategies for using histopathology as a guide in this delicate balancing act, with attention to the interdependencies among GVHD severity, immune reconstitution patterns, infection risk, and treatment modifications.

## The Complex Interplay Between GVHD, Immunosuppression, and Infection Risk

The relationship between graft-versus-host disease and infection represents a bidirectional pathological process that fundamentally complicates clinical management[2]. Patients who develop acute GVHD experience significant increases in infection risk prior to symptom onset, with the first infection occurring at a median of 26 days post-transplantation in those who eventually develop GVHD, compared to 35 days in patients without GVHD[45]. This temporal relationship suggests that immune dysregulation precedes clinically evident GVHD, indicating that patients with impending acute GVHD may already have compromised immune function even before diagnostic manifestations appear. The cascade of events following GVHD recognition creates a vicious cycle in which the need for immunosuppressive therapy to control donor T cell-mediated tissue damage paradoxically increases vulnerability to opportunistic pathogens through multiple mechanisms[2]. First, the immunosuppressive medications themselves directly impair immune reconstitution by inhibiting T cell proliferation, B cell development, and natural killer cell function. Second, GVHD itself causes immune dysregulation through both destruction of lymphoid tissues and alterations in immune cell subsets and function. Third, the inflammatory environment associated with GVHD and its therapy increases susceptibility to secondary infections[2].

The cumulative mortality attributable to acute GVHD and its associated complications remains substantial despite advances in transplantation technology, with acute GVHD significantly increasing the risk of developing both life-threatening infections (hazard ratio 1.97, 95% confidence interval 1.33–2.90) and fatal infections (hazard ratio 2.8, 95% confidence interval 1.10–7.08)[45]. These statistics underscore why histopathological assessment must be coupled with careful consideration of immune reconstitution status when determining whether to intensify, maintain, or reduce immunosuppression. The challenge becomes particularly acute when early histopathological findings show minimal disease burden, forcing clinicians to decide whether continued immunosuppression is necessary given its inherent risks[1]. Understanding this fundamental tension is essential before considering specific histopathological guidance principles.

## Histopathological Diagnosis and Its Limitations in Guiding Treatment Adjustments

Histopathology remains the gold standard for confirming GVHD diagnosis across multiple target organs, yet its role in guiding treatment adjustments is significantly limited by several inherent characteristics of the technique[1]. Histopathological assessment represents "a snapshot in time" of a complex and dynamic biologic process that reflects the duration of disease, the current intensity of immunosuppressive therapy, possible concurrent pathological processes, the anatomical location of biopsy sampling, and the quality of histological preparation[1]. This temporal limitation means that a single histopathological specimen cannot definitively determine whether GVHD is improving, stable, or worsening relative to the patient's treatment trajectory. Following immunosuppressive treatment, skin biopsies may contain a combination of residual changes to the damaged epidermis and appendages, preexistent dermal sclerosis, and a reduction or absence of active apoptosis and inflammation, making it difficult to distinguish true disease improvement from residual architectural changes[1]. Similarly, gastrointestinal biopsies taken after immunosuppressive treatment showing persistent or worsening histological changes portend poor outcomes, yet mild histopathological findings early in disease course do not reliably predict clinical response or prognosis[1].

The minimal diagnostic thresholds for GVHD have become increasingly stringent and controversial as transplant practices evolve[1]. Modern conditioning regimens, the use of reduced-intensity approaches, and newer immunosuppressive strategies have altered both the timing and severity of GVHD presentation, leading clinicians to diagnose GVHD based on minimal histological changes—particularly the presence of isolated apoptosis—in appropriate clinical contexts[1]. However, defining the exact threshold of minimal histological change sufficient for definitive GVHD diagnosis remains problematic. In gastrointestinal biopsies specifically, the injury is unevenly distributed, which increases diagnostic difficulty and creates substantial risks for false negative diagnoses when limited biopsy sampling is performed or too few serial sections are examined[1]. At least 8 to 20 serial sections should be analyzed to avoid missing infrequent apoptotic changes, yet this level of detail is not uniformly performed across all institutions[1]. These methodological variations mean that two patients with similar clinical presentations might receive different histopathological diagnoses depending on biopsy technique, specimen quality, and pathologist interpretation standards.

### The Problem of Minimal and Uncertain Histological Findings

A particularly vexing scenario arises when skin biopsies or other tissue samples show minimal features of active GVHD but remain nonspecific[4]. In a recent analysis of cutaneous GVHD, nearly all endoscopic biopsies from erythematous cutaneous GVHD showed minimal features of active GVHD, including apoptosis in the epidermal basal layer (94% of cases), vacuolar change (92.5%), and lymphocyte satellitosis (85.1%)[4]. Yet the majority of these biopsies also shared features with inflammatory skin diseases that they clinically resembled, including lichen planus-like patterns or other dermatologic conditions[4]. This overlap creates diagnostic uncertainty: does the presence of minimal apoptotic activity in a patient with a rash definitively indicate GVHD requiring intensified immunosuppression, or could the findings reflect a drug reaction, viral exanthem, or other reversible dermatologic condition? The distinction has profound implications for treatment decisions, as unnecessary intensification of immunosuppression in a patient without true GVHD would increase infection risk without therapeutic benefit, while under-recognition of true GVHD could allow progression to more severe multiorgan involvement.

When such uncertain histopathological findings are encountered, clinicians must integrate multiple additional sources of information to guide immunosuppressant adjustments. The clinical context—including timing of symptom onset relative to transplantation, distribution and character of organ involvement, response to initial interventions, and concurrent infections—becomes essential in distinguishing true GVHD from mimics[1]. Patients presenting with acute hepatitic onset after donor lymphocyte infusions or tapering of immunosuppressive medications demonstrate more active necroinflammatory activity and portal inflammation than those receiving ongoing immunosuppression, reflecting the dynamic nature of GVHD expression in different therapeutic contexts[1]. This variability underscores why static histopathological assessment alone cannot guide treatment modifications; instead, histopathology must be interpreted as one component of a comprehensive clinical assessment that includes serial clinical evaluations, laboratory markers, radiographic findings, and immune reconstitution status.

## Immune Reconstitution as a Critical Balancing Factor

The degree and kinetics of immune reconstitution fundamentally influence how aggressively immunosuppression should be maintained or tapered based on histopathological findings[20][23]. Early CD4+ T cell immune reconstitution defined as achievement of greater than 50 CD4+ T cells per microliter within 100 days after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is associated with reduced GVHD-related mortality in patients who develop moderate to severe acute GVHD[20]. This finding carries significant clinical implications: patients achieving adequate early CD4+ reconstitution have better survival prospects even when they develop GVHD, potentially allowing more cautious tapering of immunosuppression because their recovering immune systems may be better positioned to control GVHD while maintaining antimicrobial defenses[20]. In contrast, patients without adequate early CD4+ reconstitution who develop GVHD face substantially higher mortality risk and may require more aggressive and prolonged immunosuppression, despite the increased infection risk this entails[20].

The mechanisms by which adequate CD4+ immune reconstitution protects against GVHD-related mortality remain incompletely understood but likely involve multiple factors[20]. One hypothesis proposes that adequate CD4+ reconstitution reflects a more balanced overall immune reconstitution characterized by higher regulatory T cell counts and broader T cell receptor diversity, resulting in more effective homeostatic control of immune dysregulation[20]. Another mechanism may involve the induction of regulatory T cells during steroid treatment for GVHD, a process that requires an adequate pool of CD4+ T cells to occur effectively[20]. Regardless of mechanism, the practical implication is that in patients with histopathologically confirmed GVHD who also demonstrate good CD4+ reconstitution (≥50 cells/μL), more cautious immunosuppression tapering may be reasonable, whereas patients with concurrent GVHD and delayed CD4+ reconstitution require more sustained immunosuppressive therapy[20][23].

In contrast to the protective effect of early CD4+ reconstitution, delayed T cell recovery after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation—defined as CD4+ counts remaining below 200 cells per microliter—is associated with increased infection risk and poorer overall survival[23]. When histopathological findings suggest GVHD activity in patients with delayed T cell recovery, the risk-benefit calculation shifts markedly toward accepting some degree of GVHD in order to avoid further immunosuppressive intensification that would perpetuate the already-impaired immune reconstitution[23]. This represents a fundamental departure from treatment of GVHD in immunocompetent patients, where immune reconstitution is proceeding normally. In patients with delayed recovery, the threshold for accepting histologically-confirmed GVHD without escalating immunosuppression must be substantially higher, and consideration should be given to using GVHD treatments targeting specific pathways rather than broad immunosuppression that impairs overall immune function[20].

The complexity of immune reconstitution monitoring has led to development of more sophisticated biomarker-based approaches to guide decision-making. An immune reconstitution-based score incorporating CD3+CD4+ counts, natural killer cell numbers, and immunoglobulin levels at the time of chronic GVHD diagnosis independently predicted severe GVHD and overall survival[13]. Notably, higher CD3+CD4+ counts at chronic GVHD diagnosis were paradoxically associated with worse outcomes, likely reflecting the pathophysiologic role of CD4+ helper T cells in GVHD pathogenesis[13]. The implications for treatment adjustment are nuanced: patients demonstrating CD3+CD4+ counts greater than 233 cells/mm³ at GVHD onset had worse prognosis despite intuitively appearing to have better immune reconstitution, suggesting that in such patients, more aggressive immunosuppression may be justified to prevent severe disease[13].

## Biomarkers for Distinguishing Active Disease from Resolved or Inactive GVHD

Beyond classical histopathological examination, emerging biomarkers offer promise for better distinguishing active GVHD from residual or resolved disease, thereby guiding more rational decisions regarding immunosuppressant adjustments. Soluble ST2 (suppression of tumorigenicity-2), an interleukin-33 receptor, has emerged as the most extensively validated biomarker for acute GVHD and is associated with treatment-refractory disease and non-relapse mortality[30][35]. Serum ST2 levels measured at steroid treatment initiation for GVHD patients who are destined to develop steroid-refractory GVHD are typically elevated more than twofold compared to steroid-responsive patients[30]. More critically, ST2 measured at day 14 after starting steroid treatment predicted treatment failure by day 56, with the combination of ST2 and TIM3 (T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3) values further improving prediction accuracy[30]. These temporal relationships suggest that biomarker monitoring during the early treatment phase, rather than at GVHD diagnosis alone, better informs decisions about whether current immunosuppression is adequate or requires intensification[30].

For chronic GVHD, soluble BAFF (B-cell activating factor) levels appear particularly useful for guiding immunosuppressant adjustments[32][35]. In patients with skin-predominant chronic GVHD, lower BAFF levels (less than 4 ng/mL at one month of therapy) predicted clinical response or complete remission at 3 to 6 months, supporting corticosteroid tapering, whereas persistently elevated BAFF was associated with poorer outcomes[32]. This finding supports the hypothesis that biomarker-guided de-escalation of immunosuppression may be safer and more rational than arbitrary tapering schedules. CXCL9 and CXCL10 (C-X-C chemokine ligands) are elevated in chronic GVHD, with early post-transplant CXCL9 levels positively correlating with chronic GVHD severity, and this correlation appears modulated by single-nucleotide polymorphisms in chemokine receptor genes[35]. The multi-protein biomarker panel comprising ST2, CXCL9, matrix metalloproteinase-3, and osteopontin has demonstrated diagnostic and prognostic value, with the four-protein panel demonstrating area under curve of 0.86 for diagnosing GVHD at symptom onset and providing prognostic information independent of GVHD severity[34].

The key advance these biomarkers enable is dynamic monitoring rather than reliance on static histopathological snapshots. REG3α, a gastrointestinal tract-specific marker secreted by intestinal Paneth cells, is elevated at GVHD diagnosis but decreases following effective treatment, and the trajectory of REG3α changes after therapy initiation predicts treatment response more reliably than any single measurement[35]. REG3α concentrations were three-fold higher at GVHD diagnosis in patients with no response to therapy at four weeks compared to patients experiencing complete or partial response[35]. Patients responding to therapy still exhibited REG3α concentrations more than three times that of non-GVHD controls, demonstrating the importance of tracking changes in biomarker levels rather than interpreting absolute values. When adjusting immunosuppressants based on histopathology, therefore, serial biomarker measurements—particularly ST2 at two weeks of treatment for acute GVHD, or BAFF at one month for chronic GVHD—should inform whether current therapy is achieving biological goals, even if histopathological reassessment has not yet been performed[27][32].

## Organ-Specific Histopathological Findings and Treatment Implications

Different organ systems demonstrate distinct relationships between histopathological findings and clinical significance, requiring organ-specific interpretation when adjusting immunosuppressants. In gastrointestinal GVHD, the unevenness of injury distribution creates particular challenges for biopsy-based decision-making[14]. A novel activity grading system developed specifically for gastric and intestinal biopsies based on apoptotic cell frequency better stratifies patients at risk of poor outcomes than traditional grading systems[14]. This system distinguishes GVHD of minimal histologic activity (0.07–<0.25 apoptotic cells per section), mild activity (0.25–<4), moderate activity (4–<25), severe activity (≥25), and severe activity with destruction[14]. Most GVHD biopsies are low-grade—minimal or mild histologic activity—yet traditional grading systems poorly correlate with clinical manifestations and prognosis[14]. The activity-based grading system appears more useful for decision-making because mild activity changes frequently signal the need for prompt therapeutic intervention before progression to higher-grade injury with worse prognosis[14].

Hepatic GVHD presents particular challenges for histopathology-guided treatment adjustment because the degree of bile duct damage often does not correlate with clinical outcome, yet duct loss is accepted as a poor prognostic factor[59]. When hepatic biopsies show evidence of GVHD with bile duct injury, clinicians should not automatically intensify immunosuppression based solely on histological findings; instead, clinical staging based on total serum bilirubin levels should guide treatment intensity, with biopsy-confirmed hepatic GVHD in the setting of normal bilirubin scored as stage 0[59]. This distinction recognizes that histopathological findings may lag behind clinical improvement, and functional markers (bilirubin) more accurately reflect disease severity than tissue injury patterns[59]. Conversely, persistent or worsening histological changes on follow-up biopsies in patients receiving immunosuppressive treatment portend poor outcomes and should prompt treatment intensification rather than de-escalation[1].

Cutaneous GVHD demonstrates remarkable heterogeneity in histopathological patterns that can be better understood through endotyping based on predominant histological features rather than treating all cutaneous GVHD as a single entity[4]. Erythematous cutaneous chronic GVHD predominantly demonstrates lichen planus-like interface dermatitis patterns (47%), while sclerotic cutaneous GVHD typically shows lichen sclerosus and morphea-like patterns (62.5%)[4]. Importantly, despite these distinct histopathological patterns, current clinical practice treats all cutaneous GVHD subtypes with identical therapeutic approaches, missing opportunities for more targeted interventions[4]. When adjusting immunosuppressants for cutaneous GVHD, recognition of the predominant histological pattern may help clinicians select between systemic corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, targeted agents like JAK inhibitors (particularly for interface dermatitis), or anti-fibrotic approaches (for sclerotic disease), rather than applying one-size-fits-all escalation or de-escalation strategies[4].

## Clinical Decision-Making Framework for Immunosuppressant Adjustment

A rational framework for adjusting immunosuppressants based on histopathology must integrate seven key dimensions of clinical information. First, the histopathological diagnosis itself must be established with confidence, requiring adequate biopsy sampling, multiple tissue sections, and careful pathologist interpretation in clinical context[1]. Second, the temporal relationship between symptom onset and transplantation must be determined, as this affects interpretation of histopathological findings and prognosis[25]. Third, the current intensity of immunosuppression must be documented, as histopathological changes in a patient receiving high-dose corticosteroids carry different meaning than identical findings in a patient receiving minimal immunosuppression[1]. Fourth, immune reconstitution status must be assessed through CD4+ T cell counts and preferably through biomarker panels that capture multiple dimensions of immune function[13][20].

Fifth, infectious complication history must be reviewed, as patients with recent or active infections require sustained or intensified immunosuppression despite GVHD concerns to prevent life-threatening progression, whereas patients with no recent infections may tolerate immunosuppression reduction[45]. Sixth, functional organ system status should be assessed through appropriate laboratory and imaging studies, as some histopathological findings reflect resolved or inactive disease rather than active pathology requiring treatment[1]. Seventh, prognostic biomarkers should be incorporated if available, as these refine risk stratification and identify patients who might tolerate less intensive immunosuppression despite GVHD[13][30][35].

When histopathology demonstrates mild or minimal GVHD activity, maintenance of current immunosuppression rather than escalation is often appropriate if: (1) immune reconstitution is proceeding at a normal pace with CD4+ counts greater than 50 cells/μL; (2) no serious infections are present or recently resolved; (3) clinical GVHD manifestations are mild and stable; and (4) prognostic biomarkers such as ST2 are not markedly elevated or are declining with treatment[20][30]. Conversely, escalation of immunosuppression should be considered when: (1) histopathology demonstrates moderate or severe activity; (2) immune reconstitution is delayed, requiring additional immunosuppression despite increased infection risk; (3) prognostic biomarkers indicate high risk of non-relapse mortality; or (4) clinical manifestations are progressive despite current therapy[14][30].

De-escalation or tapering of immunosuppression based on histopathology should be pursued cautiously and only when: (1) serial biopsies or biomarkers demonstrate clear evidence of decreasing disease activity; (2) immune reconstitution has achieved CD4+ counts greater than 100 cells/μL; (3) no active infections are present; and (4) the patient has demonstrated stable response to current therapy for a minimum of 4-8 weeks[1][39]. Slow, gradual tapering—typically reducing corticosteroids by 10 mg every 1-2 weeks after stabilization, slowing at 20 mg/day—is preferred to abrupt cessation of immunosuppression[39]. In chronic GVHD specifically, tapering must be particularly cautious as exacerbations are frequent when steroid withdrawal is attempted[46]. Documentation that histological signs of active disease remain absent—including residual apoptotic changes in epidermis or appendages for skin, or absence of new bile duct loss for hepatic involvement—supports continued tapering if clinical improvement is maintained[1].

## Infectious Complications and Immunosuppressive Intensification

When adjusting immunosuppressants based on histopathological findings, the presence or risk of infectious complications creates a critical constraint on treatment decisions. Despite higher risk of early cytomegalovirus reactivation when using certain GVHD prophylaxis approaches such as post-transplant cyclophosphamide (Cy-post), one-year viral burden and cytomegalovirus disease risk are comparable with methotrexate-based prophylaxis when appropriate monitoring and preemptive therapy are employed[22]. This equivalence suggests that GVHD prophylaxis choice need not be driven primarily by infection prevention but can be optimized for GVHD prevention[22]. However, once GVHD develops, the additional immunosuppression required for treatment substantially increases infection risk, creating a dilemma when histopathological findings are equivocal or borderline[45].

The cumulative incidence of invasive fungal infections after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is approximately 7% at one year, with aspergillosis accounting for 75% of these infections[40]. Mortality from invasive fungal infections remains high, with one-year overall survival in infected patients only 32%[40]. These statistics underscore that immunosuppression intensity directly translates to fungal disease risk, and patients with marginal or minimal histopathological GVHD who already have delayed immune reconstitution should not have immunosuppression escalated beyond current levels, as doing so could precipitate life-threatening fungal disease. When faced with histopathology suggesting GVHD activity in a patient with recent fungal infection or substantial fungal infection risk factors (including chronic GVHD, significant immunosuppression, prior colonization with Candida or Aspergillus), continuing current immunosuppression without escalation represents a more defensible strategy than intensifying therapy based solely on tissue-based findings[40].

Bacterial infection risk, particularly from encapsulated organisms, increases substantially in patients with chronic GVHD requiring ongoing immunosuppression[41]. Prophylactic antibiotics with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole should be continued throughout treatment of chronic GVHD, and additional prophylaxis against Streptococcus pneumoniae should be administered to high-risk patients[41]. These preventive measures become even more critical when histopathological findings are prompting consideration of immunosuppression escalation, as augmented therapy will further compromise antimicrobial defense and increase breakthrough infection risk[41].

## Special Scenario: Immune Suppression Tapering and GVHD Reactivation

A particularly instructive clinical scenario occurs in patients who have achieved disease remission and are undergoing planned tapering of immunosuppression based on histopathology showing resolved GVHD. In patients who relapse after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation while still receiving immunosuppression, tapering or discontinuation of immunosuppressive therapy to induce graft-versus-tumor effects results in development or progression of GVHD in 97% of cases, yet many patients still achieve substantial anti-leukemic responses[7]. The median time to onset of acute GVHD after starting immune suppression tapering was 31 days (range 16–98 days), while median time to chronic GVHD was 57 days (range 21–486 days)[7]. Importantly, 97% of immune suppression tapering responders to malignancy developed GVHD, yet five of 34 responders (15%) died from GVHD or its complications, while six patients (18%) relapsed again after initial response[7].

This scenario demonstrates that anticipated GVHD reactivation from immunosuppression tapering does not necessarily lead to poor clinical outcomes if carefully monitored. Reinstitution of immunosuppression in patients who develop GVHD during tapering—including steroids with or without restarting low-dose sirolimus or tacrolimus—successfully improved GVHD symptoms in all surviving patients[7]. These observations suggest that planned tapering of immunosuppression based on histopathological evidence of disease resolution can be attempted, provided that very close monitoring is maintained and histopathological reassessment is performed promptly when clinical signs of GVHD reactivation appear. However, patients with delayed immune reconstitution or recent infectious complications should have immunosuppression tapered more slowly and cautiously, potentially even maintaining low-dose ongoing therapy rather than attempting complete discontinuation.

## Role of Targeted Immunotherapy in GVHD Management

Recent developments in GVHD therapeutics have expanded the treatment armamentarium beyond broad immunosuppression, offering opportunities to address specific immunological pathways while potentially reducing overall immunosuppressive toxicity. Ruxolitinib, a JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy for both steroid-refractory acute and chronic GVHD in clinical trials and now carries FDA approval[21][42]. The mechanism of action appears to involve selective inhibition of inflammatory signaling without completely abrogating immune function, potentially allowing better preservation of anti-infectious and anti-leukemic immunity[42]. When histopathology confirms moderate or severe GVHD activity, ruxolitinib or other second-line agents targeting specific pathways represent alternatives to escalating broad immunosuppression, potentially allowing stabilization or even reduction of corticosteroid doses while maintaining disease control[21][42].

Belumosudil, a ROCK2 inhibitor, exerts multiple immunomodulatory effects including inhibition of IL-17 and IFN-γ production, reduction of Th17 cells through STAT3 downregulation, and enhancement of regulatory T cells through STAT5 upregulation[42]. In chronic GVHD, belumosudil decreased collagen content in oral mucosa and reduced IL-17 production and inflammation, suggesting potential benefit for sclerotic disease phenotypes[58]. When histopathology demonstrates predominantly sclerotic chronic GVHD with fibrotic changes, targeted therapy with belumosudil might be superior to intensifying corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors, as it addresses the pro-fibrotic biology driving sclerotic manifestations[42].

Ibrutinib, a Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in chronic GVHD with sustained response in 61% of patients achieving partial or complete response at ≥20 weeks of therapy[42]. Axatilimab, a colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor–blocking antibody, achieved an overall response rate of 74% in the AGAVE-201 phase 2 trial of patients with refractory chronic GVHD, with adverse effects primarily consisting of transient laboratory abnormalities[21]. The development of these targeted agents means that when histopathology demonstrates GVHD activity in patients at high risk for infections or with delayed immune reconstitution, escalation to targeted therapy rather than increase of corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors represents a more nuanced approach to treatment adjustment that may better preserve immune function[42].

## Integration of Histopathology with Dynamic Biomarker Monitoring

The most sophisticated approach to guiding immunosuppressant adjustments based on histopathology involves integrating tissue-based findings with serial biomarker measurements that capture the dynamic trajectory of disease activity and immune response. Rather than viewing histopathology and biomarkers as competing diagnostic modalities, they should be seen as complementary tools providing different temporal and biological information. Biomarkers such as ST2 and REG3α reflect real-time immune and epithelial activation, changing daily in response to treatment efficacy or progression, while histopathology provides tissue-level architectural and cellular evidence of chronic or resolved disease[27][34][35]. When histopathology obtained on day 40 post-transplantation shows minimal apoptosis and an isolated biopsied area appears to have resolved, yet simultaneously measured ST2 levels are rising or failing to decline appropriately with steroid therapy, this discordance suggests that clinical disease activity is not being adequately controlled despite apparent histological resolution[30]. In such cases, biomarker trajectory should prompt treatment escalation or modification despite the histopathological findings.

Conversely, when histopathology shows persistent minimal changes but biomarkers such as BAFF are declining significantly and other clinical indicators suggest improvement, this biomarker-favorable trajectory can support gradual immunosuppression tapering even if histological reassessment has not yet been performed[32]. The advantage of integrating biomarkers with histopathology is that the clinician gains information about treatment efficacy at multiple timepoints rather than relying on serial biopsies that are invasive, carry procedural risks, and provide only snapshots of disease. A practical approach involves measuring key biomarkers (ST2, REG3α for acute GVHD; BAFF and CXCL9/10 for chronic GVHD) at diagnosis, at 1-2 weeks during treatment, at 4 weeks when clinical response assessment is typically performed, and then every 4 weeks during the maintenance and tapering phases[27][30][32]. Immunosuppressant adjustments can then be guided not solely by histopathological findings but by the integrated picture of tissue pathology, biomarker trajectory, immune reconstitution, and clinical response.

## Conclusion

Balancing immune reconstruction and infection risk when adjusting immunosuppressants based on histopathology in GVHD requires integration of multiple complementary sources of information rather than reliance on histopathological findings alone. Histopathology remains essential for confirming GVHD diagnosis and documenting active disease characteristics, but its limitations as a tool for guiding treatment modifications—including temporal discordance with clinical events, variability in sampling and interpretation, and inability to predict treatment response—necessitate supplementation with immune reconstitution assessment, biomarker monitoring, and clinical judgment[1][14]. Patients demonstrating adequate early CD4+ reconstitution (>50 cells/μL) who develop histologically confirmed GVHD may tolerate more cautious immunosuppression tapering and can accept some degree of ongoing disease without requiring aggressive escalation[20]. In contrast, patients with delayed immune reconstitution or recent infectious complications require more sustained immunosuppression despite histopathological findings, as the infection and mortality risks of further immune impairment outweigh the benefits of escalating GVHD therapy[45].

The emergence of biomarkers such as ST2, REG3α, and BAFF offers opportunities to dynamically monitor disease activity and treatment response between clinical visits, providing earlier warning of inadequate control or evidence of improvement to guide de-escalation decisions[27][30][32][34][35]. Organ-specific interpretation of histopathological findings—recognizing that gastrointestinal GVHD activity grade correlates with prognosis, hepatic GVHD severity is best determined by functional markers rather than histology, and cutaneous GVHD endotypes may benefit from tailored approaches—allows more refined decision-making than applying uniform treatment algorithms[4][59]. Finally, the availability of targeted immunotherapies addressing specific pathogenic pathways creates alternatives to escalating broad immunosuppression, offering potential for treating GVHD while better preserving immune reconstitution and reducing infectious complications[21][42]. Future progress in GVHD management will likely emphasize precision approaches that integrate histopathology with biomarkers, immune reconstitution status, organ-specific manifestations, and targeted therapeutics to optimize the balance between preventing donor immune-mediated tissue damage and enabling sufficient host immune recovery for antimicrobial and anti-malignancy functions.
