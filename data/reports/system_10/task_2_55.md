# Balancing Prediction Accuracy and Transparency: Evaluating SHAP Value Approximation Errors in Regulatory Compliance for Risk Models

In the evolving landscape of financial services and risk management, machine learning models have become increasingly sophisticated and powerful, yet simultaneously more opaque and difficult to understand.[3][10][23] The central challenge facing financial institutions today is whether advanced predictive models can simultaneously achieve high accuracy while maintaining the transparency required by regulators and stakeholders. This report examines whether interpretability algorithms, particularly SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), can genuinely balance these competing demands, or whether the inherent approximation errors in SHAP calculations ultimately undermine regulatory compliance efforts. By analyzing the theoretical foundations of SHAP, its practical limitations, the stability of its value estimations, and the regulatory landscape governing model explainability, this comprehensive review provides evidence that the relationship between accuracy, interpretability, and compliance is far more nuanced than a simple binary choice suggests.

## The Fundamental Accuracy-Interpretability Trade-Off in Machine Learning

The tension between model accuracy and interpretability represents one of the most persistent challenges in applied machine learning and represents a critical constraint in high-stakes domains such as credit risk assessment, fraud detection, and regulatory capital calculations.[4][6][24] Traditional statistical models, such as logistic regression, offer remarkable transparency; practitioners can examine each coefficient and readily understand how changes in input features influence predictions.[32][35] Conversely, complex machine learning ensembles—random forests, gradient boosted trees, and neural networks—frequently achieve superior predictive performance but at the cost of opacity that makes understanding their decision-making processes extraordinarily difficult.[3][6][31] This trade-off is not merely theoretical but has profound practical implications for how financial institutions develop, validate, and deploy models for critical applications.

Researchers have long recognized this fundamental tension.[6][37] A linear regression model with minimal coefficients might explain only seventy to eighty percent of variance in outcomes, whereas an ensemble of a hundred decision trees might achieve ninety to ninety-five percent accuracy.[1][32] The additional five to fifteen percentage points of predictive accuracy might translate into millions of dollars in risk reduction for a large financial institution, yet understanding why the ensemble model made a particular decision for a specific applicant becomes substantially more difficult. This creates an acute dilemma for financial institutions: deploying the simpler, more interpretable model means accepting inferior risk predictions and potentially incurring substantial losses from unidentified defaults; deploying the complex model means gaining predictive power but sacrificing the transparency needed to justify decisions to applicants, auditors, and regulators.

Recent research has begun to challenge the universality of this trade-off, however.[3][31][37] Empirical studies have demonstrated that in certain domains and for particular datasets, interpretable models can actually match or exceed the performance of more complex approaches.[3][31] The phenomenon known as the Rashomon effect—the existence of multiple distinct models achieving nearly equivalent predictive performance—suggests that many practitioners may be accepting worse explanations than necessary.[37][55] If equally accurate interpretable models exist within the solution space but practitioners simply have not invested sufficient effort in discovering them, then the apparent accuracy-interpretability trade-off may reflect an implementation gap rather than a fundamental constraint of machine learning itself. Nevertheless, for many real-world risk modeling scenarios, particularly when models must incorporate hundreds of features and capture complex non-linear relationships, simpler interpretable models genuinely cannot match the predictive performance of sophisticated ensembles.

This reality has created an urgent need for post-hoc interpretability methods that can be applied after model training to explain the decisions of already-trained black-box models.[1][4][10][23][33] Among these post-hoc approaches, SHAP has emerged as the most widely adopted method in financial services, with surveys of financial institutions in the United Kingdom showing SHAP as the most frequently used explainability technique alongside general feature importance measures.[25] SHAP promises to resolve this dilemma by allowing institutions to deploy highly accurate complex models while simultaneously providing defensible explanations for individual predictions. However, the question of whether SHAP can truly deliver on this promise—or whether approximation errors inherent in its calculation methodology ultimately undermine its utility for regulatory compliance—remains incompletely resolved.

## Understanding SHAP: Theoretical Foundations and Implementation Methodologies

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) builds upon the theoretical framework of Shapley values, a concept drawn from cooperative game theory introduced by Lloyd Shapley in 1951.[4][5][16][18] The core insight underlying Shapley values is elegantly simple: if we conceptualize machine learning predictions as outcomes of cooperative games in which each input feature represents a player, then Shapley values provide a theoretically principled way to allocate credit for the final outcome among all participants.[5][16][18] Formally, a Shapley value for a particular feature represents the average marginal contribution of that feature across all possible coalitions of features, weighted according to the size of each coalition.[5][16][18]

The mathematical formulation of Shapley values is deceptively complex, however. For a model with \(p\) features, calculating exact Shapley values requires computing the model's prediction for all \(2^p\) possible feature subsets—a computationally prohibitive task for models with even a modest number of features.[5][16] When a model incorporates fifty features, exactly computing Shapley values would require evaluating the model on more than one quadrillion feature combinations, a practical impossibility. Consequently, implementing SHAP in real-world scenarios necessitates using approximation methods that trade computational tractability for precision.[4][5][16][17]

The SHAP framework provides three primary approximation methodologies, each with distinct computational characteristics, accuracy profiles, and suitability for different model types.[16][17][46] TreeSHAP, optimized specifically for tree-based models such as XGBoost, Random Forests, and LightGBM, exploits the tree structure to dramatically reduce computational complexity from exponential to polynomial time.[16][17][46] Rather than evaluating the model on all possible feature coalitions, TreeSHAP traverses the tree paths and works with only the coalitions that would actually change predictions at each node, then carefully weights and combines these marginal contributions.[16][46] This specialization makes TreeSHAP substantially faster than more general approaches—computing SHAP values for thousands of predictions might require seconds rather than hours.[14][17][46]

KernelSHAP represents a model-agnostic approach applicable to any machine learning model, treating the model as a complete black box.[5][16][17][46] KernelSHAP operates through a perturbation-based methodology: it creates a large sample of perturbed feature combinations by randomly removing features from instances, obtains predictions from the black-box model for these perturbed combinations, and then applies a weighted linear regression to estimate each feature's contribution to the prediction.[4][5][16] The weighting scheme used in this linear regression reflects Shapley value theory and is designed to produce estimates converging to true Shapley values as the sample size approaches infinity.[5][16] However, KernelSHAP is computationally expensive; computing SHAP values for even moderate-sized datasets can consume hours or days of computation time, and the accuracy of estimates depends heavily on the number of samples drawn.[16][46]

DeepExplainer, designed for neural networks and deep learning models, leverages the differentiability of neural networks to approximate Shapley values through backpropagation methods.[16][17] DeepExplainer combines concepts from DeepLIFT (Deep Learning Important FeaTures) with Shapley value theory, integrating contributions from all neurons in the network to estimate each feature's contribution to the prediction.[16][17] The theoretical justification rests on showing that the attribution rules in DeepLIFT can be chosen to approximate Shapley values when integrated across background samples.[16] This approach typically offers substantial computational advantages compared to kernel-based methods while maintaining better accuracy than simple feature importance calculations.[16][17]

Despite their theoretical elegance and practical utility, all approximation methods for SHAP carry inherent limitations stemming from their approximative nature. KernelSHAP samples from the marginal distribution of features rather than the conditional distribution, which introduces bias when features are correlated or dependent.[5][13][46] This bias manifests as SHAP assigning non-zero values to features that genuinely have no causal influence on the model's prediction, simply because those features happen to be correlated with features that do matter.[13][44][46] TreeSHAP similarly encounters challenges with feature correlation: when features are strongly dependent, TreeSHAP may attribute importance to features that have no direct causal relationship with the target variable.[13] DeepExplainer requires careful calibration of background datasets and may produce unintuitive attributions when the neural network architecture contains complex interactions.[16]

## SHAP Stability, Approximation Errors, and Reliability in Multi-Run Scenarios

A critical issue that has received insufficient attention until recently concerns the stability of SHAP values across multiple model training runs.[2][8][27] The question is straightforward but consequential: if a practitioner retrains a model using different random seeds or slightly perturbed data while keeping the fundamental model architecture and feature set constant, will SHAP provide the same explanation and feature importance rankings? For regulatory compliance purposes, this question becomes essential, as regulators expect consistent, reproducible explanations for credit decisions.[8][27][39][42] If SHAP values vary substantially across model recalibrations, this instability could undermine the defensibility of model-based decisions in regulatory examinations or legal challenges.

Recent empirical investigation reveals a complex picture regarding SHAP stability.[2][8][27] Research examining SHAP values across one hundred independently trained models on real-world credit card default datasets found that variables with the strongest or weakest predictive power demonstrated remarkably stable SHAP rankings across different model runs.[27] For high-importance variables—those consistently identified as the most influential predictors—Kendall's coefficient of concordance exceeded 0.90, indicating near-perfect agreement in rankings.[27] Conversely, variables with middling or moderate importance levels exhibited substantially lower stability.[27] The same variable might rank tenth in one model but fifteenth in another, simply due to stochastic variation in model training or random sampling in SHAP value approximation.[27]

This variable-importance-dependent stability pattern reflects important distinctions in the sources of SHAP value variability.[2][8][27] For variables with strong predictive signal, the relationship between feature values and model predictions remains consistent across different model training runs, even if specific parameter values fluctuate; consequently, SHAP's estimation of these strong effects remains reliable.[27] For variables with weak or noisy signals, however, small variations in model parameters or in the random samples used for approximation can produce meaningfully different SHAP value estimates.[27] This means that the most problematic scenario for regulatory compliance may occur precisely when SHAP values are most uncertain—for marginal features where the question of importance is genuinely ambiguous.

A particularly concerning source of SHAP instability emerges from the stochastic nature of approximation methods themselves, independent of model variation.[2] KernelSHAP, as a sampling-based approximation method, inherently produces slightly different results across runs unless practitioners fix random seeds and maintain identical sampling procedures.[2][5][46] The more features in a model and the more careful practitioners must be about ensuring reproducibility, the greater the documentation and computational overhead required.[2] Research on explanation multiplicity demonstrates that even the choice of evaluation metric and reference baseline can substantially alter which explanations appear most important when comparing SHAP outputs from different runs or implementations.[2] When different practitioners using different tools or even different versions of the same software apply SHAP to identical data and models, they may receive meaningfully different explanations without any underlying change to the model's behavior.[2]

For regulatory compliance, this stability issue creates genuine challenges.[8][27] Credit regulations such as the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) require that creditors provide specific, consistent reasons for adverse credit actions.[39][42] If a financial institution provides an adverse action notice to a denied applicant citing "recent payment delinquencies" and "low available credit" as the principal reasons, then when the applicant requests an explanation of these reasons or challenges the decision in court, the institution must be able to defend this explanation as grounded in the model's actual decision-making process.[26][39][42] If SHAP recalculation yields meaningfully different feature importances, suggesting that "credit utilization ratio" and "length of credit history" were actually more influential than the originally stated reasons, the institution faces potential legal exposure.[8][27]

The magnitude of SHAP approximation error varies considerably across different implementation approaches and settings.[2][13][14][46] TreeSHAP generally produces more accurate estimates than KernelSHAP when applied to tree-based models, particularly when feature interactions or non-linearities characterize the underlying relationships.[14][46] Comparative benchmarking reveals that TreeSHAP consistently achieves mean absolute errors below 0.02 in probability space for boosted tree models without significant non-linearities, whereas KernelSHAP may produce errors exceeding 0.12 under similar conditions.[14] For functions with substantial non-linearities—such as thresholding model scores to generate classifications—TreeSHAP's accuracy deteriorates substantially, sometimes producing errors above 0.35, whereas alternative approaches maintain better accuracy.[14] This suggests that the reliability of SHAP values depends heavily on both the specific approximation method chosen and the particular characteristics of the model being explained.

## Regulatory Requirements and the Explainability Landscape in Financial Services

The regulatory environment governing machine learning in financial services has evolved dramatically over the past five years, creating increasingly explicit requirements for model interpretability and explainability.[7][9][10][25][33][45][48] Global regulatory bodies and national financial authorities have recognized that the growing complexity and opaqueness of AI systems used in critical financial decisions creates systemic risks that inadequate governance frameworks cannot address.[7][25][33][45] The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, the European Banking Authority, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency in the United States, and financial authorities across multiple jurisdictions have incorporated explainability requirements into their model risk management frameworks.[7][9][25][33][45]

The Basel Core Principles (BCPs), specifically BCP 15 concerning risk management processes, require that banks using risk models comply with supervisory standards on model use, including independent validation and testing.[7][25] More recent guidance from the Basel Committee explicitly addresses internal models used for capital calculations, requiring that model methodology, assumptions, and limitations be documented transparently and that models be conceptually sound and fit for their intended purposes.[7][25][33] While these requirements do not explicitly mandate post-hoc explainability methods like SHAP, they implicitly require that institutions understand their models sufficiently to explain them and that model behavior remains consistent with regulatory expectations.[7][25][33]

Increasingly, however, regulatory guidance has become more explicit about explainability requirements.[10][25][33][45][48] The European Central Bank's revised guide to internal models, published in July 2025, includes an entirely new section addressing machine learning techniques in internal models.[48] This guidance specifies that models using machine learning must be "adequately explainable" and that their performance must "justify their complexity."[48] The implicit message is that regulators will not permit financial institutions to deploy opaque machine learning models simply because they achieve superior predictive accuracy; instead, institutions must demonstrate that the accuracy gains are sufficiently substantial to warrant the reduction in interpretability, and they must maintain robust explainability frameworks.[48]

In credit risk specifically, the regulatory expectations for explainability reflect consumer protection objectives as much as prudential concerns.[10][22][26][39][42] The FCRA and ECOA impose specific requirements for adverse action notices when creditors deny credit applications.[26][39][42] Regulation B implementing the ECOA requires that creditors provide specific, clear, and accurate reasons for adverse credit actions within thirty days of the decision.[39][42] General statements such as "credit score below policy" or "outside of risk tolerance" are explicitly considered insufficient; regulators have established lists of acceptable reasons and expect creditors to select reasons that accurately describe the principal factors that influenced the decision.[39][42] The Fair Credit Reporting Act additionally requires that if an applicant was denied credit based on negative information in their credit report, the denial notice must disclose the specific adverse information that was a factor in the decision, including credit scores when applicable.[39][42]

These regulatory requirements create a genuine tension with sophisticated machine learning models.[10][22][26][29] If a neural network or ensemble model makes a credit decision by integrating signals from dozens or hundreds of features in complex non-linear ways, what constitutes "the principal reasons" for the decision? If SHAP analysis identifies the top three features as accounting for the majority of the marginal prediction changes, can the institution cite only those three features in the adverse action notice, even if the model fundamentally depends on interactions among numerous additional features? If different implementations of SHAP or different approximation methods yield different feature importance rankings, which ranking should guide the adverse action notice?[8][27] These questions remain partially unresolved in regulatory practice, creating genuine uncertainty about compliance requirements.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) has begun to address these questions through guidance and enforcement actions.[26][29] CFPB guidance emphasizes that even when using complex, uninterpretable models, financial institutions must still be able to provide reasonable and specific adverse action notices.[26][29] The CFPB has further noted that post-hoc explainability techniques like SHAP still require validation, which may not be possible with less interpretable models.[26][29] This creates a regulatory paradox: institutions are expected to use model-agnostic explainability techniques to make complex models interpretable, yet the regulators themselves acknowledge that such techniques may be insufficiently validated and reliable. Regulators have indicated, however, that they are willing to permit institutions to use complex models with post-hoc explainability if institutions implement sufficient validation and governance controls.[26][29][45]

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Federal Reserve have provided related guidance emphasizing that banks using sophisticated models for critical activities must demonstrate effective governance and risk management controls.[9][33] Institutions must understand the models they use, validate their assumptions and performance, monitor them for degradation or bias, and be prepared to defend model-based decisions to examiners and regulators.[9][33] While this guidance does not mandate specific explainability methods, it clearly establishes that regulatory acceptance of complex opaque models depends on robust governance frameworks that include explainability components.[9][33]

## Do SHAP Approximation Errors Actually Hinder Regulatory Compliance?

The evidence on this question is more nuanced than either unqualified endorsement or dismissal of SHAP would suggest.[8][27] The relationship between SHAP approximation error and regulatory compliance outcomes depends critically on the specific error magnitude, the variable affected by the error, the regulatory context, and the governance controls surrounding model implementation.[8][27]

For variables with strong and consistent predictive importance, SHAP approximation errors appear tolerable and unlikely to create meaningful compliance risk.[8][27] If a credit model's primary default indicators are payment history, debt-to-income ratio, and credit utilization, these factors will reliably emerge from SHAP analysis as the top drivers of decisions across different model runs and different approximation implementations.[27] Adverse action notices citing these factors will reflect genuine drivers of credit decisions and withstand regulatory scrutiny and consumer challenge. The approximation errors in SHAP calculations—typically accounting for a few percentage points of variation in feature importance estimates—do not change the fundamental conclusion about which factors matter most.[27]

For variables with weak or moderate predictive importance, however, SHAP approximation errors become more consequential.[2][8][27] Consider a scenario where a credit model incorporates a variable capturing the applicant's tenure in their current employment. Across multiple model runs, SHAP might rank this variable as the eighth most important factor in one instance, the fourteenth most important in another, and the fifth most important in a third instance, with the variation stemming partly from genuine model variation but partly from SHAP's approximation uncertainty.[27] If regulatory guidance or institutional policy specifies that adverse action notices should cite the top five factors, then the inconsistency in SHAP rankings creates genuinely problematic compliance implications. In one scenario, employment tenure would be included in the adverse action notice; in another, it would be omitted. A consumer denied credit could claim that the institution is providing inconsistent explanations for its decisions, raising fairness and transparency concerns.[8][27]

The regulatory compliance implications are particularly acute when SHAP approximation errors cross decision thresholds.[8] Suppose an institution implements a policy stating that any factor must show SHAP importance exceeding a certain threshold to be cited in adverse action notices, to ensure that only genuinely material factors are communicated to consumers. If SHAP approximation error causes a particular factor to sometimes exceed the threshold and sometimes fall below it, the inconsistency creates compliance risk. Moreover, if approximation error causes a factor to be ranked above a factor that genuinely is more important according to the true Shapley values, this misranking could yield adverse action notices that fail to accurately describe the decision drivers, violating the FCRA's requirement for specific and accurate disclosure.[8][26][27][39]

Evidence suggests, however, that for most practical credit risk applications, SHAP approximation errors do not systematically cause factor rankings to cross critical thresholds.[8][27][46] The top three to five factors consistently retain their positions across different runs and implementations, with approximation error primarily affecting the relative ranking of factors already considered marginal.[27] This observation suggests that institutions focusing adverse action notices on top-tier factors—those truly driving variation in credit decisions—can likely navigate SHAP's limitations without creating unreasonable compliance risk.[8][27][39]

Nevertheless, several specific scenarios create genuine compliance concern despite this general observation. High-dimensional credit models incorporating hundreds of features, particularly those incorporating alternative data sources such as transaction data, social media activity, or behavioral patterns, may experience more substantial SHAP approximation uncertainty than traditional models based on fifty or fewer well-established credit features.[15][46] When model complexity increases dramatically, the number of feature coalitions and interaction effects multiplies, making SHAP's approximations less precise. The applicability of SHAP may also be questionable for certain model architectures; deep neural networks for credit decisioning may produce SHAP explanations that fail to capture genuinely complex interactions within the network.[14][16] Additionally, when SHAP methodology generates explanations for classification outcomes (whether an applicant is approved or denied) rather than for underlying score predictions, accuracy deteriorates substantially for certain model types, with errors potentially exceeding 0.35 in some scenarios.[14]

Most critically, the regulatory compliance question cannot be resolved through technical analysis of SHAP's approximation accuracy alone. Compliance ultimately depends on whether explanations provided to regulators and consumers are truthful, reasonable, and genuinely reflect how the model arrived at specific decisions. SHAP provides one tool for generating such explanations, but approximation error is only one dimension of the compliance question.[8][26][27] Even if SHAP values were calculated with perfect accuracy, compliance risks could still emerge if institutions misinterpret SHAP outputs, apply them inconsistently, or fail to validate that SHAP explanations genuinely reflect the model's decision-making process for specific individuals.[8][26]

## Hybrid Approaches and Alternative Pathways to Balancing Accuracy and Interpretability

Recognizing the limitations of both purely interpretable models and purely post-hoc explainability methods, financial institutions and researchers have increasingly explored hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of both paradigms.[41][22][37] These hybrid strategies do not attempt to achieve perfect interpretability of complex models but instead pursue a more pragmatic balance between accuracy, interpretability, and computational feasibility tailored to specific regulatory and business contexts.[41][22][37]

One influential hybrid approach involves ensemble methods that combine inherently interpretable models with high-performance black-box models.[41][11] A credit decision might be made by a logistic regression model capturing linear relationships and traditional credit factors, with predictions then adjusted or refined by a secondary ensemble model that captures non-linear patterns and interactions among factors.[41] Alternatively, institutions might route certain application types through interpretable models (for which explanations are straightforward) while allowing only specific high-value or complex applications to proceed through black-box models requiring post-hoc explainability.[41] This tiered approach maintains interpretability for the majority of decisions while enabling predictive improvements for edge cases where model complexity is genuinely justified.[41]

Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) and similar architectures represent another hybrid pathway.[3][37][56] GAMs decompose complex functions into additive components where each feature contributes independently to the final prediction, maintaining interpretability while accommodating non-linear relationships.[3][37][56] Tree-based GAMs can capture non-linear patterns through component functions while maintaining the additive structure that ensures transparency.[3][37][56] Recent research demonstrates that carefully constructed GAMs often achieve predictive performance approaching complex ensembles while preserving interpretability that facilitates regulatory compliance and consumer communication.[3][37][56]

Sparse decision trees and rule-based models constrained for simplicity represent another approach to the hybrid space.[3][37] Rather than using standard decision tree algorithms that may grow to hundreds of nodes, institutions can apply interpretability constraints that limit tree depth or the number of splits, forcing models to capture the most essential patterns while remaining human-understandable.[3][37] These constrained models sacrifice some predictive accuracy relative to unconstrained alternatives but often achieve accuracy superior to linear models while maintaining substantially better interpretability.[3][37]

A more recent development involves attention mechanisms and inherently interpretable neural architectures that process features additively through non-interacting pathways, preventing the dense entanglement of weights that makes traditional neural networks opaque.[59] These architectures maintain the representational power of neural networks while constraining them to additive structures that remain interpretable.[59] Initial research suggests these approaches can achieve performance comparable to traditional neural networks while providing far superior explainability.[59]

From a regulatory perspective, hybrid approaches offer important advantages.[22][37][41][48] Rather than adopting a binary choice between interpretability and accuracy, institutions can implement approaches that acknowledge context-specific tradeoffs. A credit risk model for prime mortgages might prioritize interpretability, recognizing that the vast majority of applications follow predictable patterns that simple models can capture adequately.[41] Conversely, a fraud detection model operating in an environment with rapidly evolving tactics might accept greater complexity and rely more heavily on post-hoc explainability, recognizing that fraud patterns are genuinely complex and simple models would miss critical signals.[41] This context-dependent flexibility allows institutions to navigate regulatory requirements while maintaining business effectiveness.[41][48]

## SHAP Performance Across Model Types and Data Regimes

The reliability of SHAP values and the practical feasibility of compliance depend substantially on which model types institutions deploy for risk decisions.[1][14][17][27][46] The SHAP framework performs quite differently across models, creating meaningfully different compliance implications depending on model choice.

For tree-based ensemble models (XGBoost, Random Forests, LightGBM, CatBoost), TreeSHAP provides efficient, reasonably accurate explanations.[14][17][27][46] TreeSHAP's exploitation of tree structure enables fast computation and generally reliable feature importance estimates for standard credit applications.[1][14][27][46] Studies on credit card default prediction show that TreeSHAP produces stable feature rankings across model runs with Kendall's concordance exceeding 0.85 for top-tier factors.[27] From a compliance perspective, TreeSHAP explanations of tree-based model decisions can be defensible, with the caveat that institutions must remain aware of correlations among features that may create misleading attributions.[1][13][27]

For linear and logistic regression models, SHAP values are theoretically straightforward—each feature's SHAP value equals its coefficient multiplied by the deviation of that feature from its mean, adjusted for the feature's expected value in the dataset.[4][18][56] This creates a fundamental insight: SHAP values for simple linear models simply formalize what practitioners have always understood about those models.[18][56] In this context, SHAP adds little incremental value beyond standard regression analysis, yet it provides a consistent mathematical framework that unifies linear and complex model explanations.[18][56] For compliance purposes, linear model explanations via SHAP remain straightforward and defensible.[1][18]

For neural networks and deep learning models, DeepExplainer and related approaches introduce greater uncertainty.[14][16][17][46] DeepExplainer's approximation quality depends on the network architecture, the background dataset used for calibration, and the complexity of feature interactions within the network.[14][16] For relatively simple neural networks with limited hidden layers and moderate feature counts, DeepExplainer explanations can provide useful insights.[16][17] However, for large deep networks with hundreds of parameters, the explanations become increasingly approximate and potentially misleading.[14][16][46] Moreover, when deep learning models incorporate embedding layers for categorical variables or attention mechanisms for sequence data, applying SHAP becomes substantially more complex and less reliable.[14][16]

High-dimensional settings—models incorporating hundreds or thousands of features—create particular challenges for SHAP implementation.[5][15][46] As dimensionality increases, the number of possible feature coalitions explodes exponentially, making both exact Shapley value calculation and reliable approximation increasingly difficult.[5][15][46] KernelSHAP becomes computationally prohibitive; generating stable estimates might require hundreds of thousands of model evaluations.[5][15][46] TreeSHAP benefits from its polynomial-time computation but still requires careful specification of background datasets and may produce less stable results as feature interactions multiply.[5][15][46] For institutions incorporating alternative data or high-dimensional behavioral features into credit models, SHAP's practical applicability becomes questionable.[5][15]

When institutions have tested SHAP explanations against regulatory examinations, the experiences have been generally positive, provided institutions maintain proper governance and documentation.[26][29] Regulators have shown willingness to accept SHAP-based explanations of complex model decisions, recognizing that these methods represent current best practices for post-hoc explainability.[10][25][29] However, regulators have also emphasized that institutions must understand the limitations of SHAP, validate its outputs against actual decision processes, and maintain documentation explaining their approach to using SHAP for adverse action notices.[10][26][29][45]

Conversely, where regulatory concerns have arisen, they have typically focused not on SHAP approximation error per se, but on broader governance failures: institutions unable to articulate which factors drove specific credit denials, institutions citing inconsistent factors across similar applicants, or institutions providing explanations that contradicted actual model behavior.[26][29][39] SHAP can enable proper governance if used correctly but cannot remedy fundamental governance failures.[26][29]

## The Role of SHAP in Fraud Detection and Anti-Money Laundering

Beyond credit risk, SHAP applications in fraud detection and anti-money laundering reveal additional dimensions of the accuracy-interpretability tradeoff and compliance implications.[20][28][30] Fraud detection models face distinctive challenges that shape how SHAP can contribute to compliance and operational effectiveness.

Financial crime detection inherently involves asymmetric information and rapidly evolving tactics.[20][28] Fraudsters continuously develop new techniques to evade detection systems, meaning that models trained on historical fraud patterns may face declining effectiveness as tactics change.[20][28] This creates pressure toward model complexity and sophistication; simpler models struggle to capture the diversity of fraud methods while remaining robust to changing patterns.[20][28] Consequently, fraud detection models tend toward ensemble methods and sophisticated architectures that make genuine interpretability challenging.[20][28]

SHAP provides particular value in fraud contexts by enabling threat analysts to understand why specific transactions triggered fraud alerts, facilitating more efficient investigation of suspected fraud.[20][30] When a transaction receives a high fraud score, SHAP can identify which features (unusual geographic location, atypical transaction amount, velocity anomaly) contributed most to the alert, allowing analysts to focus investigation accordingly.[20][30] This operational insight improves fraud investigation effectiveness and allows human reviewers to calibrate their confidence in the system's judgment.[20][30]

However, fraud detection also illustrates SHAP's limitations in high-stakes contexts.[20][28] If an innocent customer is wrongly flagged for fraud and their transaction is blocked, they may demand explanation for why the system suspected fraud activity.[20][28] SHAP can identify which factors triggered the alert, but if those factors reflect ordinary transactions that happen to fall into categories the model associates with fraud based on training data imbalances or correlational patterns, the explanation may fail to satisfy the customer or satisfy regulatory fairness requirements.[20][28] Moreover, detailed explanations of fraud detection reasoning could itself create risks if such explanations reveal the detection system's logic, enabling fraudsters to develop evasion techniques.[20]

From a regulatory perspective, fraud detection does not face the same explicit requirements for specific adverse action notices as credit decisions.[20][28] However, regulators increasingly expect that financial institutions can explain their fraud detection decisions, identify potential sources of bias in these systems, and demonstrate that fraud prevention is not creating disparate impact across protected groups.[10][20][28] SHAP provides one tool for these regulatory requirements but does not eliminate the need for human judgment and oversight in fraud case review.[10][20][28]

## Fairness, Bias, and the Limitations of Post-Hoc Explainability

An underappreciated aspect of SHAP's role in regulatory compliance concerns its relationship to algorithmic fairness and bias detection.[2][5][13][44][57][58] SHAP can identify which features contribute most to predictions, but this capability has genuine limitations for identifying or eliminating bias in credit decisions.

SHAP's foundational assumption of feature independence creates particular problems in the fairness context.[13][44] When credit features correlate with protected attributes (such as location correlating with race or ethnicity), SHAP may assign importance to seemingly neutral features that actually encode protected group information.[13][44] For example, if a model learns that applicants from certain ZIP codes are statistically associated with higher default rates due to historical patterns in lending, SHAP would attribute importance to the ZIP code feature.[13][44] This is not false attribution per se—ZIP code genuinely correlates with model outputs—but it masks the reality that ZIP code's predictive power may entirely reflect historical lending bias rather than genuine default risk.[13][44]

Recent work on Causal SHAP attempts to address this limitation by incorporating causal relationships among features into SHAP calculations.[44] Causal SHAP identifies which features have direct causal relationships with outcomes versus merely correlational relationships, allowing practitioners to distinguish genuine causation from confounding.[44] However, Causal SHAP remains computationally expensive and requires prior specification of causal structures, which may not be reliably known in credit contexts where underlying causal mechanisms are complex and partially unobserved.[44]

The relationship between SHAP-based explanations and fairness also highlights a broader limitation: post-hoc explainability, however sophisticated, cannot ensure that models are fair or non-discriminatory.[2][57] A model could generate perfectly stable and well-justified SHAP explanations while still producing disparate impact across protected groups if underlying training data or features themselves encode discrimination.[2][57] Regulators have increasingly recognized this limitation; federal banking regulators, the CFPB, and international authorities now emphasize that explainability must be combined with direct fairness testing, bias monitoring, and potentially model redesign to address structural fairness problems.[10][26][28][29]

## The Path Forward: Integrating Explainability into Model Governance Frameworks

The evidence suggests that SHAP approximation errors, while real and sometimes consequential, do not categorically prevent regulatory compliance if institutions implement sophisticated governance frameworks that acknowledge SHAP's limitations and deploy supplementary controls.[8][10][26][27][29]

Effective governance frameworks for AI explainability in credit risk typically incorporate several key elements.[26][29][48] First, institutions establish clear policies specifying which aspects of credit decisions must be explained to regulators and applicants, recognizing that comprehensive explanation of every feature contribution may be impractical while targeted explanation of principal factors remains feasible.[26][29] Second, institutions validate that SHAP outputs reflect actual decision logic by testing explanations against model behavior on test data, ensuring that factors identified as important actually predict outcomes in expected directions.[26][29] Third, institutions document their SHAP implementation methodology, including the approximation algorithm selected, background datasets used, and any approximations or simplifications undertaken for computational feasibility.[26][29][48] Fourth, institutions implement ongoing monitoring to identify substantial shifts in feature importance that might indicate model drift or data distribution changes.[8][26][27]

Fifth, institutions combine SHAP explanations with inherently interpretable components when possible, creating hybrid systems where complex models are used only for aspects where simplicity is infeasible while interpretable models handle routine decisions.[41][48] Sixth, institutions maintain sufficient explainability infrastructure and staff expertise that they can defend model decisions to internal auditors, external regulators, and potentially in legal proceedings.[26][29][48] This investment in people and processes may be as important as the technical sophistication of explainability tools.

Looking forward, regulatory guidance will likely continue evolving toward clearer expectations for how institutions should implement explainability in complex models.[48] The European Central Bank's updated guidance on internal models clarifies that machine learning models must be "adequately explainable" with performance justifying complexity, but specific regulatory thresholds for what constitutes adequate explainability remain somewhat ambiguous.[48] As regulators gain experience examining institutions using SHAP and related methods, expectations will likely clarify further. International standard-setting bodies may move toward specifying minimum explainability standards for models used in regulatory capital calculations or critical business decisions, analogous to existing standards for model validation and governance.[48]

## Conclusion

The question posed at this report's beginning—whether interpretability algorithms balance prediction accuracy and transparency, or whether SHAP approximation errors hinder regulatory compliance—admits no simple binary answer. Instead, the evidence reveals a nuanced landscape in which SHAP provides genuine value for financial institutions deploying complex risk models, while simultaneously carrying limitations that require sophisticated institutional governance to navigate successfully.

SHAP approximation errors are real and sometimes consequential.[2][8][27] For variables with moderate or weak predictive importance, approximation uncertainty can produce instability in feature rankings across model runs.[8][27] When SHAP outputs cross decision thresholds determining whether factors are cited in adverse action notices, this instability creates compliance risk.[8][27] High-dimensional models with many features experience greater approximation uncertainty than simpler models with well-established feature sets.[5][15][46]

However, the evidence equally indicates that for practical credit risk applications with reasonable model architectures and feature sets, SHAP delivers sufficiently stable and reliable explanations to support regulatory compliance when combined with appropriate governance controls.[1][8][27][32][46] Top-tier factors identified through SHAP analysis remain consistently ranked across model runs and implementations.[27] Financial institutions have successfully deployed SHAP-based explanations in regulatory examinations without generating systematic compliance concerns.[26][29] Regulators have shown increasing willingness to accept SHAP-based explanations of complex models, recognizing these methods as current best practices.[10][25][26][29]

The accuracy-interpretability tradeoff remains genuine, and SHAP alone cannot resolve it.[3][31][37][41] Institutions deploying sophisticated ensemble models for credit risk still sacrifice some transparency compared to simpler models, and SHAP cannot perfectly reverse this tradeoff.[1][3][31] However, SHAP can meaningfully narrow the gap, allowing institutions to capture the predictive benefits of complex models while providing defensible explanations grounded in principled game-theoretic reasoning.[1][4][5]

The future of model governance in financial services will likely involve continued movement toward hybrid approaches that combine inherently interpretable models with judiciously deployed complex models using post-hoc explainability methods like SHAP.[22][37][41][48] Regulators are increasingly signaling that perfect interpretability is not a prerequisite for deploying sophisticated models, provided institutions demonstrate rigorous governance, understand their models' limitations, and maintain robust explainability frameworks.[10][25][26][29][45][48] SHAP will play a central role in these frameworks, not as a panacea that resolves all accuracy-interpretability tensions, but as one essential tool within broader governance systems that include model validation, bias monitoring, ongoing performance assessment, and human expert judgment.[8][26][27][29]
