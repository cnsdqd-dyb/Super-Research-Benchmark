# Optimizing Privacy-Traceability Balance in CBDC Distributed Ledger Architectures: Navigating Zero-Knowledge Proof Complexity and Throughput Constraints

Central bank digital currencies represent a fundamental reimagining of how monetary systems can leverage distributed ledger technologies to serve retail and wholesale payments simultaneously[1][5]. At the heart of CBDC design lies a critical tension: policymakers and central banks must reconcile the legitimate public demand for transaction privacy with the regulatory imperative to trace and prevent illicit financial activity. This balance has proven extraordinarily difficult to achieve in practice, particularly when incorporating advanced cryptographic techniques like zero-knowledge proofs that promise to enable both privacy and auditability simultaneously. The search for optimal CBDC architectures reveals that while distributed ledger systems and zero-knowledge proofs offer genuine technological pathways toward this balance, they introduce computational overhead, throughput limitations, and implementation complexities that fundamentally constrain their practical application at scale[2][14]. The research question of whether these architectures can truly optimize the privacy-traceability balance must contend with the sobering reality that zero-knowledge proof systems currently suffer from performance degradation that can reduce transaction throughput by factors of several hundred times compared to traditional cryptographic approaches, and that no universal technical solution has yet emerged that completely resolves this foundational tension without significant trade-offs in speed, scalability, or operational complexity.

## Understanding CBDC Architectures and the Fundamental Privacy-Traceability Tension

The architecture of a central bank digital currency fundamentally determines how privacy and traceability can coexist within the system. Different CBDC designs create vastly different privacy landscapes, and understanding these architectural choices is essential to evaluating whether distributed ledger technologies can meaningfully resolve the privacy-traceability dilemma. The foundational architectural decision begins with the legal structure of claims and the operational roles that different entities play within the payment system[8][43]. Three primary architecture models have emerged in the research and pilot phases undertaken by central banks globally, and each creates distinct implications for how personal data flows through the system and who has access to sensitive information about transaction participants[5][8].

The **indirect CBDC model** represents an architecture where consumers hold claims on private intermediaries rather than direct claims on the central bank itself[8][43]. In this structure, the central bank maintains only wholesale accounts with commercial banks, while retail customers hold accounts with these intermediaries and transact through them[8]. From a privacy perspective, the indirect model creates a natural data compartmentalization where the central bank remains largely isolated from detailed knowledge of individual consumer transactions[5][8]. This separation of data repositories can be conceptually advantageous for privacy, as it means that no single entity maintains a comprehensive view of all transaction flows and participant identities[8]. However, this advantage comes with significant operational complexity and creates dependencies on private-sector intermediaries whose security posture directly affects the integrity of the entire system[5]. The intermediaries themselves must maintain substantial personal data about their customers to comply with anti-money laundering and know-your-customer requirements, meaning that while the central bank's data exposure is minimized, the risk of data breaches remains significant because the information is now distributed across multiple entities[4][25]. Furthermore, the indirect model reduces the central bank's direct operational responsibility for transaction processing, which may limit the bank's ability to implement real-time compliance monitoring or respond immediately to suspicious activity patterns[8].

The **direct CBDC model** inverts this arrangement entirely, with the central bank maintaining direct accounts for all retail users and handling transaction processing directly[8][43]. In this architecture, consumers have direct claims on the central bank rather than on intermediaries, and all transaction data flows through the central bank's infrastructure[8][43]. From an operational efficiency standpoint, this model can simplify certain aspects of system design because the central bank maintains complete control over the ledger and can implement comprehensive transaction monitoring and compliance procedures[8]. However, from a privacy perspective, the direct model creates extraordinary risks because the central bank becomes the repository for comprehensive personal financial data on every citizen in a jurisdiction[6][43]. This concentration of sensitive information creates a profound surveillance risk, as noted by both central banks and data protection authorities: if the central bank's systems are compromised, breached, or misused by government actors, the damage extends to every citizen's complete financial history[1][6]. The direct model also requires the central bank to maintain massive technical infrastructure capable of processing retail-level transaction volumes, which may require resources and expertise that exceed what many central banks possess[5][8][43].

The **hybrid CBDC model** attempts to thread the needle between these two extremes by maintaining both wholesale and retail ledgers with different characteristics[8][28][43]. In this architecture, the central bank maintains records of all retail CBDC holdings to enable portability and continuity in case an intermediary fails, but the actual day-to-day transaction processing is handled by the intermediaries themselves[8][43]. This approach creates a natural three-tier data flow: consumers interact with intermediaries who maintain KYC information; intermediaries maintain the operational ledger for their customers' transactions; and the central bank maintains a backup ledger and wholesale settlement layer[4][25][28]. The hybrid model offers theoretical advantages for privacy because the central bank's view is limited to aggregate balances and does not typically include transaction-by-transaction details or the identities of transacting parties[8][28]. However, the hybrid model also introduces architectural complexity because it requires constant synchronization between retail and wholesale ledgers, creates potential consistency challenges, and still maintains centralized backup data repositories that represent attractive targets for attackers or state actors[8][28].

Critically, none of these architectural choices operates in isolation from the choice of underlying technology infrastructure. The decision to use distributed ledger technology, centralized databases, or hybrid approaches fundamentally affects which entities have access to data and how that data flows through the system[4][5][8]. **Distributed ledger technology** enables multiple independent entities to maintain synchronized copies of transaction records through consensus mechanisms, creating theoretical advantages for resilience and decentralization[5][8][11][16]. When implemented in a **permissioned blockchain network** where only identified and authorized participants can validate transactions, DLT creates opportunities to distribute privacy-sensitive data across multiple nodes in a controlled fashion[20][23][25]. However, the fundamental characteristic of DLT systems is that every participant node must maintain a synchronized copy of all transactions, which means that implementing strong privacy protections requires additional cryptographic layering on top of the base ledger technology[5][8][49][55].

In contrast, **centralized ledger technologies** concentrate all transaction processing and data storage in a single entity or a small number of coordinated entities[4][5][8][25]. Centralized approaches can achieve significant performance advantages because they eliminate the consensus overhead that distributed systems require, but they concentrate privacy risks by ensuring that all data flows through potentially fewer, more vulnerable access points[4][5]. The Bank of England's recent guidance noted that "centrally governed, distributed database technologies" might prove more appropriate for CBDC than distributed ledger approaches, partly because centralized systems can achieve superior performance while still maintaining distributed backups for resilience[4][25]. This perspective suggests that the privacy-traceability balance may not inherently favor DLT over centralized approaches, contrary to some cryptocurrency advocates' assumptions.

## Zero-Knowledge Proofs as a Privacy Solution in CBDCs: Capabilities and Conceptual Promise

Zero-knowledge proofs have emerged as the primary cryptographic technology that CBDC designers hope will enable the simultaneous achievement of strong privacy protections and robust regulatory compliance. At their core, **zero-knowledge proofs** are cryptographic protocols that allow one party to prove to another party that a statement is true without revealing any information beyond the truth of that statement[2][10][50]. In the CBDC context, zero-knowledge proofs enable transactions to be validated as compliant with regulatory requirements without exposing the personal information, account balances, or transaction amounts underlying those transactions[2][18][50]. This represents a conceptual breakthrough because it decouples verification of transaction correctness from exposure of transaction details—the fundamental problem that has plagued privacy-preserving payment systems for decades[2][18].

The mathematical foundation of zero-knowledge proofs rests on the principle that the verifier can be convinced of the truth of a statement through a proof that provides no information about the witness or the underlying data[2][10][50]. Consider a concrete CBDC example: instead of revealing a customer's full account balance to prove they have sufficient funds for a transaction, a zero-knowledge proof could cryptographically demonstrate that the account balance exceeds the transaction amount without disclosing the actual balance[3][18][50]. Similarly, instead of revealing a customer's identity to prove they have passed anti-money laundering checks, a zero-knowledge proof could demonstrate that the customer has been verified as passing those checks, without exposing the customer's personal identifying information or the specific verification procedures used[18][50]. This capability addresses what many consider the central dilemma of CBDC design: how can systems enable regulatory compliance and prevent illicit activity without creating comprehensive personal surveillance infrastructure[1][3][6][18].

Several distinct categories of zero-knowledge proof systems have been developed, each with different performance characteristics and security assumptions[2][10][31]. **zk-SNARKs** (Succinct Non-Interactive Arguments of Knowledge) offer very compact proof sizes, typically around 200 bytes, and fast verification times, making them attractive for on-chain verification or resource-constrained environments[2][10][31]. However, SNARKs typically require a "trusted setup" ceremony where cryptographic parameters are generated under the assumption that they remain secret; if these setup parameters are compromised, the security of the entire system fails[2][10][31]. **zk-STARKs** (Scalable Transparent Arguments of Knowledge) eliminate the need for trusted setup, providing transparency and theoretical resistance to quantum computing attacks, but at the cost of much larger proof sizes, typically 100 to 200 kilobytes or more[2][10][31]. The size difference between SNARKs and STARKs becomes significant in high-throughput payment systems where proof size directly affects bandwidth consumption and settlement latency[2][10][31].

**Bulletproofs** represent another approach to zero-knowledge proving that avoids trusted setup and offers relatively compact proofs for certain classes of statements, though proof size grows with the complexity of the statement being proven[2][10]. For CBDCs, this means that simple proofs about transaction validity might be quite compact, but more complex proofs involving multiple conditions or attributes could become impractically large[2][10]. The **Halo 2** proving system, which has been developed for production applications, uses recursive composition of SNARKs to achieve both privacy and scalability without requiring circuit-specific trusted setups, representing a more recent advance that addresses some earlier limitations[10][14]. However, even Halo 2 requires careful architectural consideration to ensure that the computational overhead of proof generation does not create performance bottlenecks in real-time payment systems[10][14].

In the context of CBDCs specifically, several concrete applications of zero-knowledge proofs have been explored by central banks and researchers. **Privacy-preserving compliance** represents one of the most promising applications: instead of central banks and intermediaries seeing transaction details to verify AML/CFT compliance, zero-knowledge proofs could enable compliance verification based on cryptographic proofs of rule adherence[2][18][50][51]. For example, a transaction could generate a proof that the transaction amount is below a specified threshold without revealing the actual amount, satisfying AML reporting requirements without exposing transaction details[2][18][50]. Similarly, zero-knowledge proofs could demonstrate that a transaction participant has been verified through KYC procedures without requiring the central bank or payment intermediaries to actually see the customer's identity documents or personal data[2][18][50][51].

**Attribute-based authentication** represents another significant application where zero-knowledge proofs enable users to prove they possess certain verified attributes—such as being over a certain age, having a particular occupation, or having passed specific security assessments—without revealing the underlying personal information used to establish those attributes[15][50][51]. This capability becomes particularly valuable for CBDCs that support conditional payments, where a payment might only complete if the recipient possesses certain verified attributes, such as an age-restricted purchase that only completes if the recipient can prove they are over eighteen[15][50][51].

**Transaction privacy with aggregate visibility** represents perhaps the most conceptually elegant application: central banks could implement zero-knowledge proofs such that individual transaction privacy is protected from all parties, including the central bank, while the central bank can still observe aggregate statistics about CBDC circulation, transaction volumes, and flows without seeing individual transactions[2][3][21][27]. This approach addresses the core privacy concern about CBDCs—that they could enable total financial surveillance—while still enabling central banks to meet their policy objectives regarding monetary aggregates and systemic risk monitoring[2][3][21][27].

## Technical Performance Limitations of Zero-Knowledge Proof Systems: Where Cryptographic Elegance Meets Practical Constraints

Despite the conceptual promise of zero-knowledge proofs for CBDC privacy, these systems suffer from substantial computational overhead that fundamentally limits their practical deployment in high-throughput payment systems. The computational cost of generating zero-knowledge proofs represents the primary bottleneck and creates the most severe constraint on system throughput[2][7][31][34]. Research on the "cost of verifiability" reveals that the proving step—where a prover generates a zero-knowledge proof of some computation—currently requires computational resources that are orders of magnitude greater than the cost of either the original computation or verification of the proof[34]. One analyst estimated that the "kappa" (the ratio of proving cost to computing cost) currently stands at approximately 10,000, meaning that generating a zero-knowledge proof is roughly 10,000 times more expensive computationally than simply performing the computation directly[34].

This computational overhead translates directly into latency and throughput constraints in production CBDC systems. The **Project Tourbillon** experiments conducted by the Bank for International Settlements Innovation Hub provide concrete empirical evidence of these constraints[2][21][24][27]. Project Tourbillon implemented two distinct prototypes of eCash-based CBDCs designed to provide payer anonymity while maintaining security and scalability[21][24][27]. When testing with quantum-safe cryptography—which becomes increasingly important as quantum computing capabilities advance—the results revealed devastating performance degradation: throughput was reduced by a factor of 200 compared to classical cryptography, and transaction latency increased by a factor of five[2][21][24][27]. For context, these prototypes were still using relatively straightforward blind signature schemes rather than full zero-knowledge proofs; implementing comprehensive zero-knowledge proof systems could plausibly result in even more severe performance impacts[2][21][24][27].

In more general zero-knowledge proof implementations, research has demonstrated that **simple circuits with only 1,000 constraints** generate proofs in approximately 100 to 2,000 milliseconds on modern desktop browsers, while **complex circuits with 100,000 or more constraints** can require 10 to 60 seconds, depending on device capabilities[31]. For a payment system attempting to process thousands of transactions per second, adding proof generation times in the range of seconds or even hundreds of milliseconds becomes catastrophic[31][37][38]. Mobile devices, which many retail CBDC users would employ, typically experience 3 to 10 times slower performance than desktop computers, further constraining the feasibility of zero-knowledge proof-based CBDCs for broad retail deployment[31].

The **verification overhead** of zero-knowledge proofs, while dramatically smaller than proving overhead, still contributes meaningfully to transaction processing latency in resource-constrained environments. Current Groth16 SNARK verification costs approximately 150,000 to 200,000 gas on the Ethereum blockchain, equivalent to $5 to $15 per verification depending on network congestion[31][34]. While PLONK-based verification is more efficient at around 600,000 to 800,000 gas per verification, STARKs can require 2 to 5 million gas, representing substantially higher verification costs[31]. For a CBDC system processing millions of transactions daily, accumulating verification costs across that volume becomes significant, both in terms of computational resources consumed and in terms of potential transaction fees that might be passed through to end users[31][34].

**Succinctness**—the property that proof size is short compared to the statement being proven—represents the theoretical advantage that makes zero-knowledge proofs attractive for scalable payment systems[2][10]. However, in practice, proof size depends substantially on which zero-knowledge proof system is employed and how complex the statement being proven is[2][10][31]. SNARKs achieve genuinely succinct proofs around 200 bytes, but STARKs generate proofs in the 100 to 200 kilobyte range, creating meaningful bandwidth overhead if millions of proofs must be transmitted and stored[2][10][31]. For a CBDC settlement system, this bandwidth overhead could create bottlenecks, particularly during periods of high transaction volume when bandwidth becomes a scarce resource[2][10][31].

Beyond the raw computational overhead, zero-knowledge proof systems introduce several additional technical challenges that further constrain practical implementation in CBDC systems. **Circuit complexity and development overhead** represents a significant barrier: developers building zero-knowledge proof systems must work with specialized domain-specific languages like Cairo, Circom, or Noir that require learning entirely new skill sets and impose high cognitive overhead[2][7][31]. An incorrectly implemented circuit might fail to provide the required functionality or security, and the highly technical nature of these systems means that the opportunities for subtle errors are substantial[2][7][31]. Furthermore, the lack of standardized, well-tested libraries and frameworks for zero-knowledge proofs means that most implementations require custom development or reliance on relatively immature open-source tools[2][7][31].

**Trusted setup requirements** in SNARKs create governance and security challenges that extend beyond pure computational cost[2][10][31]. For each distinct zero-knowledge proof circuit, the cryptographic parameters must be generated in a ceremony where participants must be trusted not to compromise the setup parameters[2][10][31]. This requirement creates operational complexity and governance challenges: who conducts these ceremonies? How are the parameters stored and protected? What happens if setup parameters are eventually compromised through quantum computing or other future attacks?[2][10][31]. While newer systems like Halo 2 address some of these concerns through recursive composition, eliminating the circuit-specific setup overhead, they introduce other complexities related to recursive proof composition[10][14].

The **security assumptions underlying zero-knowledge proofs** introduce vulnerabilities that sometimes receive insufficient attention in discussions of zero-knowledge proof applications. Some classes of zero-knowledge proofs have been demonstrated to be vulnerable to specific attacks under certain conditions[5][31]. For example, certain SNARK constructions have shown vulnerabilities to collusion attacks where multiple provers coordinate to extract information that should remain hidden[5][17][31]. Additionally, the security of zero-knowledge proofs that rely on discrete logarithm assumptions or other number-theoretic problems is fundamentally threatened by sufficiently advanced quantum computers, which motivates the intense interest in quantum-resistant zero-knowledge proofs but also introduces the performance degradation observed in Project Tourbillon[2][21][24][27].

## Architectural Optimization Strategies: Can Distributed Ledgers Enable Better Privacy-Traceability Balance?

Given these significant constraints on zero-knowledge proof performance, CBDC designers have explored architectural strategies aimed at optimizing the privacy-traceability balance while minimizing reliance on full zero-knowledge proof systems for every transaction. These optimization strategies typically involve careful choices about where and when to apply advanced cryptographic techniques, how to distribute computation across system components, and how to leverage the structure of the CBDC system itself to achieve privacy goals without full cryptographic overhead on every transaction.

One of the most promising architectural approaches involves **tiered privacy systems** where different transactions receive different levels of privacy protection based on risk assessment[6][22][26][45]. In this approach, small transactions below a certain threshold would receive cash-like anonymity and protection from surveillance, since the compliance risk associated with small transactions is generally lower[6][22][26][45]. Transactions above the threshold would trigger increasingly detailed compliance verification, with the privacy level graduated according to the risk profile[6][22][26][45]. This architecture allows CBDC systems to achieve strong privacy protection for the vast majority of transactions—which are small routine payments—while preserving detailed visibility for higher-risk, higher-value transactions[6][22][26][45]. The European Central Bank's anonymity voucher approach exemplifies this principle: the system issues time-limited vouchers that allow users to spend up to a certain threshold anonymously, after which compliance verification is triggered[30][42]. This design enables privacy by default for routine transactions while preserving regulatory compliance for higher-value transactions, without requiring full zero-knowledge proof overhead on every transaction[30][42].

Another significant architectural optimization involves **data separation and minimization**, where different entities in the CBDC system maintain different subsets of data, and zero-knowledge proofs are deployed at the boundaries between entities to enable compliance verification without full data sharing[4][25][29][39][51][56]. In this architecture, payment service providers would maintain KYC information about their customers and would conduct compliance checks locally using this information[4][25][29][39][51][56]. The central bank would never see customer identity information or transaction details, but instead would receive zero-knowledge proofs that transactions are compliant with regulatory requirements[4][25][29][39][51][56]. This architectural choice both reduces the central bank's data exposure and potentially reduces the total volume of zero-knowledge proofs that must be generated, since compliance verification could happen locally within intermediaries using traditional cryptographic techniques rather than requiring full zero-knowledge proof generation[4][25][29][39][51][56].

The **Bank of Canada's research on privacy-enhancing technologies for CBDCs** exemplifies this architecture-driven approach to optimizing privacy while managing performance[14][29][39][51]. The Bank of Canada's proposed framework emphasizes that privacy requirements should be wired into the core architecture from the design stage rather than added as an afterthought[14][29][39][51]. By carefully separating the roles of the central bank, intermediaries, and specialized compliance entities, the architecture can minimize how much personal data any single entity needs to access while still enabling regulatory compliance[14][29][39][51]. For example, a specialized AML authority could maintain the capability to verify customer identity and conduct compliance checks, but would operate with restricted data access and would not see transaction data unless specific compliance flags are triggered[14][29][39][51].

**Permissioned distributed ledger approaches** represent another architectural strategy for optimizing the privacy-traceability balance[4][20][25][49][55]. Unlike permissionless blockchains where every participant can theoretically access all transaction data, permissioned DLT systems restrict which entities can read and write specific data[4][20][25][49][55]. This architectural feature enables sophisticated access control strategies where different participants have visibility to different subsets of the ledger: the central bank might see only aggregate flows and wholesale settlement, intermediaries might see only transactions involving their customers, and compliance authorities might see only transactions that trigger suspicious activity flags[4][25][49][55]. This approach leverages the distributed nature of DLT to achieve privacy through data compartmentalization rather than through comprehensive cryptographic hiding[4][25][49][55].

Project Aurum, a Bank for International Settlements proof-of-concept for a two-tier CBDC, demonstrates this architectural approach in practice[28][43]. In Project Aurum's design, retail transactions are processed through intermediaries using their own ledgers, while wholesale settlement uses a separate blockchain layer[28][43]. Critically, retail transactions are not reflected directly on the wholesale ledger; instead, only the aggregated settlement flows between intermediaries appear at that layer[28][43]. This architecture achieves significant privacy advantages because no single entity sees the full transaction graph[28][43]. Retail customers' detailed transaction histories remain at the intermediary level, the central bank sees only wholesale flows, and regulatory authorities can request specific transaction details when compliance concerns emerge, but comprehensive transaction surveillance is structurally prevented by the architecture itself rather than by cryptographic hiding[28][43].

**Offline payment capabilities** represent an alternative architectural strategy for achieving privacy that does not rely on cryptographic complexity[47][48]. If CBDC systems enable peer-to-peer offline payments where value transfers directly between devices without broadcasting to the central ledger, those transactions could achieve cash-like privacy without requiring zero-knowledge proofs[47][48]. The Bank of England's research on offline payments notes that device-based offline payments could enable truly private transactions, though at the cost of accepting risks of counterfeiting, double-spending, and fraud that cash faces but digital systems typically prevent[47][48]. The challenge with offline CBDC architectures is achieving sufficient fraud prevention without requiring comprehensive cryptographic verification at settlement time, when the transactions finally reconnect to the online system[47][48].

**Project Tourbillon**, conducted by the Bank for International Settlements Innovation Hub Swiss Centre, represents the most comprehensive public attempt to explore privacy, security, and scalability trade-offs in eCash-based CBDCs[2][21][24][27]. The project implemented two distinct prototype designs, eCash 1.0 (EC1) and eCash 2.0 (EC2), both based on Chaum's original eCash cryptographic framework, to test how privacy, security, and scalability could be balanced[21][24][27]. eCash 1.0 focused on achieving maximum payer anonymity by implementing completely blind signatures, such that neither the central bank nor intermediaries could link specific coins to their withdrawers[21][24][27]. eCash 2.0 maintained privacy from merchants and users but allowed the central bank to track unspent coins through cryptographic commitments, enabling enhanced security against counterfeiting[21][24][27].

The key findings from Project Tourbillon demonstrated that **payer anonymity is technically feasible while maintaining regulatory compliance and reasonable security properties**[21][24][27]. In both prototypes, consumers remained anonymous to all parties—merchants, banks, and the central bank—when making payments, yet the central bank could monitor aggregate CBDC circulation in real time without seeing personal transaction data[21][24][27]. This finding directly contradicts the assertion that privacy and regulatory visibility are fundamentally incompatible; instead, it demonstrates that architectural choices about what data different entities observe can decouple user privacy from regulatory oversight[21][24][27].

When testing with quantum-safe cryptography, the throughput reduction of 200x and latency increase of 5x proved devastating for system performance[2][21][24][27]. Furthermore, the prototypes demonstrated that even with classical cryptography, supporting efficient coin rebalancing and handling variable transaction amounts created significant complexity[21][24][27]. The research team concluded that while payer anonymity in eCash-based CBDCs is technically feasible and can handle thousands of transactions per second, doing so requires specialized expertise, introduces additional latency and computational overhead, and still faces unresolved challenges around quantum-safe implementation[2][21][24][27].

## Current Bottlenecks and Technical Constraints: Where Theory Encounters Reality

Despite significant research effort and multiple pilot programs, several fundamental bottlenecks prevent optimal privacy-traceability balance from being achieved at production scale with current technology. These bottlenecks represent not theoretical limitations but practical constraints that significantly constrain what architectures can actually achieve.

The **computational cost of proof generation** remains the dominant bottleneck limiting CBDC throughput if comprehensive zero-knowledge proof deployment is attempted[2][7][34]. Even the most optimized zero-knowledge proof systems require orders of magnitude more computation to generate proofs than to verify them, and this asymmetry creates fundamental limits on transaction processing speed[2][7][34]. While researchers have achieved significant improvements—Snarkify demonstrated a 900x speedup in proof generation using GPUs and optimized algorithms—the absolute performance still constrains what is feasible[34]. For context, even 900x speedups leave zero-knowledge proof generation substantially slower than traditional cryptographic operations, meaning that substituting zero-knowledge proofs for standard transaction verification would still create noticeable performance degradation[34].

The **quantum computing threat** to current cryptographic systems creates a paradoxical constraint on CBDC privacy: implementing quantum-safe cryptography to protect long-term privacy of CBDC systems introduces performance degradation so severe that it may render systems impractical at scale[2][21][24][27]. Project Tourbillon's findings that quantum-safe cryptography reduces throughput by 200x illustrate this tension starkly[2][21][24][27]. While quantum computers capable of breaking RSA and other current cryptographic systems likely remain years or decades away, CBDC systems will operate for decades and must protect transactions that may retain sensitivity for similar timeframes[2][21][24][27][31]. This creates a genuine design challenge: central banks must implement quantum-safe cryptography despite severe performance costs, because the alternative—deploying systems that will be retroactively compromised if quantum computers eventually emerge—is unacceptable[2][21][24][27][31].

The **standardization vacuum** around zero-knowledge proofs and privacy-enhancing technologies for CBDCs creates implementation uncertainty and delays adoption[1][14][29][39][51][56]. Most CBDC projects implementing zero-knowledge proofs or other advanced privacy techniques must either develop custom implementations or rely on relatively immature open-source tools, since standardized, production-ready libraries specifically designed for CBDC applications remain limited[1][14][29][39][51][56]. This standardization gap increases development costs, extends timelines, and creates security risks because bespoke implementations of complex cryptographic systems are inherently more vulnerable to subtle errors than well-tested standardized approaches[1][14][29][39][51][56].

**Secure multiparty computation (SMPC)** represents an alternative approach to achieving privacy in CBDC systems by enabling multiple parties to jointly compute results from their private data without ever exposing that data to one another[14][29][33][36][39][51][56]. SMPC enables scenarios where banks can perform fraud detection across their customer bases, or where compliance authorities can detect suspicious patterns across institutions, without any individual institution seeing other institutions' data[14][33][36]. However, SMPC systems face significant scalability challenges: the computational overhead of multiparty protocols can be 100x or more compared to single-party computation, and the requirement for continuous communication among all participating parties creates operational complexity and potential points of failure[14][33][36][39][51][56]. Furthermore, SMPC research has found that even optimized protocols require significant waiting times; one empirical study of SMPC using real-world trading data found that 41% of trades incurred wait times longer than 5 seconds, with the longest trade taking 101 seconds[17][36]. For payment systems, waiting times of this magnitude render SMPC impractical for real-time retail transactions[17][36].

**Homomorphic encryption**—which enables computations to be performed on encrypted data without decryption—represents another privacy-enhancing technology approach, but it faces even more severe performance constraints than SMPC[14][32][35][39][51][56]. Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) introduces computational overhead of 3 to 7 times current performance levels, though research suggests this gap could eventually narrow to 1.5 to 3 times over several years[32][35]. For a payment system attempting to process thousands of transactions per second, even 3x overhead becomes significant[32][35]. Additionally, homomorphic encryption introduces substantial memory overhead and energy consumption, making it particularly unsuitable for mobile devices that many retail CBDC users would employ[32][35][39].

The **ledger architecture choice** creates fundamental trade-offs between privacy, performance, and operational complexity that cannot be completely resolved through cryptographic techniques alone[4][5][8][25][49][55][56]. Distributed ledger systems offer theoretical advantages in resilience and decentralization, but require all participating nodes to maintain synchronized copies of transaction data, which constrains privacy because data distribution increases the risk of unauthorized access[4][5][8][25][49][55][56]. Centralized ledger systems offer performance and privacy advantages through concentrated data management, but create single points of failure and concentration of surveillance risk[4][5][8][25][49][55][56]. No architectural choice provides optimal performance, privacy, and resilience simultaneously; instead, CBDC designers must accept trade-offs based on jurisdiction-specific priorities[4][5][8][25][49][55][56].

The **data minimization challenge** represents another persistent bottleneck: while privacy advocates recommend minimizing the data that systems collect and store, regulatory requirements for AML/CFT compliance effectively mandate comprehensive data collection about customer identities and transaction patterns[1][6][14][29][39][51][56]. This creates an inherent tension: better privacy requires collecting and retaining less data, but regulatory compliance requires collecting and retaining comprehensive data[1][6][14][29][39][51][56]. Architectural strategies can minimize which entities have access to data, and cryptographic techniques can hide data in transit and in use, but they cannot eliminate the fundamental requirement for data collection without compromising regulatory compliance[1][6][14][29][39][51][56]. This tension suggests that the privacy-traceability optimization problem may not be purely technical but may require policy-level decisions about acceptable regulatory scope and government surveillance capabilities[1][6][14][29][39][51][56].

## Emerging Solutions and Future Directions: Toward Practical Privacy-Traceability Optimization

Despite these significant constraints and bottlenecks, several promising research directions and emerging technologies suggest pathways toward better optimization of the privacy-traceability balance in future CBDC systems. These solutions typically involve careful combinations of architectural strategies, privacy-enhancing technologies, and policy-level decisions rather than relying on any single silver-bullet technology.

**Layered privacy and compliance architectures** represent one of the most promising near-term approaches where different layers of the CBDC system provide different privacy guarantees and operate under different governance rules[29][39][51][56]. The foundational layer would implement strong cryptographic privacy protections and would not expose transaction details to any entity except the parties directly involved in the transaction[29][39][51][56]. A second layer would enable compliance authorities to access transaction data only under specific legal procedures, such as warrants or court orders, with comprehensive logging of all data access attempts[29][39][51][56]. A third layer would maintain aggregate statistics about transaction flows and volumes to enable central bank monetary policy monitoring, but would not include transaction-level details[29][39][51][56]. This layered approach allows CBDCs to achieve privacy by default while preserving the regulatory oversight that democratic societies require[29][39][51][56].

**Threshold cryptography and decentralized key management** represent emerging approaches to addressing privacy and governance concerns in CBDC systems[14][29][39][51][56]. Instead of the central bank maintaining comprehensive access to CBDC transaction data, key management could be split among multiple parties—perhaps the central bank, the financial regulator, and an independent oversight body—such that no single entity can unilaterally access transaction data[14][29][39][51][56]. This approach would require collusion among multiple parties to compromise privacy, and would create institutional checks and balances around data access[14][29][39][51][56].

**Trusted execution environments (TEEs)** and specialized secure hardware represent another emerging approach to achieving privacy while maintaining performance[14][29][39][51][56]. TEEs like Intel SGX or ARM TrustZone enable computations to be performed on sensitive data within hardware-protected enclaves where external parties cannot observe either the inputs or intermediate computations[14][29][39][51][56]. This approach could enable compliance authorities to perform sophisticated analysis on encrypted transaction data without ever seeing the data in unencrypted form[14][29][39][51][56]. However, TEE systems face security challenges related to side-channel attacks that can leak information through power consumption patterns or timing variations, and recent research has demonstrated multiple categories of TEE vulnerabilities that require careful mitigation[14][29][39][51][56].

**Offline CBDC capabilities with enhanced cryptography** represent another promising direction where device-based offline payments could provide privacy for routine transactions without requiring comprehensive zero-knowledge proof deployment[15][47][48]. If CBDC systems enabled truly offline peer-to-peer transfers with reasonable fraud prevention but accepted some fraud risk (similar to cash), those transactions could achieve privacy without cryptographic overhead[15][47][48]. Settlement would occur when devices reconnect to the online system, at which point compliance verification could happen on aggregated data rather than on individual transactions[15][47][48].

**Quantum-resistant privacy-preserving techniques** represent an essential research direction where alternatives to zero-knowledge proofs and other quantum-vulnerable systems are developed and optimized[2][21][24][27][31]. The severe performance degradation observed with quantum-safe implementations suggests that new cryptographic approaches designed specifically for quantum-resistant privacy may offer better performance than retrofitting quantum-safety onto existing systems[2][21][24][27][31]. Research into lattice-based cryptography, hash-based signatures, and other post-quantum approaches may eventually provide quantum-resistant privacy at acceptable performance levels[2][21][24][27][31].

**Hybrid architectures combining multiple approaches** represent the most pragmatic near-term path forward where different privacy techniques are deployed where they perform best rather than attempting to use any single technology uniformly[14][29][39][51][56]. Some transactions might use zero-knowledge proofs for maximum privacy, others might use tiered access controls, others might use encryption with key management, and still others might accept different privacy models based on transaction value[14][29][39][51][56]. This hybrid approach would require sophisticated architecture design and governance frameworks, but could avoid forcing any single privacy technique to perform all functions despite its inherent limitations[14][29][39][51][56].

The **Bank of Canada's comprehensive framework for privacy-enhancing technologies** illustrates this hybrid approach in detail, identifying distinct classes of techniques—data alteration, data shielding, and system architecture approaches—and matching each technique to specific use cases where it performs most effectively[14][29][39][51]. By acknowledging that no single approach solves all privacy challenges, this framework recognizes that practical CBDC privacy will require combining multiple techniques thoughtfully rather than seeking a universal solution[14][29][39][51].

## Fundamental Tensions and Architectural Trade-offs: Why Complete Optimization May Be Impossible

Beyond specific technical constraints, deeper analytical examination reveals fundamental tensions in the privacy-traceability optimization problem that may not be completely resolvable through any architecture or technology combination. These tensions reflect opposing policy objectives and different values that democratic societies prioritize differently, suggesting that optimization will ultimately involve political choices about acceptable risk and surveillance rather than purely technical solutions.

The **surveillance versus privacy tension** represents perhaps the most fundamental trade-off in CBDC design. Comprehensive regulatory compliance and effective prevention of financial crime require visibility into transaction patterns, identity information, and fund flows at scales that inevitably enable systematic surveillance of populations[1][6][14][22][29][39][51][56][59]. Conversely, strong privacy protection for individuals requires limiting government and institutional access to financial data in ways that necessarily reduce regulatory visibility and create opportunities for illicit activity[1][6][14][22][29][39][51][56][59]. These objectives are not technically incompatible—the various pilot programs demonstrate that privacy and compliance can coexist through careful architectural choices—but they are directionally opposed, meaning that strengthening one inherently weakens the other[1][6][14][22][29][39][51][56][59]. Different democratic societies will reach different conclusions about where this balance should be struck, and those policy decisions will inevitably shape CBDC architectures more fundamentally than any technical consideration[1][6][14][22][29][39][51][56][59].

The **performance versus privacy trade-off** reflects the computational reality that cryptographic privacy protection requires computational resources that reduce system throughput[2][7][14][31][34][37][39]. While hardware improvements and algorithmic optimization have reduced this trade-off significantly, they have not eliminated it[2][7][14][31][34][37][39]. CBDCs attempting to achieve both maximum throughput and comprehensive privacy must accept that some privacy protections will be computationally expensive, creating latency and reducing transaction speed[2][7][14][31][34][37][39]. CBDCs prioritizing throughput must accept reduced privacy in at least some transactions[2][7][14][31][34][37][39]. This trade-off appears to be fundamental rather than representing a temporary limitation that future technology will overcome[2][7][14][31][34][37][39].

The **transparency versus auditability tension** reflects that strong privacy necessarily reduces transparency, but regulators require both: they want to prevent illicit activity (which requires transaction transparency) while also respecting individual privacy (which requires transaction opacity)[1][6][14][22][29][39][51][56][59]. Zero-knowledge proofs and cryptographic techniques can decouple transparency and auditability—enabling systems to verify compliance without seeing actual transaction details—but only at significant computational cost[2][18][50][51]. Simpler architectures that accept reduced privacy but maintain transparency can achieve better performance[2][5][8][37]. CBDCs must choose which combination of these properties to prioritize[2][5][8][37].

The **decentralization versus performance trade-off** reflects that distributed ledger systems offer resilience and decentralization advantages but require consensus mechanisms that reduce throughput and increase latency[5][8][11][16][37][41]. Centralized systems achieve better performance but concentrate operational and privacy risks[5][8][11][16][37][41]. Project Hamilton's findings that parallel-processing architectures can achieve 1.7 million transactions per second but sacrifice ordered transaction histories illustrate this trade-off starkly: maximum throughput requires accepting architectural compromises that may create auditability challenges[37].

## Conclusion: The Privacy-Traceability Optimization Remains Constrained by Fundamental Tensions

The analysis of CBDC distributed ledger architectures and zero-knowledge proof applications reveals a nuanced reality: while these technologies enable meaningfully better privacy-traceability balance than traditional financial systems, they cannot achieve simultaneous optimization of privacy, traceability, performance, and resilience. The question posed in the original query—whether CBDC distributed ledger architectures can optimize the privacy-traceability balance, or whether zero-knowledge proof complexity limits throughput caps—presents a false dichotomy. Both statements are partially true: distributed ledger architectures and zero-knowledge proofs genuinely enable better privacy-traceability balance than alternatives, but zero-knowledge proof complexity does create meaningful throughput constraints, and fundamental tensions between privacy and surveillance objectives prevent complete optimization.

The pilot programs and experimental prototypes conducted by central banks globally demonstrate that architectures exist enabling reasonable privacy protections while maintaining regulatory compliance and acceptable performance characteristics[21][24][27][28][37][43]. Project Tourbillon showed that payer anonymity is technically feasible at thousands of transactions per second; Project Aurum demonstrated that architectural data separation can achieve privacy without comprehensive cryptography on every transaction; and Project Hamilton established that multi-million-transaction-per-second throughput is achievable if architectural trade-offs are accepted[21][24][27][28][37][43]. China's e-CNY, Sweden's e-krona, and the European Central Bank's anonymity research all demonstrate that operational CBDC systems can implement meaningful privacy protections while satisfying regulatory requirements[3][26][30][42].

However, these successes come with qualifications and constraints. Project Tourbillon found that quantum-safe cryptography reduces throughput by 200x, suggesting that achieving long-term security while maintaining privacy and performance represents a genuine unsolved technical challenge[2][21][24][27]. Project Hamilton's findings that maximum throughput requires sacrificing ordered transaction histories suggest that CBDCs choosing highest performance will sacrifice certain auditability and compliance advantages[37]. The lack of standardized privacy-enhancing technology frameworks means that each CBDC project implementing advanced privacy features must develop largely custom solutions, increasing cost and risk[1][14][29][39][51][56].

Most fundamentally, the privacy-traceability optimization problem reflects deeper tensions between societal values that transcend technical considerations. Democratic societies must balance individual privacy rights against collective security interests, and that balance involves political choices rather than pure technical optimization[1][6][59]. Central banks implementing CBDCs will make different architectural choices reflecting their societies' different values, with some prioritizing privacy even at performance cost, others prioritizing regulatory visibility even at privacy cost, and most attempting some hybrid balance[3][26][30][42].

The realistic conclusion is that CBDC distributed ledger architectures and zero-knowledge proofs represent genuine technological progress in the privacy-traceability domain, enabling privacy protections substantially better than traditional alternatives, but they do not represent a complete solution that makes the privacy-traceability trade-off disappear. The evidence suggests that reasonable CBDCs combining multiple architectural strategies, privacy-enhancing technologies, and policy-level governance choices can achieve adequate privacy protections and effective regulatory compliance simultaneously, but only by accepting performance limitations, architectural complexity, and ongoing technical maintenance burdens. CBDCs that attempt to maximize all dimensions simultaneously—maximum privacy, maximum traceability, maximum throughput, maximum resilience, and minimal complexity—will necessarily fail; successful CBDC implementations will reflect explicit prioritization choices about which objectives matter most for their specific jurisdictions.
