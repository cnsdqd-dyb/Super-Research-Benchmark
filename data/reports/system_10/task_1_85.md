# Setting Similarity Thresholds for Substitute Recommendations in Shopping Basket Analysis

Market basket analysis represents one of the most powerful analytical techniques for understanding consumer purchasing behavior and optimizing retail operations. Within this domain, the identification and recommendation of substitute products represents a critical yet complex challenge that requires careful consideration of multiple computational and business factors. **The similarity threshold for substitute recommendations is not a single fixed value but rather a multidimensional parameter that must be strategically calibrated based on product characteristics, similarity metrics, business objectives, statistical significance measures, and domain-specific requirements.** This comprehensive examination explores the theoretical foundations, practical methodologies, algorithmic approaches, and implementation considerations that collectively determine how organizations establish appropriate similarity thresholds for identifying and recommending product substitutes in shopping basket analysis systems.

## Foundational Concepts of Market Basket Analysis and Product Relationships

Market basket analysis represents a data-driven approach to understanding which products customers purchase together and how these purchasing patterns relate to broader consumer behavior patterns[4]. At its most fundamental level, market basket analysis creates if-then scenario rules, such as "if a customer purchases item A, then they are likely to purchase item B,"[4] with these rules being probabilistic in nature and derived from the frequencies of co-occurrence observed in transactional data. The methodology has evolved from its origins in the retail industry to become a foundational technique used across multiple sectors for understanding customer behavior, optimizing product placement, and generating personalized recommendations[23][52].

Within the broader framework of market basket analysis, products can maintain several distinct types of relationships, each requiring different analytical approaches and thresholds. **Complementary products** are those that customers purchase together because they serve related purposes or enhance each other's utility—such as peanut butter and jelly, or computers and software[38][41]. **Substitute products**, conversely, are those that can be purchased instead of one another because they serve essentially the same purpose or fulfill the same customer need[9][15]. The distinction between these product relationships has profound implications for how retailers stock shelves, structure promotions, and generate recommendations. Understanding that product A and product B are substitutes suggests they should be positioned differently in a store than if they were complements, and recommendations must acknowledge that a customer purchasing one substitute may be less likely to purchase another[38][39].

Traditional market basket analysis has focused primarily on identifying complementary products through transaction-level analysis, examining what items appear together within a single shopping occasion[3]. However, this single-transaction perspective has significant limitations when attempting to identify substitutes. The fundamental challenge emerges from the fact that substitute products, by definition, are purchased alternatively rather than together, making them invisible to simple transaction-level analysis[3][39]. A customer who purchases Brand A's cereal in one transaction and Brand B's cereal in another transaction is demonstrating a substitution behavior, but this pattern only becomes apparent when analyzing cross-transaction, longitudinal purchase history[3][39]. This requirement to analyze product relationships across multiple transactions and longer time horizons fundamentally changes the computational complexity and the metrics used to assess product similarity.

## The Evolution from Traditional Basket Analysis to Cross-Basket Analytics

The field of market basket analysis has experienced significant methodological evolution driven by advances in computational capabilities and data collection infrastructure. Traditional market basket analysis relies on the **Apriori algorithm**, which systematically searches transactional databases for frequent itemsets and generates association rules based on support and confidence metrics[4][8][11][31]. The support metric indicates how frequently a set of items appears together across all transactions, while confidence measures the probability that item B is purchased given that item A was purchased[4][8][11].

However, cross-basket purchase analytics represents a paradigm shift that extends analysis across multiple dimensions beyond the single transaction[3]. This advanced approach analyzes purchase patterns across days, weeks, or months to identify lifecycle-based buying behaviors, examines relationships between product categories rather than just individual SKUs, integrates data from multiple purchase channels to create unified customer profiles, and incorporates behavioral metrics such as customer demographics and engagement patterns[3]. The implications for substitute identification are substantial: while traditional basket analysis might identify that peanut butter and jelly appear together, cross-basket analytics can reveal that customers who purchase organic baby food in January are more likely to purchase premium skincare products three months later, identifying a temporal substitution pattern that reflects broader lifestyle transitions[3].

The technical requirements for cross-basket analytics differ fundamentally from traditional approaches. Organizations must maintain granular, line-item data that tracks individual products across transactions rather than aggregated transaction summaries[3]. They require standardized product identifiers such as UPCs and EANs to accurately track the same product across channels and time periods, consistent category hierarchies to enable category-level analysis, accurate customer identification to link purchases across transactions, and clean temporal data with proper time zone handling[3]. These data infrastructure requirements exceed what traditional point-of-sale systems commonly provide, necessitating additional investment in data collection and integration technologies[3].

## Understanding Similarity Metrics and Their Role in Threshold Setting

The selection of an appropriate similarity metric represents one of the most critical determinants of threshold-setting effectiveness for substitute identification. Different similarity metrics emphasize different aspects of product relationships, leading to substantially different results when applied to the same data with identical threshold values. Understanding these metrics and their underlying assumptions becomes essential for organizations seeking to implement effective substitute recommendation systems.

**Jaccard similarity**, one of the most fundamental metrics used in market basket analysis, measures the overlap between two product sets by calculating the intersection divided by the union[1][21][24]. Mathematically, the Jaccard index for two sets A and B equals the number of items in both A and B divided by the total number of distinct items in either A or B[1]. For example, if one product appears in 500 transactions, another product appears in 500 transactions, and they appear together in 450 transactions, their Jaccard similarity would be 450 divided by 550, yielding approximately 0.82[1]. The metric ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating identical sets and 0 indicating no overlap whatsoever[21]. Jaccard similarity possesses several advantages that have led to its widespread adoption in industry applications: it remains interpretable across different contexts, requires only set membership data rather than frequency information, scales efficiently for large datasets through approximation techniques such as MinHash, and serves as an effective baseline metric before deploying more sophisticated approaches[21]. However, Jaccard similarity also exhibits meaningful limitations, particularly its unsuitability for continuous feature data and its inability to capture partial similarities or degrees of relevance without additional adaptation[21].

**Cosine similarity**, derived from the cosine of the angle between two vectors in high-dimensional space, represents an alternative metric particularly powerful for text-based product information and embedded vector representations[17][27]. Cosine similarity ranges from -1 to 1, with 1 indicating vectors pointing in identical directions, 0 indicating orthogonal vectors with no correlation, and -1 indicating vectors pointing in opposite directions[17]. The metric proves especially valuable when products are represented as embeddings derived from product descriptions, reviews, or other textual information[7][17]. Because cosine similarity operates on normalized vectors, it considers only the direction of vectors rather than their magnitude, making it particularly suited for semantic similarity assessment[17]. The metric's bounded range between -1 and 1 avoids numerical overflow issues common with other distance measures and aligns well with embedding algorithms that are often designed with cosine-similarity-based comparisons in mind[17]. Practical implementations frequently use thresholds in the range of 0.5 to 0.8 for cosine similarity, with specific threshold selection depending on the desired balance between coverage and precision[26][56].

**Log-likelihood similarity**, introduced by Ted Dunning, addresses a critical limitation of simpler metrics by accounting for statistical significance and sample size considerations[1]. Traditional metrics like Jaccard can produce misleadingly high similarity scores when rare products happen to co-occur in very few transactions[1]. Dunning's approach recognizes that product frequencies in market basket data follow binomial rather than normal distributions, and it incorporates this understanding into similarity calculations[1]. Log-likelihood similarity proves particularly valuable when organizations need to screen out spurious associations arising from random chance rather than genuine product relationships[1]. For instance, if organic llama meat and black teeth bubble gum each appear in only one transaction and happen to appear together in that transaction, simpler metrics would assign them a Jaccard similarity of 1.0, suggesting a very strong relationship[1]. Log-likelihood similarity appropriately downweights such associations by considering the rarity of both items and the fact that their co-occurrence might easily be explained by chance[1].

**Basket complementarity measures**, developed by Puka and Jedrusik, introduce the concept that true product relationships should be bidirectional and symmetric[2]. This approach defines two items as "basket complementary" when their purchasing probabilities remain close and reasonably high regardless of direction[2]. For example, if customers who buy cornflakes are 40% likely to also buy milk, and customers who buy milk are 33% likely to also buy cornflakes, these products demonstrate two-sided complementarity with modest asymmetry[2]. By allowing tolerance bands around perfect equality, researchers can identify degrees of complementarity and assess product inseparability[2]. This framework proves valuable for substitute identification because true substitutes often exhibit strong asymmetry: customers purchasing a house might be highly likely to purchase a bookcase, but customers purchasing a bookcase are unlikely to purchase a house, reflecting one-sided rather than two-sided complementarity[2].

The choice among these similarity metrics fundamentally shapes what threshold values should be selected. A metric designed for binary set membership data might recommend thresholds in the 0.5 to 0.8 range, while cosine similarity operating on continuous embeddings might employ thresholds of 0.6 to 0.9. Different metrics emphasize different aspects of product relationships, making direct threshold comparisons across metrics problematic. Organizations must first select a metric appropriate to their data characteristics and analytical objectives, then calibrate threshold values specifically for that metric.

## Distinguishing Substitutes from Complements Through Lift and Association Rules

The foundational metrics of support and confidence, while valuable for identifying complementary products, prove insufficient for reliably identifying substitutes. **Lift** represents a critical metric for this distinction because it measures how much more (or less) likely two products are to be purchased together compared to what would be expected if their purchases were independent[8][11][31][34]. Lift is calculated by dividing the probability of purchasing both items by the product of their individual purchase probabilities[8][11].

When lift exceeds 1.0, it indicates a positive association where the presence of one product increases the likelihood of purchasing the other, suggesting complementary products[8][11][34]. For example, a lift value of 1.25 for bread and butter indicates that customers are 25% more likely to purchase bread and butter together than would be expected by random chance[8][11]. Conversely, when lift falls below 1.0, it indicates a negative association where the presence of one product decreases the likelihood of purchasing the other, suggesting substitution relationships[8][11][34][37][40]. A lift value of 0.4 for two competing soft drinks indicates that customers are 60% less likely to purchase both drinks in the same transaction than would be expected if the products were independent, reflecting their substitutive nature[37].

This lift-based approach to identifying substitutes recognizes a fundamental behavioral principle: customers rarely purchase multiple substitute products in the same transaction because substitutes serve essentially identical functions[37][40]. The logical extension of this principle suggests that threshold-setting for substitute identification should focus on identifying products with lift values significantly below 1.0, with the specific threshold depending on the strength of substitution effect desired. A retailer seeking to identify products that rarely appear together might set a lift threshold of 0.5, while a more conservative approach identifying only very strong substitutes might employ a threshold of 0.7[37].

However, lift-based identification of substitutes presents practical challenges when applied to all possible product pairs in large catalogs[37]. The raw number of potential association rules explodes combinatorially, and many rules identified through lift calculations may represent statistical artifacts rather than meaningful product relationships[1]. The "rule explosion problem" occurs when organizations lower support and confidence thresholds to capture more potential relationships, only to generate thousands of rules, most of which lack practical value[1][22]. This challenge has prompted researchers to explore alternative approaches for identifying meaningful substitution relationships while avoiding computational overload.

## Advanced Algorithms for Substitute Product Identification

Beyond traditional market basket analysis algorithms, sophisticated machine learning and network embedding approaches have emerged to identify substitute products with greater accuracy and nuance. These advanced methods leverage rich product information sources and learning mechanisms to overcome limitations of simpler association rule approaches.

**Deep learning-based embedding approaches** represent a significant advancement in substitute identification capability[7]. Rather than treating products as discrete items in transaction baskets, deep learning models learn distributed vector representations (embeddings) that capture product semantics and characteristics[7]. Siamese Networks with Bidirectional LSTM structures can process product textual information such as titles, descriptions, and specifications to learn product embeddings that reflect semantic similarity[7]. These models leverage co-compared data—instances where customers viewed multiple products side-by-side to compare their features—as training signals to learn similarity spaces that match customer preferences[7]. The approach demonstrates substantial improvements over baseline methods, with A/B testing in production environments showing 12% conversion rate improvements compared to hybrid algorithms combining attribute-based and co-purchasing data[7].

The SCEPTRE system (Substitute and Complementary Edges between Products from Topics in Reviews) represents another sophisticated approach that combines topic modeling with supervised link prediction[9][15]. This system models and predicts links between products by extracting topics from product reviews and descriptions, then using logistic regression to predict whether relationships exist[9][15]. SCEPTRE simultaneously learns multiple types of relationships—substitutes and complements—by training a unified model to differentiate between the two rather than training separate models for each relationship type[9][15]. The system leverages product category hierarchies to discover "micro-categories" of closely related products and can operate in cold-start scenarios where review data is unavailable by utilizing alternative data sources such as product metadata[9][15].

**Graph embedding approaches** using algorithms such as Cleora provide another powerful technique for identifying substitute products[35]. These methods learn product embeddings by analyzing transaction networks and product relationships, with the number of embedding iterations determining whether outputs emphasize complementary or substitutable relationships[35]. Single-iteration embeddings highlight complementary products identified by nearest neighbors in the embedding space, while six-iteration embeddings (when the algorithm has converged) identify substitutable products[35]. The approach demonstrates superior performance compared to state-of-the-art alternatives, achieving accuracy improvements of at least 10 times higher than benchmark algorithms while maintaining computational efficiency[35].

**Dual embedding approaches** introduce the concept of separate input and output embedding spaces to capture the directional asymmetry inherent in some product relationships[45]. Unlike complements which should show symmetric relationships in both directions, substitutes often show asymmetry where recommending Product B to someone purchasing Product A makes more sense than recommending Product A to someone purchasing Product B[45]. Dual embeddings capture this asymmetry by learning separate representations that, when combined through different dot product operations, produce recommendations appropriate to the asymmetric nature of substitution relationships[45]. Data augmentation techniques can extend coverage by synthesizing training pairs for products not represented in raw transactional data, with a minimum similarity threshold parameter controlling the quality of synthetic pairs[45].

## Threshold Setting Methodologies and Data-Driven Approaches

Establishing appropriate similarity thresholds for substitute recommendations requires systematic, data-driven methodologies rather than arbitrary selection. Organizations must balance competing objectives: maintaining high precision to ensure recommendations are meaningful and valuable to customers, achieving adequate coverage to provide recommendations for a broad range of products, adapting to different product categories with distinct substitution patterns, and responding dynamically to evolving market conditions and customer preferences.

**Precision-recall tradeoffs** represent the fundamental consideration in threshold selection[25][26][28]. Lower similarity thresholds generate more recommendations (higher recall) but increase the probability of false positives where unrelated products are incorrectly identified as substitutes (lower precision)[25][26]. Higher thresholds produce more conservative recommendations with higher precision but risk missing valid substitution relationships (lower recall)[25][26][28]. The optimal threshold depends on the business context and cost structure: a retailer experiencing stockouts where lost sales from unavailable inventory are costly might tolerate lower precision to achieve higher recall and ensure substitute recommendations are available, while a premium retailer prioritizing customer experience might enforce higher precision thresholds to maintain recommendation quality[26].

Organizations can visualize this tradeoff through precision-recall curves and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves generated by testing various threshold values on labeled datasets where human experts have verified which products are actually substitutes[25][26][28]. The optimal threshold often corresponds to the point where the F-measure (harmonic mean of precision and recall) is maximized, though some applications might select thresholds that optimize for specific business metrics rather than the F-measure[25].

**Validation dataset approaches** involve creating labeled datasets where substitute relationships have been verified through expert judgment or historical transaction analysis[26][56]. Organizations can then test different threshold values against these labeled datasets, calculating precision, recall, and other evaluation metrics for each threshold[26]. For instance, a testing protocol might evaluate thresholds of 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, and 0.8 on cosine similarity scores, observing how precision and diversity metrics change across threshold values[56]. Research examining similarity thresholds in recommendation systems found that lower thresholds (0.5) achieved higher individual diversity in recommendations while higher thresholds (0.8) achieved higher precision but lower diversity[56]. The optimal threshold selection depends on whether the primary objective is maximizing diversity to expose customers to varied products or maintaining high precision for recommendation reliability.

**Quartile-based relative scoring** approaches provide an alternative methodology particularly suitable when organizations lack extensive labeled datasets[23]. This approach sorts all items by their similarity scores and assigns scores based on quartile position rather than absolute similarity values[23]. Items in the highest quartile receive scores of 4, second-highest quartile items receive scores of 3, and so forth[23]. This relative approach adapts automatically to the distribution of similarity scores in the specific dataset and business context, eliminating the need to specify absolute threshold values that might prove inappropriate for different product categories or market conditions[23].

## Handling Asymmetry and Directionality in Substitute Relationships

A fundamental characteristic of many substitute product relationships is their asymmetry or directionality—the probability or suitability of recommending Product B as a substitute for Product A may differ substantially from recommending Product A as a substitute for Product B. This asymmetry reflects differences in product characteristics such as price, quality, brand reputation, and customer perception.

**One-sided versus two-sided complementarity frameworks**, while developed for analyzing complement relationships, provide valuable conceptual foundations for understanding asymmetry in substitutes[2]. Two-sided complementarity occurs when products share modest asymmetry and approximately equal bidirectional purchasing probabilities, while one-sided complementarity involves large asymmetries where the relationship flows primarily in one direction[2]. For substitutes, similar frameworks apply: a house and a bookcase might exhibit one-sided substitution characteristics where houses could substitute for bookcases in limited contexts, but bookcases cannot reasonably substitute for houses[2]. Implementing asymmetric threshold approaches requires systems capable of learning and storing directional relationships rather than treating all relationships as bidirectional symmetric connections.

Organizations can address asymmetry by establishing multiple directed threshold values rather than a single symmetric threshold[2]. For instance, a system might accept Product B as a substitute for Product A when cosine similarity exceeds 0.7, while requiring cosine similarity above 0.8 for the reverse recommendation. This approach acknowledges the reality that some recommendations make stronger sense than others while maintaining the ability to generate meaningful suggestions in both directions when sufficient similarity exists.

**Tolerance band approaches** in the basket complementarity framework establish acceptable ranges around perfect bidirectional equality[2]. Products whose bidirectional purchasing probabilities remain within narrow tolerance bands (such as within 1-5%) are considered "inseparable" and represent the strongest substitution candidates, while products outside these bands but still showing reasonable similarity within broader tolerance ranges represent weaker or more asymmetric substitutes[2]. This framework enables organizations to classify substitute relationships into multiple strength categories, allowing different recommendation strategies for different relationship strengths.

## Statistical Significance Testing and Multiple Comparison Corrections

Identifying meaningful substitute relationships from large product databases presents significant statistical challenges. When examining many thousands of potential product pairs, statistically significant associations will occur by random chance alone, creating false positive errors that undermine recommendation quality.

**Log-likelihood ratio tests**, grounded in proper statistical theory, provide rigorous approaches to assessing statistical significance of association rules[1][37]. Rather than treating all associations equally, log-likelihood tests weight associations by the strength of evidence supporting them relative to what would be expected by chance[1]. The approach recognizes that an association supported by hundreds of observations deserves different treatment than an association supported by only a few observations[1]. Specifically, associations between rare products that happen to appear together face downweighting because their co-occurrence could easily be explained by random chance[1][37].

**Multiple comparison corrections** become essential when testing thousands of product pairs against significance thresholds. Without corrections, false positive rates escalate dramatically as the number of tests increases—testing 10,000 product pairs at a 0.05 significance level would expect roughly 500 false positives by random chance alone. Standard corrections such as Bonferroni adjustment (dividing the significance threshold by the number of tests) prove overly conservative for market basket analysis, reducing power to detect true substitution relationships. More sophisticated approaches such as false discovery rate (FDR) control balance power against false positive control by focusing on limiting the proportion of false positives among significant results rather than limiting absolute false positive numbers.

## Implementation Considerations and Practical Adjustments

Successfully implementing substitute recommendation systems with appropriate similarity thresholds requires navigating numerous practical considerations beyond pure statistical optimization.

**Product category heterogeneity** necessitates category-specific threshold calibration[27][30]. Different product categories exhibit substantially different substitution patterns: grocery products might show clear substitution relationships where different cereal brands compete directly, while electronics might show less obvious substitution relationships where brand differentiation and feature variation are greater. Category-specific thresholds allow systems to reflect these differences, employing perhaps 0.6 as the cosine similarity threshold for grocery products while using 0.75 for electronics[27]. Some sophisticated systems learn separate threshold models for different product categories rather than enforcing a single global threshold[26].

**Price delta thresholds** recognize that products differing radically in price rarely serve as true substitutes despite potential name or feature similarity[27][30]. A system might exclude from consideration any product pairs where price differences exceed a specified percentage, such as 50% or 100%, preventing recommendations of luxury products as substitutes for budget alternatives and vice versa[27][30]. These price filters work in conjunction with similarity thresholds rather than replacing them, providing additional dimension for ensuring recommendation appropriateness.

**Cold-start problems** challenge substitute recommendation systems when new products enter the catalog[45]. New products have limited transaction history, review data, or purchase-based co-occurrence information, making it difficult to identify appropriate substitutes using purely collaborative approaches. **Inference augmentation strategies** address this by retrieving the most similar existing product from the training set (based on similarity functions that may incorporate product metadata, descriptions, or images), then identifying substitutes for that product as candidate substitutes for the new product[45]. This approach enables recommendations for new products while they accumulate historical data.

**Feedback loops and continuous refinement** enable systems to adapt threshold values based on actual recommendation performance[26][52]. When users report that recommendations are inappropriate or when conversion data shows recommendations are not driving desired behaviors, systems can adjust threshold values within specified ranges to improve performance. Some advanced systems implement A/B tests comparing different threshold values in production environments, with the best-performing threshold selected for broader deployment[26]. This empirical approach complements theoretical threshold optimization by validating that thresholds produce desired business outcomes.

## Cross-Basket and Temporal Dimensions of Substitute Analysis

Modern substitute recommendation systems increasingly incorporate temporal and cross-basket dimensions that traditional single-transaction analysis cannot capture.

**Temporal affinity scores** quantify the strength of product relationships across specific time intervals[3]. Rather than simply identifying that Product A and B never appear in the same transaction, temporal analysis reveals that customers purchasing Product A are likely to purchase Product B within 30, 60, or 90 days following their initial purchase[3]. This temporal information suggests product substitution or complementarity relationships driven by lifecycle factors rather than immediate need—customers purchasing organic baby food might be likely to purchase premium skincare products as their lifestyle transitions[3]. Threshold values for temporal relationships can be established independently from transaction-level thresholds, recognizing that temporal patterns may require higher or lower similarity values depending on the specific business context.

**Channel migration probabilities** in omnichannel retail environments measure the likelihood of customers switching between purchase channels for substitute products[3]. A customer might purchase Brand A through a physical store but order Brand B through online channels, revealing a channel-specific substitution behavior. These channel-aware insights enable more nuanced recommendations that account for customer channel preferences and behavior patterns across channels[3].

**Lifecycle value correlations** link product substitution patterns to customer lifetime value predictions[3]. Rather than treating all substitute relationships equally, systems can weigh substitution relationships more heavily when they occur between products that drive customer lifetime value and weight them less when they occur between low-value products[3]. A retailer discovering that customers substituting premium product A for lower-cost product B experience reduced lifetime value would appropriately treat this substitution differently than one showing no lifetime value impact.

## Threshold Optimization for Diverse Recommendation Contexts

Different business applications and recommendation contexts require distinct threshold optimization strategies reflecting their unique objectives and constraints.

The business objective—ensuring that substitute products are available when preferred products stock out—tolerates some false negatives where actual substitutes are missed, as long as recommendations are sufficiently reliable.

**Promotional and marketing applications** might employ lower similarity thresholds (such as 0.5 cosine similarity) to generate a broader array of candidate substitutes for promotional consideration[39][52]. Marketing teams might intentionally promote substitutes that customers might not naturally consider, expanding market share through product trial. The lower precision threshold reflects willingness to tolerate some false positives in exchange for broader coverage and customer discovery opportunities.

**Personalized recommendation applications** might employ higher similarity thresholds (such as 0.75-0.8 cosine similarity) reflecting the understanding that personalized recommendations reach individual customers who may find lower-precision suggestions frustrating rather than beneficial[26][56]. The premium placed on customer satisfaction experience justifies accepting lower recall to maintain higher precision.

**Real-time operational systems** must balance threshold selection with computational and latency constraints[10][29]. Graph database approaches excel at handling flexible threshold adjustments by allowing expanded threshold bands when insufficient results are available within strict thresholds, providing users with complete recommendation sets without operational delay[10][29]. This adaptability ensures consistent user experience across diverse product catalog regions.
