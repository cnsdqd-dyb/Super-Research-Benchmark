# Ocean Heat Redistribution Parameterization and Its Impact on Seasonal Prediction Skill for ENSO Amplitude

The accurate prediction of El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) amplitude on seasonal timescales hinges critically on how ocean models represent the redistribution of heat within the tropical Pacific system. Parameterization schemes—mathematical approximations used in climate models to represent subgrid-scale processes that cannot be explicitly resolved—play a pivotal role in determining whether forecasts capture the true intensity of ENSO events. Recent research demonstrates that improvements in how these parameterizations treat vertical mixing, thermocline feedback, heat transport, and related processes can substantially enhance seasonal prediction skill for ENSO amplitude, with some approaches reducing cold tongue biases by up to 30 percent and extending skillful forecast horizons by several months. This comprehensive review examines how various parameterization strategies affect ENSO amplitude predictability, synthesizing evidence from dynamical models, data-driven approaches, and ensemble systems to illuminate the mechanistic pathways through which parameterization accuracy translates into forecast skill.

## Fundamental Ocean Dynamics and the Role of Heat Redistribution in ENSO

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation represents the most prominent mode of interannual climate variability originating from coupled ocean-atmosphere interactions in the tropical Pacific Ocean[1][53]. At its core, ENSO involves substantial variations in sea surface temperature (SST), subsurface thermal structure, sea level anomalies, and surface winds that interact through positive and negative feedback mechanisms[1][2]. The equatorial Pacific thermocline—the layer of water with the strongest vertical stratification separating warm surface waters from cooler deep waters—plays an absolutely critical role in ENSO development and amplitude determination. During El Niño events, anomalous westerly winds in the western and central Pacific trigger oceanic Kelvin waves that propagate eastward, causing the thermocline to deepen in the eastern Pacific and reducing upwelling of cold water to the surface[1][20]. This shoaling of cold water is opposed by the thermocline feedback, which represents the vertical advection of subsurface temperature anomalies beneath the mixed layer[8][44].

The accurate representation of heat redistribution processes fundamentally determines whether a model captures the correct ENSO amplitude because these processes govern how perturbations in one region of the tropical Pacific translate into surface temperature anomalies elsewhere. Ocean models must parameterize several classes of heat redistribution mechanisms that operate below the scale of explicit model resolution. These include turbulent vertical mixing in the ocean surface boundary layer, the transport of heat by submesoscale eddies and tropical instability waves, the advection of temperature anomalies by mean and anomalous currents, and the response of the thermocline to wind forcing[19][49][50]. When these parameterizations are inaccurate or poorly tuned to tropical conditions, they introduce systematic biases that degrade ENSO amplitude predictions. The cold tongue bias—an excessive cooling of the equatorial Pacific that develops in many climate models—exemplifies how parameterization deficiencies cascade into forecast errors[31]. This bias arises partly from parameterizations that overestimate vertical mixing or misrepresent thermocline feedback, causing models to produce SST anomalies that are systematically too weak or spatially displaced[31].

## Traditional Parameterization Approaches and Their Limitations in Tropical Systems

Operational seasonal forecast systems have historically relied on parameterization schemes developed and calibrated using observations from mid-latitude oceans, where different physical regimes govern vertical mixing and heat transport[18][19][49]. The K-profile parameterization (KPP) scheme, adopted by more than one-third of the climate models participating in the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP), exemplifies this challenge[19][49]. The KPP scheme significantly overestimates downward turbulent heat flux in the Pacific cold tongue region, where the vertical temperature structure and stratification differ markedly from mid-latitude conditions[19]. This overestimation translates directly into excessively strong damping of surface temperature anomalies, reducing the growth rate of ENSO perturbations and producing forecasts with weaker amplitude than observed[19][31].

The mean climatological state of the equatorial Pacific simulated by models using traditional parameterizations exhibits a pronounced cold bias that intensifies with forecast lead time[31]. Studies of models like CESM1 reveal that this cold bias develops particularly rapidly during boreal summer and autumn, when the thermocline-SST feedback strengthens seasonally[31]. The mechanism linking parameterization deficiencies to this bias involves errors in the simulation of vertical temperature gradients and thermal stratification in the upper equatorial Pacific[31]. When vertical mixing parameterizations such as KPP produce excessive diffusivity in the tropical Pacific, they artificially enhance the downward penetration of surface heat anomalies and damp the development of strong subsurface thermal gradients[19]. This weakens the thermocline feedback—the fundamental mechanism by which subsurface heat content changes propagate to the surface and drive ENSO amplitude[5][8].

The implications for ENSO amplitude prediction are profound. Models with pronounced cold tongue biases systematically underestimate both the amplitude and the spatial extent of ENSO-driven SST anomalies[31]. Forecast errors in ENSO amplitude become most strongly associated with errors in both the thermocline slope response and surface wind response to SST forcing over the tropical Pacific[5]. This occurs because an overly diffuse thermocline with weak vertical temperature gradients produces a weaker feedback between wind anomalies and thermocline displacement, reducing the system's sensitivity to perturbations[5]. Studies employing the Bjerknes coupled stability (BJ) index analysis reveal that enhanced errors in ENSO amplitude with increasing forecast lead times are well represented by errors in the growth rate estimated from feedback analysis[5]. The upper ocean temperature bias in the equatorial Pacific, which becomes more intense with increasing lead time, emerges as the primary cause of forecast errors in thermocline feedback and thus in ENSO amplitude prediction[5].

## Data-Driven Parameterization Advances and Neural Network Approaches

Recent breakthroughs in machine learning offer novel solutions to the parameterization challenge by training artificial neural networks on direct observations of turbulent mixing processes in the tropical Pacific[19][49]. Rather than relying on theoretical frameworks that may not accurately represent tropical physics, neural network-based parameterizations learn the actual relationships between large-scale ocean variables and the resulting vertical eddy diffusivity from decade-long time records of high-resolution hydrographic and turbulence observations[19][49]. When implemented in ocean-only models, neural network-based parameterizations demonstrate substantially improved performance compared to traditional schemes. In particular, the neural network approach predicts vertical eddy diffusivity values that agree much more closely with direct observations, showing significantly better representation of mixing above the Equatorial Undercurrent core[19][49].

The impact of neural network-based parameterizations on temperature simulation demonstrates the practical importance of these advances. In ocean-only simulations, the warm bias above approximately 120 meters and the cold bias below this depth—characteristic problems in ocean model simulations—are notably reduced when neural network parameterizations replace traditional KPP schemes[19]. Specifically, warm biases between 60 and 80 meters can be reduced by approximately 44 percent when the neural network parameterization is employed[19]. This improvement arises because the neural network produces more realistic turbulent heat fluxes that better balance the competing effects of different mixing processes in the tropical environment. The generalization ability of these parameterizations has been verified under physical constraints, ensuring that they represent physically plausible mixing processes rather than merely fitting noise in the training data[19][49].

When neural network parameterizations are integrated into coupled climate models, they continue to improve simulations despite the greater complexity introduced by atmosphere-ocean coupling[19]. The coupled simulations show a reduction of the Pacific cold tongue bias by approximately 30 percent when the neural network approach replaces KPP[19]. This is particularly significant because the cold tongue bias directly impacts ENSO simulation and prediction. Models with weaker cold biases produce more realistic ENSO amplitude and spatial patterns, which translates into improved seasonal forecast skill[31]. The mechanism linking improved parameterization to reduced cold tongue bias involves more accurate simulation of the vertical temperature structure and the thermocline depth, which governs how efficiently upwelling brings cold water to the surface[19].

## Advanced Global Models with Optimized Parameterizations

The development of data-driven global ocean models like ORCA-DL represents the frontier of parameterization optimization, incorporating multiple advanced techniques to capture heat redistribution processes with unprecedented fidelity[1][2][7]. ORCA-DL achieves skillful ENSO predictions across three dimensions and multiple key variables by integrating reanalysis data spanning four decades with century-long simulations from dozens of climate models[1][2]. The model demonstrates superior performance in predicting ENSO amplitude compared to state-of-the-art dynamical models and ensemble systems, achieving a correlation skill of 0.75 at a 12-month lead time, effectively overcoming the spring predictability barrier[1][2].

The success of ORCA-DL in capturing ENSO amplitude reflects its ability to accurately represent subsurface dynamics and thermocline variations. Detailed analysis of temporal evolution in the Niño3.4 region from the surface to 500 meters depth reveals that subsurface temperature anomalies exhibit alternating patterns of warm and cold phases along the thermocline[1]. These patterns are associated with the deepening and shallowing of the thermocline and reflect the propagation of equatorial Kelvin and Rossby waves—crucial mechanisms for ENSO development and termination[1]. ORCA-DL captures these subsurface signals at least at 6- and 12-month lead times, providing earlier indications of ENSO events that serve as reliable sources for prediction[1]. The model's success demonstrates that data-driven approaches can encode the complex parameterizations of heat redistribution processes implicitly through pattern recognition across multiple spatial and temporal scales.

ORCA-DL also shows superior skill than comparison models in predicting the Central Pacific (CP) Niño4 index and the Eastern Pacific (EP) Niño3 index, suggesting better forecasts of ENSO's spatial diversity and the different flavors of El Niño events[1][2]. This capability reveals how accurate parameterization of heat redistribution processes enables models to capture not merely ENSO amplitude but also the spatial structure of amplitude variations. The different mechanisms governing Central Pacific and Eastern Pacific ENSO events rely differentially on thermocline feedback versus zonal advective feedback[8][44][47]. Data-driven models with optimized parameterizations can capture these mechanistic differences more accurately than traditional models that rely on simplified or poorly calibrated schemes[1].

## Parameter Estimation and Its Impact on ENSO Amplitude Prediction

Parameter estimation methods offer an alternative approach to improving ENSO amplitude predictions by directly tuning critical parameterization coefficients based on observational constraints within the data assimilation system[13]. Studies employing parameter estimation with the CESM coupled model show that reducing uncertainty in ocean vertical mixing parameters and improving the quality of analysis through better initial conditions can significantly enhance ENSO forecast skill[13]. When parameter estimation is applied to estimate critical parameters in vertical mixing parameterization, the resulting forecasts show notably improved accuracy for ENSO amplitude compared to cases using only state estimation without parameter adjustment[13].

The mechanism linking parameter estimation to improved ENSO amplitude predictions involves better representation of subsurface thermal structure during initialization[13]. Parameter estimation reduces analysis error within the thermocline of the equatorial Pacific, yielding improved initial conditions for ensemble prediction[13]. Simultaneously, the refined parameters contribute to enhanced global vertical mixing that more accurately represents the background diffusivity processes[13]. Consequently, predicted SST anomalies in the tropical Pacific derived from forecasts incorporating parameter estimation exhibit higher correlation with observational data[13]. The improvement in ENSO forecasts is likely attributable to better simulations of subsurface temperatures, as the accuracy of initial subsurface conditions has been consistently shown as crucial for ENSO event prediction[13].

Notably, parameter estimation can extend effective ENSO prediction skill beyond what state estimation alone achieves. For lead times exceeding five months, parameter estimation cases exhibit higher anomaly correlation coefficients compared to state estimation cases[13]. By setting an effective prediction threshold at an anomaly correlation coefficient of 0.5 (equivalent to 99 percent statistical confidence level with an independent sample size of 30), the state estimation case can effectively predict ENSO at lead times up to nine months, whereas parameter estimation demonstrates comparable or superior performance[13]. This extension of skillful forecast range, even if marginal in some cases, reflects the cumulative impact of more accurate parameterization throughout the forecast integration period.

## Tropical Instability Waves and Nonlinear Heat Redistribution Feedback

Beyond the primary vertical mixing and thermocline feedback processes, the parameterization of tropical instability waves (TIWs) represents an increasingly recognized source of ENSO amplitude prediction error[14][29]. Tropical instability waves are the dominant form of eddy variability in the tropics with typical periods of approximately 5 weeks, and their activity is strongly modulated by both the seasonal cycle and ENSO itself[14][29]. TIWs transport heat down the meridional temperature gradient through nonlinear advection processes, a mechanism known as nonlinear dynamical heating (NDH)[14][29]. This process acts as an asymmetric negative feedback on ENSO by damping the growth of SST anomalies, particularly on the equatorward flank of the SST front in the eastern equatorial Pacific[14][29].

The interannual variability of TIW-induced heat flux and NDH can be approximated in terms of the mean meridional temperature gradient and depends on background ENSO conditions[14][29]. Most climate models simulating ENSO asymmetry poorly because they underestimate NDH and produce too-weak linear dynamical ocean-atmosphere coupling[29]. Models with relatively realistic NDH show that El Niño-likeness of the equatorial Pacific warming pattern is linearly related to ENSO amplitude change in response to greenhouse warming[29]. Therefore, better simulating the dynamics of ENSO asymmetry through improved TIW parameterization potentially reduces uncertainty in both seasonal predictions and long-term projections[14][29].

Parameterization schemes for TIW-induced heat flux have been developed based on the theoretical framework of ENSO-TIWs interaction, expressing the effective heat flux as proportional to the local slowly-varying meridional temperature gradient modulated by the EEP annual cycle and ENSO conditions[14][29]. These schemes can realistically represent TIWs-induced heat effect on ENSO without requiring bandpass filtering to extract high-frequency TIWs signals, making them suitable for online application in theoretical and intermediate complexity ENSO models[14][29]. The ability to accurately parameterize TIW feedback directly impacts predictions of ENSO amplitude, as this feedback modulates how efficiently SST anomalies grow and decay during different phases of the oscillation[14][29].

## Cold Tongue Bias as a Central Challenge in Parameterization and ENSO Amplitude Prediction

The cold tongue bias—systematically simulated SST that is too cold across the equatorial eastern Pacific—emerges as the most critical manifestation of parameterization deficiencies for ENSO amplitude prediction[31]. The cold tongue bias in CESM1 forecasts develops faster during boreal summer-autumn than other seasons due to the strengthening of vertical temperature advection in the eastern equatorial Pacific[31]. Importantly, this bias is influenced by initialization shock—imbalances created by data assimilation between initial conditions and model dynamics—which compounds parameterization-induced biases[31]. Different initialization months show different magnitudes of cold tongue bias, with March and June-initialized forecasts displaying more pronounced cooling biases than November-initialized forecasts[31].

The cold tongue bias affects not only the pattern and amplitude but also the duration of ENSO in forecasts by altering ocean-atmosphere feedbacks[31]. When the cold tongue bias is strong, predicted ENSO SST anomalies extend anomalously far into the western equatorial Pacific during boreal summer, while predicted anomalies are too weak in the central-eastern equatorial Pacific[31]. These forecast errors of both pattern and amplitude subsequently lead to errors in ENSO phase transition by affecting the amplitude of the negative thermocline feedback[31]. The forecast errors further degrade predictions of wintertime atmospheric teleconnections, land surface air temperature, and rainfall anomalies over the Northern Hemisphere—downstream impacts of ENSO that depend critically on accurate amplitude prediction[31].

The origins of cold tongue bias in forecasts can be decomposed into three components: the inherent climatological bias of the underlying model, imbalance between initial conditions and model dynamics introduced during initialization, and errors in the ocean initial conditions themselves[31]. The strong seasonality of thermocline-SST feedback in the equatorial eastern Pacific means that the relative importance of these components varies with initialization month[31]. Models with stronger cold tongue biases tend to have systematically weaker Bjerknes feedback, implying that their parameterizations produce excessive damping of the air-sea coupling that drives ENSO growth[31]. This demonstrates that parameterization-induced biases in the climatological state directly translate into degraded ENSO amplitude prediction through altered feedback strengths.

## Thermocline Depth, Sea Surface Height, and the Central Pacific Complexity

Recent discoveries about the relationship between thermocline depth and subsurface temperature in the central equatorial Pacific have revealed important complications for parameterization and ENSO amplitude prediction[8][11][37][44][47]. In the central equatorial Pacific, ENSO subsurface temperature correlates poorly with thermocline depth but well with sea surface height anomalies—a relationship that differs from the eastern Pacific where thermocline and SST are strongly coupled[8][11][44][47]. This observed difference occurs in nearly all CMIP6 models and arises from a unique vertically slanted distribution of subsurface temperature anomalies in the central Pacific in response to ENSO winds[8][11][44][47].

The vertically slanted temperature structure in the central Pacific reflects an adiabatic response to wind forcing that is even present in linear dynamical models, provided the model includes enough baroclinic modes to adequately represent the observed vertical complexity[8][11][44][47]. High-order vertical modes (modes 3-8) are responsible for generating this vertically slanted response, whereas models with only low-order modes (1-2) fail to reproduce the observed structure[8][44]. This finding has profound implications for parameterization and ENSO amplitude prediction, as it indicates that the traditional 1.5-layer reduced gravity framework underlying some parameterization schemes cannot capture the full complexity of heat redistribution in the central Pacific[8][44][47].

The difference in thermocline feedback effectiveness between Central Pacific and Eastern Pacific ENSO events reflects these structural differences[8][44]. The thermocline feedback is weaker and less effective in the central Pacific than in the eastern Pacific, which leaves room for other influences on central Pacific SST, particularly the zonal advective feedback mechanism[8][44]. Parameterizations that do not explicitly represent the vertical complexity necessary to generate the vertically slanted response may systematically mispredict the amplitude of Central Pacific ENSO events relative to Eastern Pacific events[8][44]. This mechanistic insight emphasizes that accurate ENSO amplitude prediction requires parameterizations sophisticated enough to encode the full 3D structure of heat redistribution, not merely simplified vertical profiles[8][44].

## The Spring Predictability Barrier and Parameterization-Related Sources

The spring predictability barrier—a universal reduction in ENSO forecast skill during boreal spring (May-June-July)—has multiple roots, some of which relate directly to parameterization deficiencies[23][30][40]. The enhanced chaotic degree of ENSO systems during spring leads to more rapid growth of initial errors in forecast models, potentially related to how parameterizations represent seasonal changes in ocean-atmosphere coupling strength[23][40]. The chaotic degree of the ENSO system, which measures the sensitivity of forecasts to initial condition perturbations, increases during spring, suggesting that parameterizations produce seasonal variations in system stability[23]. This seasonal modulation of system sensitivity indicates that parameterizations must vary with season to maintain accuracy across the annual cycle[23].

Sea surface salinity (SSS) has emerged as another factor affecting ENSO spring predictability barrier through mechanisms related to water mass stratification and mixing parameterization[30]. The influence of SSS on spring predictability appears to operate through its effects on upper ocean stratification and the efficiency of vertical mixing[30]. Parameterizations that do not adequately represent salinity effects on density and hence vertical stratification may mispredict how efficiently wind forcing can induce vertical mixing and thermocline displacement during spring[30]. This suggests that improvements to parameterizations of haline stratification and its interaction with thermal stratification may help mitigate the spring predictability barrier for ENSO amplitude[30].

Diffusion model approaches to ENSO forecasting, which learn probabilistic representations of ENSO evolution directly from data, reveal that the spring barrier manifests as a pronounced uncertainty surge before the barrier onset[15]. This model demonstrates the ability to generate large ensembles that objectively quantify evolving forecast uncertainty and detect the uncertainty surge weeks to months before the barrier takes effect[15]. The identification of such early warning signals for the spring barrier may allow operational forecasters to downweight predictions during vulnerable periods and could inform efforts to improve parameterizations specifically for spring conditions[15].

## Initialization Strategies and Their Interaction with Parameterization

The initialization process by which ocean data assimilation creates balanced initial conditions fundamentally interacts with parameterization schemes in determining ENSO amplitude prediction skill[18][32][50][53]. Data assimilation creates initial conditions using the same model parameterizations that will be used in the forecast, so deficiencies in parameterizations propagate directly from analysis to forecasts[18][32][50]. Initialization shock—the degradation of forecast skill that occurs when moving from the data assimilation phase to the coupled forecast phase—arises partly from parameterization-induced imbalances between initial ocean and atmosphere states[32]. When parameterizations produce systematically biased representations of ocean state, assimilating observations using such biased models can produce initial conditions that are unbalanced with respect to model dynamics[32][50].

Coupled data assimilation, which adjusts both atmosphere and ocean states through their coupled interactions during the assimilation phase, can reduce initialization shock compared to uncoupled approaches[32][53]. This occurs because coupled assimilation allows parameterization-based feedback relationships to generate consistent initial conditions without imposing artificial imbalances[32][53]. The ensemble spread generated by coupled data assimilation includes flow-dependent sources of spread related to parameterization uncertainty, including decadal, interannual, and intraseasonal variability[32][53]. In contrast, uncoupled data assimilation produces ensemble spread that only captures seasonal dependence through forcing and SST perturbations[32][53].

The treatment of model error during initialization also interacts with parameterization accuracy[32]. Data assimilation systems that explicitly account for model error through bias correction can partially compensate for parameterization deficiencies during initialization[32]. However, such post-hoc bias correction may mask rather than solve fundamental parameterization problems that continue to degrade forecast skill during the integration[32]. Target observations designed to reduce sensitivity to specific forms of initial error can improve ENSO amplitude prediction by focusing observational resources on the most sensitive regions[50]. Studies identifying conditional nonlinear optimal perturbations show that initial errors in the eastern equatorial Pacific produce the largest prediction uncertainties for El Niño amplitude[50]. This suggests that parameterizations affecting how models simulate eastern Pacific heat redistribution deserve particular attention and refinement[50].

Deep learning models designed specifically for ENSO prediction offer insights into how optimal representation of heat redistribution processes translates into improved amplitude skill[15][21][27]. This extended forecast horizon substantially exceeds typical dynamical model performance and reflects the ability of deep learning to identify complex spatial-temporal patterns of heat redistribution that govern ENSO amplitude[21]. The root mean square error remains reasonably low even for long-lead forecasts, around 0.7°C, lower than typical dynamical ENSO forecast model errors[21].

Sensitivity experiments with the STIEF model reveal that ocean interbasin and tropical-extratropic interactions are critical for ENSO forecasts, suggesting that effective parameterization must account for how heat anomalies are redistributed not only within the tropical Pacific but also among ocean basins and between tropics and extratropics[21]. Adding ocean heat content information extends the effective forecast length from 18 to 23 months, demonstrating that models capturing multiple aspects of heat redistribution produce superior amplitude predictions[21]. The model shows good forecast performance with minimal SPB influence because it learns the seasonal structure of predictability from training data rather than relying on potentially biased parameterizations[21].

The ENSO-ASC model, incorporating a multivariate air-sea coupler based on graph neural network architecture, achieves correlation skill of the Niño 3.4 index over 0.78, 0.65, and 0.5 within lead times of 6, 12, and 18 months respectively[27]. The model captures the latent ENSO dynamical mechanisms and provides multivariate evolution simulations with high physical consistency, reproducing the positive SST anomalies that first appear over the eastern equatorial Pacific with westerly wind anomalies in the western and central tropical Pacific[27]. This pattern reflects the Bjerknes positive feedback mechanism—the fundamental driver of ENSO amplitude growth[27]. The multivariate air-sea coupler effectively captures spatial-temporal multi-scale oscillations of the Walker circulation and performs ocean-atmosphere energy exchange simultaneously[27], demonstrating how neural networks can implicitly encode sophisticated parameterizations of heat redistribution through coupled air-sea interactions.

## Future Perspectives: Toward More Sophisticated Parameterizations

The frontier of parameterization development includes several promising directions for further enhancing ENSO amplitude prediction skill. The explicit incorporation of radiative forcing would enhance the physical realism of seasonal cycle projections, allowing data-driven models to directly simulate shifts in seasonal cycle intensity driven by external forcings[1][2]. This capability would improve ENSO amplitude predictions by allowing models to account for how anthropogenic climate change modulates background ocean state and the seasonal cycle in ways that affect ENSO growth rates and saturation amplitudes[1][2]. Transfer learning techniques that leverage simulations from high-resolution models, in situ turbulence observations, and large eddy simulations could construct more robust parameterizations of oceanic mixing by turbulence[49].

The spatial distribution of parameter sensitivity offers an untapped frontier for parameterization improvement[13]. Rather than assuming uniform parameterization coefficients globally or within broad regions, sensitivity analysis reveals grid-point-specific patterns of which parameterization choices most strongly influence local ENSO-relevant dynamics[13]. Future parameterization schemes might exploit this information to optimize coefficients spatially, allowing the model to adapt its representation of vertical mixing and heat redistribution to local oceanographic conditions[13]. Such approaches would require increased computational expense but could yield substantial skill improvements for ENSO amplitude prediction in a changing climate where background ocean state continues to evolve[13].

The integration of machine learning-discovered parameterizations into traditional dynamical models presents another avenue for future development[15][19][49]. Rather than replacing traditional models entirely with data-driven systems, hybrid approaches could use neural networks to parameterize specific subgrid-scale processes while retaining the explicit simulation of large-scale dynamics using traditional differential equations[15][19][49]. This hybrid strategy combines the physical interpretability and long-term stability of dynamical models with the pattern recognition capabilities and superior accuracy of neural networks[15][19][49]. Early applications of this approach to ENSO prediction show promise, with diffusion models learning probabilistic representations of ENSO evolution that capture complex nonlinear dynamics while remaining interpretable in terms of physical processes[15].

The development of parameterizations tailored specifically to different ENSO flavors—Eastern Pacific versus Central Pacific events—could improve amplitude prediction by allowing models to apply different representations of heat redistribution depending on which ENSO regime develops[1][47]. The mechanistic differences between these regimes, involving different relative importance of thermocline feedback versus zonal advective feedback, suggest that optimal parameterizations might vary with ENSO type[1][47]. Models capable of predicting not only ENSO amplitude but also the spatial structure of that amplitude, as demonstrated by ORCA-DL predicting both Niño3.4 and Niño4 indices, implicitly employ parameterizations sophisticated enough to represent this spatial complexity[1][47].

## Conclusion: Bridging Parameterization Science and Operational Prediction Skill

The parameterization of ocean heat redistribution fundamentally determines seasonal prediction skill for ENSO amplitude through multiple pathways involving vertical mixing, thermocline feedback, tropical instability wave effects, and the three-dimensional structure of subsurface temperature anomalies. Traditional parameterizations like KPP, calibrated for mid-latitude conditions, systematically misrepresent tropical mixing processes and produce the cold tongue bias that degrades ENSO amplitude predictions[19][31]. Neural network-based parameterizations trained on tropical turbulence observations demonstrate substantial improvements, reducing temperature biases by 30-44 percent and improving ENSO amplitude representation[19]. Data-driven global models like ORCA-DL show that optimized parameterization of heat redistribution processes enables skillful ENSO amplitude prediction extending to 24-month lead times, substantially beyond the capabilities of traditional dynamical systems[1][2].

The mechanistic understanding of how parameterization deficiencies degrade ENSO amplitude prediction has advanced significantly through recent research, revealing critical roles for vertical mixing parameterization in controlling thermocline feedback strength, tropical instability wave parameterization in modulating nonlinear ENSO asymmetry, and the vertical mode structure affecting central Pacific ENSO predictions[8][14][31][44][49]. Parameter estimation methods and coupled initialization approaches that account for parameterization-induced imbalances show promise for further improvements[13][32][53]. The spring predictability barrier, which limits ENSO amplitude forecast skill, appears partly related to seasonal variations in parameterization-dependent feedback mechanisms that can be identified and potentially mitigated through careful model development[23][30][40].

Looking forward, the integration of physics-informed machine learning approaches with traditional dynamical modeling offers a pathway toward substantially enhanced ENSO amplitude prediction skill. Such hybrid systems could combine the physical interpretability and long-term stability of dynamical models with the superior accuracy of neural network parameterizations, creating forecasting systems that are both more skillful and more trustworthy to operational users. As climate continues to change, parameterizations must be continuously evaluated and refined to maintain prediction skill in a non-stationary climate, highlighting the importance of sustained efforts to understand and improve how ocean models represent the fundamental processes governing heat redistribution in the tropical Pacific. The evidence presented throughout this review demonstrates that investments in parameterization science yield direct operational benefits through enhanced seasonal prediction skill for ENSO amplitude and hence improved climate information for decision-makers worldwide.
