# Heparin Dosage Management in ECMO Support: Balancing Thromboembolism and Bleeding Risk

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) represents a life-saving intervention for patients with severe cardiopulmonary failure, yet the need for systemic anticoagulation to prevent circuit thrombosis creates a complex clinical challenge. When blood contacts the non-biologic surfaces of the ECMO circuit, a cascade of prothrombotic reactions occurs, necessitating anticoagulation to maintain circuit patency[1][4]. Paradoxically, the same disease processes affecting critically ill ECMO candidates often create heightened bleeding risk, and the anticoagulation itself increases hemorrhagic complications. Heparin dosage management during ECMO thus requires careful titration to prevent both thrombotic circuit failure and life-threatening bleeding. This report synthesizes current evidence on heparin dosing strategies, monitoring approaches, and management of high-risk scenarios to provide a comprehensive understanding of how clinicians achieve hemostatic balance during ECMO support. The optimal approach involves individualized, protocol-driven anticoagulation management that considers each patient's bleeding risk profile, underlying disease, and evolving coagulation status.

## The Pathophysiology of ECMO-Induced Coagulopathy

### Blood-Surface Interactions and Hemostatic Disruption

The fundamental challenge in ECMO anticoagulation stems from the profound hemostatic perturbations caused by blood exposure to the extracorporeal circuit. The non-endothelialized synthetic surface of ECMO circuits triggers activation of multiple coagulation pathways simultaneously, creating a uniquely prothrombotic environment that paradoxically coexists with bleeding diathesis[1][50]. When blood contacts the circuit, the contact system is activated within minutes, with Factor XIIa activity reaching maximal levels within ten minutes of ECMO initiation[50]. This triggers not only coagulation cascade activation but also kallikrein and bradykinin production, both of which promote inflammatory responses. The complementary system becomes activated through multiple pathways, leading to production of anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a, which further amplify inflammation and endothelial dysfunction[50].

Simultaneously, the ECMO circuit induces a bleeding diathesis through multiple mechanisms that collectively impair hemostasis. Near-universal thrombocytopenia develops through platelet consumption at the circuit interface, with platelet counts commonly remaining below physiologic ranges (150-400 × 10⁹/L) throughout the ECMO course[20]. Beyond thrombocytopenia, platelets that remain in circulation undergo profound dysfunction, losing key surface adhesion molecules including GPVI, GPIb, CD63, and P-selectin[6]. Furthermore, the high shear stress generated by ECMO pumps produces acquired von Willebrand syndrome (AVWS) through mechanical unfolding of von Willebrand factor multimers, reducing their length and adhesive activity[49][52]. Studies demonstrate that all patients on veno-venous ECMO develop severe AVWS within hours of initiation, with rapid recovery within three to six hours after decannulation[52]. This combination of contact pathway activation, platelet dysfunction, and acquired factor deficiencies creates a hemostatic milieu that is simultaneously prothrombotic and prone to bleeding.

### Incidence and Clinical Impact of Bleeding and Thrombosis

The actual incidence of bleeding complications during ECMO proves remarkably high and varies substantially depending on anticoagulation strategy and patient population. In large prospective observational studies, bleeding occurred on 16.5% of individual ECMO days, with 52.5% of patients experiencing at least one bleeding event during their ECMO course[15][44]. Major or severe bleeding was reported in approximately 27.9% to 33% of ECMO patients, with bleeding occurring once every 215 hours on ECMO[6][15]. The most common bleeding sites include ECMO cannulation sites, the gastrointestinal tract, and the nasal or oropharyngeal region[21]. Intracranial hemorrhage represents a particularly devastating complication, with reported incidence ranging from 4% to 10%, and these events carry substantial mortality risk[6][24]. Conversely, thrombotic complications remain common, with circuit thrombosis requiring oxygenator exchange occurring in 10-16% of cases and patient-related thrombosis (including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and intracardiac thrombus) occurring in 8.5-10% of patients[6]. The balance between these competing risks fundamentally shapes anticoagulation management strategies.

## Standard Heparin Dosing Protocols and ELSO Guidelines

### Recommended Bolus and Continuous Infusion Dosing

The Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) provides the most widely accepted guidance for heparin dosing during ECMO, though importantly these recommendations acknowledge substantial institutional flexibility[1][4][31][32]. The ELSO guidelines recommend an initial unfractionated heparin (UFH) bolus of 50 to 100 units per kilogram at the time of cannulation, administered to achieve an activated clotting time (ACT) of 180 to 220 seconds[1]. Following this bolus, continuous intravenous infusion is initiated at rates typically ranging from 7.5 to 20 units per kilogram per hour, with maintenance infusion rates generally maintained between 20 and 40 units per kilogram per hour to achieve therapeutic targets[1][4][31]. However, these represent broad ranges, and actual practice varies considerably among institutions. Many centers give a larger stat bolus of 75 units per kilogram at cannulation and follow this with continuous infusion once ACT levels begin to fall to 300 seconds[31]. The maintenance infusion rate is then adjusted based on laboratory monitoring results and the patient's clinical response.

Importantly, these dosing recommendations are not rigid consensus requirements but rather suggested ranges that each ECMO center is encouraged to adapt to their patient population and institutional experience[32]. The wide recommended ranges reflect the inherent variability in heparin requirements among different patient populations and the lack of universally optimal dosing strategies. Some institutions employ even lower initial infusion rates of 5 to 10 units per kilogram per hour and titrate upward based on laboratory results, while others may start at higher doses depending on patient bleeding risk and disease severity. This flexibility reflects the reality that heparin requirements vary substantially based on patient factors including age, inflammatory status, renal function, platelet count, and presence of antithrombin deficiency.

### Variation from Standard Approaches in Special Circumstances

The ELSO guidelines explicitly acknowledge that the recommended bolus dose can and should be adjusted based on clinical circumstances[4]. In patients with preexisting bleeding disorders, recent major surgery, or active bleeding at the time of ECMO initiation, a reduced or delayed bolus approach may be appropriate. Many centers delay or reduce the initial heparin bolus in postcardiotomy patients to allow sufficient time for hemostasis at surgical sites before implementing full anticoagulation[1]. In exceptional situations including disseminated intravascular coagulation, perioperative periods, possible antithrombin deficiency, and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, heparin requirements may fluctuate dramatically from predicted ranges[31]. Additional investigations such as factor Xa levels and thromboelastography may be needed when heparin requirements are disproportionate to measured levels of standard monitoring parameters.

## Anticoagulation Monitoring Methods: Comparing ACT, aPTT, and Anti-Xa

### Activated Clotting Time (ACT): Traditional but Limited

The activated clotting time has historically been the most common point-of-care test used to monitor heparin during ECMO, primarily because it provides rapid bedside results and was previously considered theoretically appropriate for monitoring UFH effects[1][7][31]. ACT measures the time required for whole blood to clot in vitro after contact activation and is available as a point-of-care test that can be performed at the bedside[1][7]. The standard target range recommended for ECMO has traditionally been 180 to 220 seconds, with some centers aiming for 160-180 seconds during veno-venous ECMO and 180-200 seconds during veno-arterial ECMO[31]. However, accumulating evidence reveals substantial limitations of ACT for monitoring heparin anticoagulation during ECMO[7][10].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that ACT correlates poorly with both heparin dose and actual heparin concentration (measured by anti-Xa activity), with correlation coefficients typically ranging from 0.11 to 0.40[7][10]. ACT values are influenced by numerous factors unrelated to heparin effect, including platelet count, fibrinogen level, body temperature, hemodilution, other coagulation factor deficiencies, and renal replacement therapy[7][34]. In ECMO patients, thrombocytopenia and platelet dysfunction are universal findings, making ACT particularly unreliable in this population. Furthermore, ACT cannot differentiate between low and therapeutic levels of anticoagulation when using aPTT as the reference standard[7]. These limitations suggest that relying solely on ACT to guide heparin dosing may lead to either inadequate or excessive anticoagulation with corresponding increased risks of thrombosis or bleeding.

### Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT): Emerging Preference

In contrast to ACT, activated partial thromboplastin time demonstrates superior correlation with actual heparin concentration as measured by anti-Xa activity and with heparin dosing requirements[7][10]. Multiple studies have demonstrated strong positive correlation between aPTT and anti-Xa levels, particularly in patients with normal antithrombin levels[10]. The aPTT is less affected by the clinical factors that confound ACT interpretation, particularly platelet-related variables. Studies comparing institutions using ACT-based versus aPTT-based monitoring for heparin titration found that patients monitored using aPTT had fewer daily heparin dose changes and lower cumulative daily heparin doses compared to the ACT-guided group, with no difference in bleeding or thrombotic events[7]. In one prospective study, patients on aPTT-based protocols required substantially lower heparin doses than those on ACT-based protocols, suggesting that ACT-guided dosing may result in unnecessary overanticoagulation[7].

The typical target aPTT range during ECMO varies by institution but generally aims for 50 to 70 or even 40 to 60 seconds, representing approximately 1.5 to 2.5 times the baseline aPTT depending on the specific laboratory method[1][7][26][44]. Importantly, several studies have demonstrated a direct relationship between higher aPTT values and increased bleeding risk. In a large prospective observational study of 652 patients on veno-venous ECMO, for every 20-second increase in aPTT, the hazard ratio for first bleeding episode was 1.07, indicating a fairly linear relationship between aPTT elevation and bleeding complications[15][44]. This finding suggests that lower aPTT targets, within the therapeutic range, may reduce bleeding complications without increasing thrombotic risk.

### Anti-Xa Activity: The Gold Standard with Practical Limitations

Anti-factor Xa activity represents the most specific and reliable measure of heparin anticoagulation and is increasingly recognized as the preferred monitoring parameter for UFH in ECMO[8][11][29]. The anti-Xa assay directly measures heparin concentration by measuring residual exogenous Factor Xa activity in a patient's blood sample, providing a direct measurement of heparin's anticoagulant effect[11][29]. Unlike ACT and aPTT, anti-Xa levels are not affected by platelet count, fibrinogen level, thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, or elevated D-dimers[29]. Multiple studies demonstrate strong correlation between anti-Xa levels and UFH dosing requirements, and centers using anti-Xa monitoring report fewer blood draws for monitoring, longer duration between circuit changes, and lower transfusion requirements compared to ACT-only monitoring[29].

The widely accepted target range for anti-Xa activity during ECMO is 0.3 to 0.7 IU/mL for most patients, though some protocols utilize slightly different ranges depending on bleeding risk[1][8][12]. The EOLIA trial employed a lower anti-Xa target between 0.2 and 0.3 IU/mL[58]. Despite its superiority, anti-Xa testing has not been universally adopted for ECMO anticoagulation monitoring due to practical limitations[8][11]. The assay is not widely available in all laboratories, requires more complex laboratory techniques than point-of-care testing, is more expensive than alternative tests, and involves longer turnaround times that may reach several hours depending on laboratory location and testing frequency[7][8][11]. These practical limitations mean that most institutions continue to rely on ACT and aPTT for real-time monitoring, despite their known limitations.

An additional concern with anti-Xa monitoring involves potential overestimation of heparin effect when anti-Xa reagents contain dextran sulfate[11]. Dextran sulfate is added to prevent heparin from binding to platelet factor 4 (PF4) and other proteins in vitro, but this additive may lead to overestimation of functional heparin levels in patients on ECMO where PF4 levels are elevated[11]. One study comparing anti-Xa assays with and without dextran sulfate found median discrepancies of 0.09 IU/mL, with higher values in the dextran-containing assay, potentially leading to under-dosing of heparin if clinicians do not account for this difference[11].

## Heparin Resistance During ECMO: Definition, Causes, and Management

### Definition and Prevalence of Heparin Resistance

Heparin resistance represents a clinically significant phenomenon during ECMO, defined as the failure to achieve therapeutic anticoagulation targets despite high doses of heparin. The most commonly used definition in ECMO literature defines heparin resistance as the requirement of 35,000 units or more of UFH within a 24-hour period to maintain anti-Xa levels or aPTT within desired therapeutic range[42][39]. Alternative definitions include heparin doses exceeding 40 IU/kg/h in pediatric patients[39]. The prevalence of heparin resistance in ECMO populations proves remarkably high, with studies reporting that more than 50% of ECMO patients meet criteria for heparin resistance on at least one day of their ECMO course[42]. This represents substantially higher prevalence than the approximately 22% rate of heparin resistance historically reported in cardiac surgery patients undergoing short-term cardiopulmonary bypass[42].

Heparin resistance during ECMO appears to result from multiple simultaneous mechanisms, reflecting the complex pathophysiology of ECMO-induced inflammation and coagulopathy[39][42]. The development of acquired antithrombin (AT) deficiency represents the most important mechanism contributing to heparin resistance. Antithrombin is the essential cofactor that allows heparin to exert its anticoagulant effects, increasing heparin's inhibitory activity toward Factor Xa and thrombin by 1000 to 4000-fold[39][19]. Without adequate antithrombin, heparin cannot achieve therapeutic anticoagulation regardless of dosing. Antithrombin levels decline during ECMO due to consumption at an accelerated rate compared to hepatic synthesis, with extravascular leakage, impaired hepatic synthesis, and protease degradation all contributing[19]. Studies demonstrate that 40 to 60% of ECMO patients have AT activity levels below 60%, and significant proportions fall below 50% during the ECMO course[19][42]. The resulting heparin resistance directly correlates with heparin requirements and increases thrombotic complications.

### Additional Mechanisms of Heparin Resistance

Beyond antithrombin deficiency, several other mechanisms contribute to heparin resistance in ECMO patients[39][42]. Elevated levels of heparin-binding acute phase reactants increase non-specific heparin binding, reducing the amount of heparin available to bind antithrombin[38][39]. These acute phase proteins increase dramatically during critical illness and inflammation associated with both the underlying disease requiring ECMO and the inflammatory response triggered by circuit contact[38]. High platelet count activates platelets during ECMO, and activated platelets release platelet factor 4 (PF4), which is a potent heparin antagonist[39][42]. Studies have demonstrated that patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass with platelet concentrations exceeding 600 × 10⁹/L exhibit heparin resistance, and several studies have identified preoperatively high platelet levels as a prognostic parameter for postoperative heparin resistance[39]. Increased heparin clearance represents another mechanism, though the exact pathophysiology remains incompletely understood.

### Management of Heparin Resistance

Management of heparin resistance during ECMO involves several strategies that can be employed individually or in combination[4][34][42]. The most direct approach involves increasing heparin dosing, though this simply provides a higher absolute amount of the same ineffective anticoagulant and increases bleeding risk[4]. Antithrombin supplementation has been proposed as an alternative strategy to restore heparin responsiveness by replacing the deficient cofactor needed for heparin activity. However, current evidence on antithrombin supplementation remains inconsistent. Some studies suggest that antithrombin replacement can improve heparin responsiveness and reduce heparin dosing requirements, particularly in pediatric patients[34][42]. However, a recent systematic review found that AT supplementation does not reduce in-hospital mortality, bleeding, or thrombotic complications in ECMO patients overall and may pose risks in certain populations[22]. Current recommendations suggest that antithrombin supplementation may be considered in patients with documented AT deficiency and clinical evidence of heparin resistance, but routine supplementation is not currently recommended[4][32].

Switching to alternative anticoagulants represents another management strategy for patients with severe heparin resistance[4][31]. Direct thrombin inhibitors such as bivalirudin and argatroban do not require antithrombin as a cofactor and thus maintain their anticoagulant effect regardless of AT levels[4][14][38]. These agents may be particularly valuable in patients with profound heparin resistance where escalating heparin doses reach the limits of feasibility. The decision to switch anticoagulants should be individualized based on the severity of heparin resistance, bleeding risk, and institutional experience with alternative agents.

## Management of High Bleeding-Risk and Actively Bleeding ECMO Patients

### Risk Stratification and Patient Selection for Reduced Anticoagulation

Identifying patients at high risk for bleeding complications is crucial for tailoring anticoagulation strategies appropriately. Risk factors for bleeding during ECMO include postcardiotomy or post-surgical ECMO status, recent trauma, surgical cannulation (especially intrathoracic and arterial), prolonged ECMO duration, preexisting coagulation abnormalities, elevated aPTT (>72 seconds), fibrinogen less than 2 g/L, and platelet count less than 38,000/mm³[1]. Additional risk factors include older age, delayed sternal closure, and central cannulation technique[24]. In one study of peripheral ECMO patients, the strongest predictive factors for major bleeding were aPTT greater than 72 seconds and fibrinogen less than 2 g/L[24]. These parameters can be assessed at ECMO initiation and daily thereafter to identify patients who may benefit from reduced anticoagulation approaches.

For patients identified as high bleeding-risk at the time of ECMO initiation, modified anticoagulation strategies can be implemented from the outset. Bleeding and post-operative patients should have all clotting element deficiencies aggressively replaced, with cryoprecipitate administered to maintain fibrinogen above 1.5 g/L and platelets maintained above 80,000/mm³ or with normal maximum amplitude on thromboelastography[36]. Heparin should not be recommended until all active bleeding has ceased for at least 12 to 24 hours, assessed on an individual case basis, with even longer anticoagulation-free periods acceptable depending on risk-benefit balance[36]. This staged approach prioritizes achieving hemostasis through blood product replacement before introducing systemic anticoagulation.

### Minimal and No-Anticoagulation Protocols

Accumulating evidence suggests that ECMO can be performed without continuous systemic anticoagulation in selected high bleeding-risk patients, challenging traditional dogma that anticoagulation is absolutely essential[1][6][25][47]. A systematic review of anticoagulant-free ECMO in 201 adult patients reported that ECMO circuit thrombosis requiring equipment exchange occurred in only 13.4% of patients and patient thrombosis in 9.5%, rates that were comparable to or lower than historical rates in patients receiving standard anticoagulation[6][47]. Bleeding events occurred in 32.8% of anticoagulation-free ECMO patients, which was actually lower than the 39-51% reported in patients receiving standard anticoagulation[1][6]. Importantly, no new cases of intracranial hemorrhage were identified during anticoagulant-free ECMO periods[47].

A meta-analysis comparing low (target ACT 140-160 seconds) versus standard anticoagulation ECMO protocols found no significant difference between groups in incidence of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, clots in the oxygenator or pump, or intracardiac thrombus[1]. However, gastrointestinal tract and surgical site hemorrhage were significantly lower in the low anticoagulation group[1]. These findings suggest that reduced-intensity anticoagulation protocols can be safely implemented in selected patient populations, particularly those with high bleeding risk or active bleeding at ECMO initiation.

Technological advances in ECMO circuits, oxygenators, and pumps have improved biocompatibility and theoretically reduced the risk of thrombotic complications[1]. Improved heparin bonding of circuit materials, phosphorylcholine coating, and enhanced pump designs all contribute to reduced thrombogenicity of modern circuits[1][26]. These improvements provide the physiologic foundation supporting the feasibility of reduced or eliminated anticoagulation in carefully selected patients. However, routine anticoagulation-free ECMO is not currently recommended for all patients[55]. Rather, this approach should be reserved for patients with significant active bleeding or very high bleeding risk, implemented as a temporary measure while maintaining the capacity to rapidly reinitiate anticoagulation should circuit thrombosis develop.

### Reduced Anticoagulation Strategies

When complete anticoagulation cessation is considered too risky, reduced-anticoagulation protocols represent a middle ground. Some institutions utilize low-dose heparin with aPTT targets of 40-55 seconds or ACT targets of 140-160 seconds[1][25]. One study comparing safety of low-dose versus standard anticoagulation in 135 ECMO patients found that bleeding occurred in 15.4% of patients in the high-anticoagulation group compared to 2.5% in the low-anticoagulation group, with no significant difference in thrombotic events[25]. The mean oxygenator change-free time was similar between groups (223 ± 57 hours in low-AC group versus 179 ± 58 hours in high-AC group), suggesting that reduced anticoagulation did not compromise circuit durability[25].

Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) at prophylactic or reduced therapeutic doses has been investigated as an alternative to standard UFH dosing in some centers[44][45]. LMWH offers theoretical advantages of more predictable pharmacokinetics, longer half-life, and lower immunogenicity compared to UFH[45]. However, comparative data remain limited, and anticoagulation monitoring of LMWH in ECMO is challenging due to the unpredictability of anti-Xa responses with fixed dosing. Current evidence does not support routine use of LMWH during ECMO outside of research protocols[45].

## Alternative Anticoagulants for Special Circumstances

### Direct Thrombin Inhibitors: Bivalirudin

Bivalirudin is a synthetic, bivalent direct thrombin inhibitor that binds both the catalytic site and exosite-1 on thrombin in a reversible fashion[14][38]. Unlike UFH, bivalirudin acts independently of antithrombin and thus maintains its anticoagulant effect regardless of antithrombin levels[14][38]. Bivalirudin is administered intravenously in doses ranging from 0.025 to 0.48 mg/kg/hour, with action time of 2 to 4 minutes[14]. In pediatric ECMO protocols, bivalirudin is initiated at lower doses and titrated to achieve aPTT goals[2]. Monitoring of bivalirudin is typically performed using aPTT or activated clotting time, with target aPTT ranges similar to UFH monitoring[14].

Multiple retrospective studies have evaluated bivalirudin use in ECMO patients, including those with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia and those using bivalirudin as initial anticoagulation[14][17]. Bivalirudin appears to be a safe anticoagulation strategy for ECMO patients, with no overall evidence of increased bleeding or thrombotic complications relative to UFH[14]. Some reports suggest lower risk of thrombosis and vascular complications, as well as decreased transfusion requirements compared to UFH, though other case series report circuit thrombosis requiring exchange despite adequate bivalirudin levels[14]. Reports of patients requiring ECMO support up to 23 days suggest that outcomes with prolonged bivalirudin use are similar to UFH[14]. A significant limitation of bivalirudin is cost, which is substantially higher than UFH. Additionally, bivalirudin undergoes organ-independent proteolytic degradation, potentially creating regional anticoagulation loss in areas of blood stasis, a concern during ECMO circuit clamping trials[27].

### Argatroban for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia

Argatroban represents another direct thrombin inhibitor that has been most commonly employed in ECMO patients with suspected or confirmed heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)[14][16][31]. Like bivalirudin, argatroban acts independently of antithrombin. In published literature on ECMO patients, argatroban has typically been dosed at 0.1 to 0.3 μg/kg/min as a continuous infusion, with lower doses employed to reduce bleeding risk compared to the standard bolus doses used in non-ECMO HIT[14][31]. Argatroban is monitored using aPTT, with target ranges typically of 45-60 seconds[16][31]. One retrospective study of 39 adult patients treated with argatroban on ECMO reported that switching from UFH to argatroban in HIT-confirmed and HIT-suspected groups was associated with recovery of platelet counts within three days[13]. However, bleeding rates were higher in argatroban-treated groups compared to control groups[13].

### Considerations for Alternative Anticoagulant Selection

The 2022 ELSO Anticoagulation Guidelines provide no specific recommendations regarding which alternative anticoagulant should be selected for ECMO patients with HIT[16]. Clinical experience remains limited, particularly for argatroban and other alternatives[16]. Most available data come from retrospective studies, case series, and case reports, with only limited prospective comparative data[16]. The choice between bivalirudin and argatroban should be individualized based on institutional experience, availability, cost considerations, and specific patient factors. In general, both bivalirudin and argatroban appear safe and effective alternatives to UFH in HIT patients[16].

## Management of Active Bleeding During ECMO

### Initial Assessment and Blood Product Replacement

When bleeding occurs in ECMO patients, the fundamental principle is aggressive replacement of consumptive coagulopathy elements while judging whether systemic anticoagulation requires reduction or temporary cessation[1][36]. Initial assessment should determine whether the bleeding represents surgical site bleeding, medical bleeding, or diffuse bleeding from multiple sites[33][36]. The type and location of bleeding influences management decisions—surgical site bleeding may benefit from local hemostatic measures or operative intervention, while medical bleeding requires focus on correcting underlying coagulation abnormalities[36].

Aggressive replacement of clotting element deficiencies should be undertaken immediately in actively bleeding patients[36]. Fibrinogen should be maintained above 1.5 to 2.0 g/L through administration of cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate[36][37]. Each 10 units of cryoprecipitate increases fibrinogen by approximately 50 mg/dL[37]. Platelets should be maintained above 50,000 to 80,000/mm³ depending on bleeding severity and risk profile[36]. Fresh frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrate can be administered to replace other coagulation factors and maintain INR below 1.3[36]. This proactive blood product strategy often stabilizes bleeding before anticoagulation reduction becomes necessary.

### Anticoagulation Adjustment in Bleeding

When blood product replacement fails to control bleeding, reduction or temporary cessation of heparin infusion should be considered. The heparin half-life is approximately 60-90 minutes when given as continuous intravenous infusion, meaning heparin levels will decline substantially within 2-3 hours if the infusion is stopped[38]. Some centers perform heparin reversal with protamine sulphate, though protamine use carries important caveats—it may promote circuit thrombosis in heparin-bonded ECMO circuit components (particularly in Bioline-coated systems) and should only be used when heparin is definitively implicated in bleeding based on timing and coagulation tests[36]. If protamine is used, a pre-primed replacement circuit must be available for emergent exchange if acute circuit thrombosis develops[36].

The duration of anticoagulation cessation should be individualized based on bleeding severity and circuit function. As noted previously, ECMO can be maintained without anticoagulation for extended periods if circuit flows remain high (>2.5-3 L/min), with some reports of successful anticoagulation-free ECMO for up to 4-7 days[6][36]. Once bleeding has ceased for 12 to 24 hours, anticoagulation can typically be restarted at reduced doses and titrated upward based on laboratory monitoring[36]. However, the decision to restart anticoagulation should consider bleeding risk versus thrombosis risk individually for each patient.

### Tranexamic Acid and Antifibrinolytic Therapy

Tranexamic acid (TXA) represents an antifibrinolytic agent that may be considered for refractory bleeding in ECMO patients[33][36]. One study of 47 ECMO patients receiving tranexamic acid for bleeding events reported that initial hemostatic control was achieved in 79% of surgical bleed patients, 67% of medical bleed patients, and 90% of patients with multiple bleeds[33]. The occurrence of venous thromboembolism and seizures (potential complications of TXA) was relatively low and similar among patient bleeding groups[33]. However, tranexamic acid carries substantial thrombotic risk, particularly circuit thrombosis, and should be used cautiously with careful consideration of benefit versus risk[36]. At some institutions, TXA is considered an option in the management of severe bleeding but should only be used after multidisciplinary consultation including ECMO specialists and hematologists[36].

## Point-of-Care Viscoelastic Testing for Anticoagulation Monitoring

### Thromboelastography (TEG) and Rotational Thromboelastometry (ROTEM)

Viscoelastic testing including thromboelastography (TEG) and rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) provides comprehensive assessment of whole blood coagulation characteristics that standard laboratory tests cannot fully evaluate[29][57][58]. These tests measure the physical properties of blood clot formation, propagation, and stabilization in real time, and crucially, they can quantify fibrinolysis—a process invisible to conventional coagulation testing[29][57]. ROTEM and TEG can be performed at the point-of-care with results available within minutes, compared to several hours for some standard laboratory tests[29].

For UFH monitoring specifically, viscoelastic testing provides advantages beyond standard ACT, aPTT, and anti-Xa measurements. The kaolin-activated TEG R parameter (clot initiation time) can be targeted to maintain a range of approximately 16-24 minutes, representing therapeutic heparin effect[58]. ROTEM INTEM parameters similarly reflect heparin anticoagulation effect, with CT (clotting time) naturally prolonged by UFH and returning to normal with heparinase treatment in a HEPTEM test, allowing direct assessment of heparin effect[51][55]. The FIBTEM test specifically evaluates fibrinogen contribution to clot strength and can guide fibrinogen replacement therapy[58][60]. Studies demonstrate that TEG-based protocols for anticoagulation management in ECMO patients are safe and feasible and are associated with lower heparin doses compared to aPTT-based protocols[58].

One particularly compelling study compared TEG-guided versus aPTT-guided anticoagulation management in ECMO patients, with the TEG-guided protocol defining therapeutic anticoagulation as aPTT of 60-80 seconds and TEG R time of 2-4 times baseline[58]. The TEG-guided group demonstrated significantly lower mortality (33.3% versus 56.9%, p = 0.01) and reduced retroperitoneal bleeding compared to aPTT-only guided group[58]. These findings suggest that viscoelastic testing may provide superior outcomes, though additional prospective randomized trials are needed to confirm these results and determine optimal viscoelastic parameters for ECMO anticoagulation.

### Practical Implementation and Limitations

Despite theoretical and practical advantages, viscoelastic testing is not routinely performed at all ECMO centers due to equipment availability, cost, training requirements, and operator dependency[26][29]. Some centers maintain that standard laboratory tests supplemented by clinical judgment remain adequate for anticoagulation management[26][29]. However, there is clear momentum toward increased adoption of viscoelastic testing as institutional experience accumulates and evidence supporting its use grows[29][51][55]. Institutions implementing viscoelastic testing-based anticoagulation management typically establish standardized institutional protocols and provide staff training to ensure consistent application[58].

## Institution-Specific Protocols and Patient Tailoring

### Variation in Anticoagulation Approaches Across Centers

One of the most striking findings from large observational studies is the remarkable heterogeneity in anticoagulation management practices across ECMO centers[15][44][51][55]. The PROTECMO study, which prospectively tracked anticoagulation management in 652 ECMO patients across 41 international centers, demonstrated that anticoagulation management represents a dynamic clinical process with frequent changes in anticoagulation strategy, stopping of medications, and modifications of target parameters[15][44]. While UFH remained the most commonly used anticoagulant (77% initial and 73% throughout ECMO course), bivalirudin was used as initial anticoagulation in some centers and as rescue therapy in others[15][44]. Some centers employed anticoagulation-free periods or minimal anticoagulation strategies, while others maintained continuous standard anticoagulation throughout the ECMO course[15][44]. Monitoring strategies similarly varied, with aPTT being the principal test used for daily monitoring but ACT and anti-Xa assays also employed across different centers[15][44].

This substantial practice variation reflects the reality that definitive, evidence-based guidelines for optimal ECMO anticoagulation remain elusive[15][32][44][51]. The 2022 ELSO Anticoagulation Guidelines explicitly state that the guidelines "do not take the place of physicians' and other health professionals' judgment" and that "each ECMO program must develop a method to monitor the anticoagulant effect of unfractionated heparin to make it most effective for the patients at their own centers"[32]. This intentional flexibility acknowledges both the current state of incomplete evidence and the reality that individual patient factors, institutional resources, and clinician expertise should all influence anticoagulation strategy selection.

### Tailored Approaches Based on Bleeding Risk

Progressive refinement of institutional protocols increasingly incorporates explicit stratification of patients into bleeding-risk categories with corresponding anticoagulation targets[23][36]. For non-bleeding patients without high bleeding risk, systemic heparin with aPTT target of 50-70 seconds represents a common approach[23][36]. For bleeding and post-operative patients, initial management focuses on aggressive blood product replacement with heparin delayed until bleeding cessation for 12-24 hours, after which anticoagulation is cautiously restarted at reduced doses[23][36]. Some institutions employ even more granular stratification with standard-risk, high-risk, and active-bleeding categories, each with specific aPTT targets and heparin dosing algorithms[2].

The combination of institutional protocols with individualized clinical judgment appears to yield the most favorable outcomes. Rigidity in following guidelines without regard to patient-specific factors risks inadequate or excessive anticoagulation[51][55]. Conversely, complete lack of standardization leads to inconsistent care and prevents learning from experience[34]. The optimal approach involves structured institutional protocols that provide clear guidance while allowing flexibility for individual patient circumstances[51][55].

## Integration of Multiple Monitoring Parameters for Comprehensive Assessment

### Composite Assessment Beyond Single Laboratory Values

The evolution toward more sophisticated anticoagulation management recognizes that no single laboratory parameter completely captures heparin's anticoagulant effect or predicts bleeding risk in isolation[29][51][55]. A comprehensive approach integrates multiple monitoring parameters including aPTT, anti-Xa levels when available, viscoelastic testing when feasible, and clinical assessment of bleeding and thrombotic complications[29][51][55]. Antithrombin levels, fibrinogen, platelet count, and D-dimer should be monitored regularly to identify consumptive coagulopathy and guide supplementation strategies[29][51].

In practice, most ECMO centers currently employ aPTT as the primary real-time monitoring parameter due to its rapid availability and superior correlation with heparin effect compared to ACT[15][44]. Many centers supplement aPTT monitoring with periodic anti-Xa levels when feasible to provide validation of heparin dosing accuracy[29]. Some centers selectively employ viscoelastic testing for complex cases, patients with unexpected bleeding or thrombosis, or for routine evaluation when institutional resources permit[29][51][55]. This layered approach using multiple information sources reduces the likelihood of being misled by a single abnormal test result and provides more complete hemostatic assessment.

### Ongoing Adjustments Based on Clinical Response

Beyond laboratory parameters, clinical assessment of bleeding and thrombotic complications should inform ongoing anticoagulation adjustments[51][55]. A patient experiencing new bleeding despite appropriate laboratory parameters may warrant reduction of anticoagulation targets, whereas a patient with apparent circuit thrombosis may require escalation despite meeting targeted laboratory goals[51][55]. The development of unexpected thrombocytopenia should raise suspicion for HIT and prompt evaluation with 4Ts scoring and, if suspicion is high, switching to alternative anticoagulation[13][16][23]. Progressive oxygenator dysfunction may indicate developing circuit thrombosis and warrant anticoagulation assessment and possible circuit change[28].

This iterative approach combining laboratory data with clinical observation represents best practice in ECMO anticoagulation management. Centers that systematize this process through established protocols and multidisciplinary rounds achieve more consistent outcomes[51][55]. Regular collaboration between intensivists, ECMO specialists, surgeons, and hematologists ensures that all relevant expertise informs anticoagulation decisions[51][55].

## Emerging Evidence on Lower Anticoagulation Targets

### Reassessment of Optimal aPTT Targets

Recent evidence has challenged the assumption that higher anticoagulation targets universally improve outcomes. A large prospective observational study examining the relationship between aPTT and hemorrhagic complications in adults on ECMO support found that maintaining aPTT targets greater than 70 seconds increased hemorrhagic event risk 2.97-fold compared to non-anticoagulated patients, and aPTT of 60-70 seconds increased risk 2.06-fold[18]. The association between target aPTT levels and hemorrhagic complications was statistically significant, with the risk of hemorrhagic complications escalating as days on ECMO increased in patients with aPTT greater than 60 seconds[18]. These findings suggest that recommended aPTT targets might be modified downward in institutions with experience and expertise in ECMO support to reduce hemorrhagic complications without increasing thrombotic complications[18].

This emerging evidence may support future adjustment of institutional aPTT targets toward the lower end of traditional ranges (e.g., 40-55 seconds rather than 50-70 seconds) in centers with capacity for careful circuit monitoring[18][44]. However, such modifications require institutional expertise, robust circuit monitoring systems, and willingness to rapidly escalate anticoagulation if circuit thrombosis develops[18][44]. Centers without such infrastructure may continue to employ traditional higher aPTT targets as a safety margin to reduce thrombosis risk[18][44].

## Conclusion and Future Directions

Heparin dosage management during ECMO represents one of the most challenging and consequential clinical decisions in ECMO care, requiring careful balancing of thromboembolism prevention against bleeding risk. Current evidence supports individualized, protocol-driven approaches that incorporate patient-specific factors including underlying disease, bleeding risk at initiation, coagulation parameters, and clinical response to anticoagulation[1][4][32]. Unfractionated heparin remains the most widely used anticoagulant globally, monitored most commonly by aPTT, with growing recognition that aPTT provides superior correlation with heparin effect compared to activated clotting time[4][7][10]. While anti-Xa activity represents the most specific measure of heparin activity, practical limitations of availability and turnaround time currently limit its use for routine anticoagulation monitoring in most centers[8][11].

Significant progress has been made in recent years toward safer anticoagulation practices. Recognition that reduced anticoagulation and even anticoagulation-free ECMO are feasible in carefully selected patients has challenged rigid adherence to standard dosing and revealed that improved circuit biocompatibility reduces absolute anticoagulation requirements[1][25][47]. Integration of viscoelastic testing, when available, provides more complete hemostatic assessment than traditional coagulation tests alone[29][58]. Explicit risk stratification of patients into bleeding-risk categories with corresponding anticoagulation strategies ensures that high-risk patients are not subjected to unnecessarily aggressive anticoagulation[23][36].

Future progress in ECMO anticoagulation will likely involve larger prospective randomized controlled trials comparing different anticoagulation strategies and targets[48]. Such trials should stratify patients by bleeding risk, ECMO modality (VV versus VA), and other relevant clinical factors to provide more definitive evidence-based guidance. Broader implementation of point-of-care viscoelastic testing as a standardized monitoring approach may improve hemostatic outcomes, though cost and training barriers must first be addressed. Development of novel circuit materials with further improved biocompatibility may ultimately reduce anticoagulation requirements while maintaining circuit durability. Finally, advancing understanding of ECMO-induced coagulopathy mechanisms may identify new therapeutic targets beyond anticoagulation alone, such as targeted anti-inflammatory therapy or selective coagulation factor supplementation. Until such innovations materialize, clinicians must rely on thoughtful application of current evidence, adherence to standardized institutional protocols with individualized flexibility, and vigilant monitoring to optimize anticoagulation outcomes in ECMO patients.
