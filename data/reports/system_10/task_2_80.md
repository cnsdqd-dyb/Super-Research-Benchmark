## The Geometric Foundation of Ballet Architecture

Ballet's relationship with geometry is neither accidental nor superficial but rather foundational to the entire technical vocabulary and aesthetic philosophy that has defined the form since its codification in the seventeenth century[45]. The five basic positions of the feet and arms, developed during the reign of King Louis XIV and codified by Jean-Baptiste Lully and Pierre Beauchamp, establish a geometric framework governed by a square that organizes the dancer's spatial orientation[21][45]. With the spine functioning as the midline, this geometric organization creates what dancers refer to as the "cross," an invisible geometric structure that determines not only where the limbs are positioned but also the energetic and spatial relationships that choreography can explore[18][20]. This foundational geometric vocabulary extends beyond mere positioning to encompass the angles, lines, and spatial relationships that constitute the visual language of ballet itself.

The concept of sacred geometry in ballet reveals how specific formations carry symbolic and emotional weight far beyond their functional purpose[21][60]. When dancers in Swan Lake form a triangle shortly after the corps enters the stage, they create a powerful geometric image that communicates the unified strength and forward momentum of the flock, with the triangle's apex directing visual energy toward the audience[21][60]. Similarly, the circular formations that subsequently surround Siegfried embody unity and sisterhood through a distinctly different geometric vocabulary, demonstrating how the choice of geometric shape directly influences the emotional and narrative content of choreography[21][60]. This principle extends throughout classical ballet repertoire, where the geometry of formations is never accidental but rather deliberately crafted to reinforce the music's emotional and energetic charge[21].

Rudolf Laban, the dance theorist whose movement analysis system has profoundly influenced contemporary understanding of spatial dynamics, was himself inspired by the mathematical and spiritual theories of Plato and Pythagoras when developing his philosophy of "space harmony"[21][34]. Laban's approach to understanding spatial organization linked movements to geometric shapes such as the cube and octahedron, proposing that certain spatial configurations possessed inherent harmonic properties comparable to musical harmony[21]. He incorporated complex mathematical sequences including the golden ratio—the divine proportion that appears throughout nature and has been used by artists from Michelangelo to modern architects—into his understanding of how the human body relates to space[21][33]. This integration of mathematical principles with movement created a systematic approach to understanding how geometry operates not as an aesthetic overlay but as an essential organizational principle of how bodies can move through and organize space[21].

The geometric language of ballet establishes specific rules about how the body's lines and forms must align with the spatial environment. The concept of "lines" in ballet—the visual pathways created by the extended limbs and the overall silhouette of the body—represents the ballet dancer's interpretation of geometric form[15][18]. When a dancer performs a tendu in effacé, the acute angle created by the body's orientation differs fundamentally from the oblique angle of a battement croisé, and these different geometric configurations carry distinct aesthetic and emotional meanings[21]. Professional ballet companies achieve visual cohesion not through approximation but through precise geometric alignment, with each dancer maintaining specific angles and proportions that create unified visual formations[16][55]. A single dancer whose geometric alignment is incorrect can disrupt the entire visual image that a choreographed formation is meant to project, demonstrating how integral geometric precision is to successful ballet performance[16][55].

Contemporary choreographer William Forsythe revolutionized how geometry is employed in ballet by treating geometric concepts as generative tools for movement creation rather than merely as constraints to be observed[32][57]. Forsythe's method involves analyzing the geometric shapes that ballet dancers already understand from their classical training and then systematically distorting, extending, and transforming those shapes through movement[32][57]. By moving "from a point to a line to a plane to a volume," Forsythe conceptualized geometric space as composed of interconnected points contained within the dancer's body, allowing for endless "foldings" and "unfoldings" that generated infinite variations of movement[32][57]. This approach demonstrates how geometric thinking can function not as a historical artifact but as an active creative methodology, where manipulating geometric relationships becomes a systematic way to generate choreographic material[32][57].

## The Mechanics of Balance and Bodily Alignment

The mechanical principle of balance in ballet operates through a complex interaction of gravitational forces, bodily alignment, and muscular engagement that requires sophisticated understanding of anatomy and physics[7][8][9]. The center of gravity, located just below the navel at the body's midline anterior to the second sacral vertebra, represents the point at which all parts of the body balance each other[20][9]. For a ballet dancer to successfully maintain balance on a single supporting leg—a requirement that frequently appears in ballet technique—this center of gravity must be positioned directly over the base of support, which shifts dramatically depending on whether the dancer is standing on both feet or supporting the entire body weight on a single foot[8][9]. This principle of weight distribution is not intuitive but must be learned through deliberate training, as the alignment required for balanced movement on a single leg differs significantly from the alignment natural to human locomotion in other contexts[8].

Proper alignment in ballet establishes the conditions under which balance becomes mechanically possible while simultaneously creating the aesthetic lines that characterize classical ballet technique[8][9][12]. The four key building blocks of alignment—arches of the feet vertical, knees over centers of the feet, front of the pelvis vertical, and ribcage vertical over the pelvis—create a stacked vertical structure that allows weight to distribute efficiently through the skeleton rather than requiring constant muscular compensation[9][41]. When the arches of the feet are properly engaged, the body weight is distributed equally among three alignment landmarks: the base of the big toe, the base of the little toe, and the heel[9][41]. This distribution across three points creates inherent stability, whereas allowing the weight to roll inward or outward toward a single edge of the foot creates instability and forces the smaller muscles of the foot and ankle to work excessively to compensate[8][9].

The positioning of weight over the ball of the foot rather than distributed between heel and ball fundamentally changes the dancer's ability to move freely while maintaining balance[25][27]. When a ballet dancer shifts weight completely onto the ball of the supporting foot during movements such as tendu, the heel becomes free and available for articulation, allowing the standing leg to remain active and supportive while the working leg moves through space[11][25]. Conversely, if weight remains distributed toward the heel, the working leg is still partially bearing weight and therefore cannot move as freely or expressively[25]. This principle reveals how balance in ballet is not merely static equilibrium but rather a dynamic state that must be continuously adjusted through subtle weight shifts that should ideally be invisible to the audience[11][25]. The paradox of ballet balance is that achieving the appearance of effortless stability and control actually requires constant micro-adjustments of weight and alignment that happen at speeds and scales too small for the untrained eye to perceive[28].

Pelvic alignment represents a critical structural variable that influences the entire body's ability to achieve balanced movement while maintaining classical aesthetic lines[9][20][41]. The pelvis serves as the link between the lower limbs and the torso, and its alignment directly affects the capacity for turnout, the quality of the supporting leg, and the overall verticality of the spine[9][41]. When the pelvis maintains neutral alignment—a position in which the anterior superior iliac spines (the top front corners of the hip bones) and the pubic symphysis (the lower front of the pelvis) sit on the same vertical plane—the abdominal and spinal muscles can work together efficiently to maintain the body's vertical alignment without excessive strain[9][41]. An anterior tilt, where the pelvis tips forward, creates unnecessary stress on the lumbar spine, shortens muscles that cross the front of the hip, and compromises the engagement of the inner thigh muscles critical for turnout and stability[9][41]. Conversely, posterior tilt, achieved by over-engaging the abdominals or "tucking" the pelvis, creates tension and strain while reducing the capacity for hip extension movements such as arabesques[9][41].

The concept of proprioception—the body's ability to sense its position in space through internal sensory feedback—underlies the development of mechanical balance in ballet[39][42]. Unlike visual balance, which requires looking at a fixed point or at one's reflection, proprioceptive balance relies on the constant stream of information that proprioceptors (sensory receptors in joints, muscles, and tendons) send to the brain about joint angles, muscle length, and muscle tension[39][42]. Ballet training systematically develops proprioceptive awareness through repeated practice of positions and movements, training the nervous system to rapidly recognize and correct deviations from ideal alignment[39][42]. This is why professional ballet dancers can execute complex balance movements—such as balancing on a single foot while performing upper-body movements and maintaining directional focus—with an apparent ease that would seem impossible to the untrained observer[39]. The development of proprioceptive control requires years of consistent practice and represents one of the genuine accomplishments of serious ballet training[39].

The standing leg in ballet must maintain an extraordinarily high degree of stability and correct alignment while the working leg moves freely through space, creating a biomechanical challenge that distinguishes ballet from many other movement forms[18][25]. The standing leg must remain aligned along the coronal plane, maintaining the knee over the toes and preventing any tilting of the hip, even as the working leg moves to different positions and levels[18]. This requires extreme rotation and extreme stability simultaneously, creating a muscular demand that, while appearing effortless, actually involves the constant engagement of deep stabilizing muscles[18]. The challenge intensifies during movements such as développé or grand battement where the working leg must achieve extension while the standing leg maintains perfect alignment without any compensatory movement in the hip or pelvis[18]. Many dancers unconsciously allow the standing hip to tilt forward or backward or allow the standing knee to bend slightly in an attempt to facilitate greater extension in the working leg, but these compensations reduce the visual line and compromise both the technical accuracy and the mechanical stability of the movement[18].

Oppositional force represents a sophisticated principle in ballet balance wherein one part of the body moves in one direction while another part simultaneously creates opposition to maintain equilibrium[25]. This principle extends beyond the simple concept of a counterbalance where the arm lifts as the body leans, becoming instead a complex muscular and postural strategy where the engagement of opposing muscle groups creates the internal tension necessary to maintain balance while moving[25]. When a dancer shifts weight to the ball of the supporting foot while performing a tendu to the side, the hip must remain level and the pelvis must remain directly above the supporting foot despite the temptation to shift sideways[25]. Achieving this requires engaging the inner thigh muscles and lateral stabilizers on both the supporting side and the working side, creating an internal pattern of opposition that allows the leg to move while the torso remains centered[25].

## Spatial Dynamics and the Organization of Movement Through Space

The concept of the kinesphere—the personal space surrounding a dancer's body that extends as far as the limbs can reach while the body remains stationary—fundamentally shapes how ballet choreography operates within performance spaces[31][34]. As articulated by Rudolf Laban, the kinesphere represents not only the physical sphere that the body can influence through movement but also the psychological sense of personal space that dancers develop through training[31][34]. The kinesphere connects conceptually to Leonardo da Vinci's Vitruvian Man, that iconic drawing of a male figure inscribed within both a circle and a square, demonstrating how the human body's extended limbs define a natural geometric space that surrounds it[21][31][33]. Understanding and working consciously with the kinesphere allows choreographers to deliberately manipulate how dancers relate to the space immediately surrounding their bodies, creating varying degrees of expansion and contraction that communicate different emotional and spatial intentions[31][34].

Pathways—the routes that dancers trace on the floor as they move from one location to another—constitute a primary element through which spatial dynamics are organized in ballet choreography[1][3][4][19]. Pathways may be direct, following straight lines from one point to another, or indirect, utilizing curves, spirals, or zigzag patterns[4][19]. The choice between direct and indirect pathways carries both aesthetic and symbolic implications; a direct pathway communicates intention, decisiveness, and forward momentum, while curved and spiral pathways suggest exploration, indirection, or emotional complexity[1][4][19]. In the classical ballet repertoire, when the wilis in Act II of Giselle form four parallel lines of traveling arabesques, they create a pathway pattern that registers as a force of nature through their coordinated, line-based movement[21][60]. Conversely, when Myrtha subsequently slices across the stage with a fierce diagonal of jetés, the diagonal pathway communicates a different quality of power and intention[21][60].

The spatial dimensions of height, width, and depth establish the three-dimensional possibilities within which ballet movement occurs[20][23]. The vertical plane involves movement that manipulates height and width through positions such as forward bends and backbends[20]. The sagittal plane, operating along the forward-backward axis, includes movements that extend the limbs toward or away from the body's center[20]. The horizontal plane, organizing rotation and turns, adds another dimension to spatial exploration[20]. The transverse plane, which divides the body into upper and lower halves, becomes particularly important for understanding how pirouettes and other rotational movements operate within the body's structural organization[20]. Understanding these planes intellectually allows choreographers to make conscious choices about which spatial dimensions to emphasize within particular movements or phrases, creating deliberate contrasts between movements that explore different planes[20][23].

Spatial levels—typically categorized as high, middle, and low—provide another fundamental variable through which spatial dynamics are organized[3][4][19]. High-level movements that involve jumping, leaping, or moving on the balls of the feet create a different energetic quality than middle-level movements performed in standing positions, which differ from low-level movements that involve floor work, crawling, or deep bending[19]. The psychological and emotional associations of these levels are significant; high levels typically suggest aspiration, freedom, or exuberance, middle levels convey stability and presence, and low levels may suggest grounding, vulnerability, or introspection[19]. Contemporary choreography frequently plays with shifting between levels within a single phrase, creating dynamic contrast and preventing visual monotony[19]. The interplay of levels becomes particularly important in pieces that deliberately destabilize the classical vocabulary, as seen in works by choreographers who manipulate the expected relationship between specific movements and specific levels[19].

Formations—the spatial arrangements of multiple dancers on stage—operate as a primary compositional tool in ballet choreography, from the geometric precision of classical corps de ballet work to the experimental arrangements of contemporary pieces[50][51]. The triangle formation, for instance, carries specific visual and psychological properties; it naturally directs visual attention toward its apex, creates a sense of groundedness from base to peak, and communicates power and unified intention[21][60]. Circular formations emphasize unity, connection, and cyclical patterns, while linear arrangements create visual clarity and facilitate rapid spatial transitions[21][60]. The precision required in maintaining these formations distinguishes professional ballet from amateur performance; if multiple dancers are meant to form a perfect geometric shape, even small deviations in spacing or alignment become visually apparent to trained observers[16][55]. The development of spacing etiquette in ballet class—learning to maintain appropriate distance from other dancers while moving through space—represents essential preparation for executing choreographed formations accurately[51].

The concept of symmetry and asymmetry in spatial formations operates as a primary tool for creating visual meaning and establishing the overall aesthetic tone of a choreographic phrase[13][16][49][55]. Symmetric formations, where dancers mirror each other across a line of symmetry, create visual balance and a sense of resolution[16][49]. These symmetric positions are typically easier for dancers to execute because finding the center point literally becomes the center of each dancer's body[16][55]. Asymmetric formations, by contrast, create visual tension and instability; they require greater technical precision because any small shift in the center point can cause an entire formation to become visually unbalanced[16][55]. The deliberate choice to employ asymmetry communicates a different emotional tone than symmetric arrangement; asymmetry can suggest conflict, imbalance, individuality, or innovation, whereas symmetry typically suggests order, unity, and classical completeness[16][55].

Diagonal movement across the stage carries particular significance in ballet choreography, as the diagonal pathway activates different visual properties than horizontal or vertical pathways[50][60]. From a viewer's perspective, the left-to-right diagonal carries inherent kinetic energy because the human eye naturally moves from left to right; movement along this "active" diagonal reads as forward-moving and assertive[50]. The right-to-left diagonal, conversely, is sometimes termed the "passive" or "forced departure" diagonal, carrying visual associations with retreat or reluctant movement[50]. The most prominent examples of diagonal deployment appear in classical ballet; the traveling arabesques of the wilis in Giselle advance along a diagonal before yielding to Myrtha's path, and the spatial dynamics of this choreographic moment depend fundamentally on how the diagonal pathways organize the visual experience[21][60].

## Balance as a Spatial and Dynamic Variable

The relationship between balance and spatial dynamics becomes particularly complex when considering how balance functions not as a static condition but as a dynamic state that constantly shifts as dancers move through space[28]. The concept of "on balance" versus "off balance" represents a crucial distinction in contemporary ballet vocabulary, reflecting different stylistic approaches to movement[28]. Dancers trained in classical technique typically work to remain "on balance," meaning that their weight remains directly over their base of support, creating an appearance of control, stability, and composure[28]. However, contemporary choreography frequently employs "off balance" movement, where dancers deliberately position themselves in slight positions of imbalance, creating a sense of vulnerability, energy, or imminent movement[28]. This off-balance state can express "vitality and energy," in contrast to the more composed expression of "control, power and nobility" that on-balance movement conveys[28].

The height of the dancer's center of gravity directly influences what movements are mechanically possible and how those movements function within the overall spatial design of choreography[1][7]. When a dancer rises onto the balls of the feet, the center of gravity naturally rises, creating a taller and more elegant line while simultaneously creating a smaller and more unstable base of support[7]. This mechanical shift has both technical and choreographic implications; movements executed while on pointe or on demi-pointe require greater proprioceptive control and muscular engagement to maintain balance, but they also create visual lines that extend the apparent length of the body[7]. The deliberate manipulation of the center of gravity's height becomes a choreographic tool through which dancers can control the visual proportions of their bodies and the energetic quality of their movement[1].

The relationship between the body's center of gravity and the supporting foot represents the fundamental mechanical principle governing whether a dancer can execute specific movements while maintaining balance[8][9][25][27]. To achieve true balance on a single supporting leg, the entire weight must transfer completely onto that supporting leg, positioning the center of gravity directly above it[25][27]. This principle operates across all ballet movements; in tendu, the supporting leg must receive the complete weight even as the working leg moves; in développé, the supporting leg must maintain absolute stability as the working leg unfolds into extension; in pirouettes, the supporting foot must receive complete weight while the dancer rotates around this single point of contact[25][27]. The precision required in achieving this complete weight transfer distinguishes proficient ballet dancing from inadequate execution; many developing dancers inadvertently distribute weight between both feet or allow part of the working leg's weight to remain on the supporting leg, compromising both the clarity of the movement and the stability of the balance[25].

## Spatial Composition and Choreographic Intention

The conscious manipulation of space in ballet choreography operates as a primary vehicle for conveying narrative, emotion, and artistic meaning[1][3][21][60]. Dance notation systems, particularly Labanotation developed by Rudolf Laban, emerged specifically because space represents such a crucial variable in dance composition that it requires systematic documentation[1][22]. The relationship between spatial constraints and movement dynamics operates reciprocally; movement is inherently shaped by its spatial framework, and different spatial configurations will naturally generate different movement qualities[1]. When a personal notation system designed for a full-body performance was tested within three distinct spatial conditions—rectangle, circle, and triangle—the variations in spatial constraints revealed measurable impact on movement dynamics, demonstrating that choreography cannot be considered independently from the spatial environment in which it will be performed[1].

The concept of spatial perspective within the performance environment influences both the execution and the reception of choreographed material[50]. Just as painters use vanishing points and perspective lines to create depth and direct viewer attention, ballet choreographers deliberately organize spatial arrangements to guide audience focus[50]. The positioning of dancers in relation to the stage's vanishing point, the utilization of sloping stages in some theatre configurations, and the careful arrangement of corps dancers to create linear perspective all represent sophisticated uses of spatial principles adapted from visual art[50]. The physical execution of choreography must account for how the choreography will appear from the audience's viewing angle; a movement that reads clearly from the audience's perspective may require a different spatial organization than one that reads clearly from a profile view[50].

The eight directions of the stage—upstage right, stage right, downstage right, downstage center, downstage left, stage left, upstage left, and upstage center—create a coordinate system that allows choreographers to specify exact spatial locations with precision[44][51]. This directional vocabulary, systematized by Enrico Cecchetti and adopted across most classical ballet training, allows choreographers and dancers to communicate spatial intentions unambiguously[44]. The distinction between moving "to the front," "to the back," and "to the side" carries both practical and aesthetic implications, as each direction utilizes different muscle groups and creates different visual lines[44][51].

## The Integration of Turnout, Alignment, and Spatial Organization

Turnout, the external rotation of the lower limbs from the hip, represents a fundamental principle of classical ballet that directly connects mechanical balance, bodily alignment, and spatial organization[14][37][40]. Professional ballet dancers typically achieve a functional turnout range of approximately 133.6 degrees, achieved through a combination of hip external rotation (contributing approximately 60 percent), tibial external rotation (contributing 20-30 percent), and foot positioning (contributing the remaining portion)[37][40]. The mechanically efficient execution of turnout depends fundamentally on maintaining proper alignment; attempting to force additional turnout through the feet or knee rather than generating it from the hip creates mechanical stress and compromises both stability and the aesthetic line[14][37].

The hip joint itself possesses significant individual variation in how much external rotation it can achieve, determined by factors including the angle of femoral anteversion (the forward angle of the femur's neck), the orientation of the acetabulum (hip socket), and the shape and length of the femoral neck[37][40]. These anatomical variations mean that dancers possess inherently different turnout capacities; a dancer with favorable hip anatomy might achieve optimal turnout relatively easily, whereas another dancer with less favorable anatomy might require years of training and still never achieve 180 degrees of turnout in fifth position[37][40]. Contemporary ballet pedagogy increasingly recognizes that forcing turnout beyond an individual's anatomical capacity creates injury risk and compromises movement quality, encouraging instead the development of whatever turnout is available to an individual through proper alignment and muscular engagement[14][37][40].

The relationship between turnout and spatial organization becomes apparent when considering how turnout enables specific directional movements and creates particular visual lines[15][58]. When executed correctly, turnout originates from the hip and flows through the ankle and foot, allowing the foot to articulate clearly and communicate the quality of the movement[15][58]. The aesthetic principle that "honest range looks more classical than forced height" applies particularly to turnout; a dancer who maintains a genuinely achievable level of turnout through proper hip engagement will appear more classically correct than a dancer who forces additional turnout through the ankle or who compensates by twisting the knee[15][58]. The spatial orientation of the body in relation to the audience is fundamentally affected by turnout quality; when turnout is authentic and emerges from proper hip mechanics, it creates spatial planes and lines that read clearly and beautifully from the audience's perspective[15][58].

## Épaulement and Upper Body Spatial Orientation

Épaulement—the nuanced positioning and coordination of the head, shoulders, and torso in relation to the movement of the lower body—represents a sophisticated spatial and mechanical strategy that profoundly influences both the technical execution and the artistic quality of ballet movement[26][29][58]. The term literally translates as "shouldering," but the concept encompasses far more than shoulder position; it involves the active coordination of the entire upper body from the ribs through the head tilt[26]. Proper épaulement allows the head, which constitutes a substantial weight, to contribute to the balance and momentum of movement rather than remaining rigidly fixed, potentially creating counterbalance that prevents successful execution of specific movements[26][29].

The four primary orientations of the body in classical ballet—en face, croisé, effacé, and écarté—each establish distinct spatial relationships between the dancer's body and the audience, and each demands specific épaulement to read clearly[29][44]. In croisé, the body presents at an angle with the working leg placed directly in front or back of the center line, the neck curved, and the head positioned with a particular tilt[29]. The upstage arm typically frames the face, with fingers positioned over the center line of the head rather than pulled away from the body[29]. In effacé, the body maintains a different angular relationship to the audience, with épaulement that opens the torso while maintaining specific head positioning[29]. These distinctions are not merely aesthetic preferences but rather reflect how the human form creates optimal visual lines when combined with specific spatial angles[29].

The development of correct épaulement represents a significant technical undertaking that many dancers neglect in favor of focusing on footwork and leg movement[26]. Yet épaulement fundamentally affects a dancer's ability to execute certain movements with proper balance and control; the placement of the head influences the center of gravity and can facilitate or hinder specific movements depending on how it is positioned[26]. Moreover, épaulement communicates artistic intention and emotional quality; dancers who maintain rigid upper body positioning appear stiff and mechanical, while dancers who coordinate their upper body responsively with their lower body appear more grounded, intentional, and artistically engaged[26][58].

When the physical constraints of a space are deliberately manipulated—by reducing available floor space, modifying ceiling height, or changing the shape of the performance area—dancers naturally adjust their movement quality and spatial organization in response[1][23]. ## Contemporary Innovation and Geometric Exploration

Contemporary approaches to ballet choreography continue to expand how geometry and spatial dynamics can be explored through movement[32][35][57]. William Forsythe's algorithmic approach to choreography, which treats geometric manipulation as a systematic method for generating movement material, represents one significant contemporary development[32][57]. By analyzing the geometric properties of classical ballet positions and then systematically distorting these shapes through operations such as rotation, extrusion, inscription, and refraction, Forsythe develops new movement vocabularies while maintaining the fundamental balletic principles of precision, line, and control[32][57].

In Forsythe's methodology, dancers learn to perceive relationships between points on curves and other parts of their bodies, executing these perceived relationships through movement to reveal geometric patterns not visible to the untrained eye[32][57]. This approach transforms how dancers conceptualize their bodies in space, moving from understanding the body as a collection of positions to perceiving the body as a system of interconnected points that can create infinite geometric relationships[32][57]. The resulting movement maintains balletic rigor while opening possibilities that extend far beyond the classical vocabulary, demonstrating that geometric thinking can be employed in radically innovative ways without abandoning ballet's fundamental principles[32][57].

Forsythe's concept of "space harmony," adapted from Laban's original theories, involves organizing movement and spatial relationships according to principles of mathematical harmony[21][32][57]. In this approach, certain spatial configurations and movement patterns possess inherent harmonic properties that make them feel aesthetically satisfying and mechanically efficient, while others create tension or awkwardness[21][32]. This connects to fundamental principles of proportion and ratio that appear throughout nature and have been employed in visual art and architecture for centuries[21][33]. The application of these principles to movement creates a methodology through which choreography can be grounded in geometric and mathematical principles rather than relying solely on intuition or aesthetic preference[21][32].
