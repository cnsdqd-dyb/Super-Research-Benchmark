# The Convexity of Responsibility Attribution Functions and Stability Boundaries in AI Moral Decision Spaces: A Theoretical Framework

This comprehensive analysis examines how the mathematical properties of responsibility attribution functions—specifically their convexity characteristics—determine the stability boundaries of moral judgments within artificial intelligence decision systems. By synthesizing concepts from dynamical systems theory, moral philosophy, and AI ethics, this report demonstrates that the convexity properties of responsibility functions create topological constraints on the equilibrium states of moral judgment systems, fundamentally affecting the resilience and coherence of ethical reasoning in automated decision-making contexts. The stability of these boundaries depends critically on how responsibility is distributed among human and artificial agents, the degree of system complexity, and the coherence of underlying moral principles that structure the decision space. Understanding these relationships provides essential insights for designing AI systems that maintain ethical stability while navigating uncertainty and competing moral claims.

## Responsibility Attribution Functions: Mathematical Formulation and Ethical Dimensions

The challenge of assigning responsibility in artificial intelligence systems represents one of the most pressing problems in contemporary AI ethics. Rather than treating responsibility as a simple categorical assignment, modern frameworks conceptualize it as a function that distributes accountability across multiple stakeholders based on varying degrees of agency, causation, intentionality, and foresight.[1] This functional approach allows researchers to model how responsibility is allocated among software developers, data engineers, deploying organizations, end-users, and the AI systems themselves when operating in uncertain and complex environments.

A responsibility attribution function, formally conceived, can be represented as a mapping from the space of possible AI incidents and their causal chains to a multi-dimensional space of responsibility assignments. Each dimension corresponds to a different stakeholder or type of responsibility claim—for instance, technical responsibility, legal responsibility, moral responsibility, and epistemic responsibility.[1][4] The domain of such a function encompasses the complex set of circumstances surrounding an AI-enabled system's operation: the training data utilized, the algorithms implemented, the design choices made, the human oversight structures in place, the consequences that materialized, and the chain of causation connecting these elements.

The structure of a responsibility attribution function determines how the system responds to perturbations in its operating conditions. When responsibility functions are convex, small changes in a single factor—such as a slight increase in algorithmic transparency or a marginal improvement in human oversight—produce proportionally larger shifts in overall responsibility assignments. This property makes convex responsibility functions potentially more unstable in their boundary regions, where competing claims about who bears responsibility are most contested.[4] Conversely, when responsibility functions exhibit non-convex characteristics, the same perturbations might produce minimal changes, creating flat regions where responsibility allocations remain relatively insensitive to variations in underlying conditions.

The mathematical property of convexity in responsibility functions interacts with the ethical principle of answerability to create specific constraints on how moral reasoning can proceed in AI systems.[1] When a responsibility function is convex in the space defined by different stakeholder contributions, the system exhibits certain stability properties that make it easier to identify clear responsible parties. However, this mathematical convenience comes at a philosophical cost: convex responsibility functions may obscure the distributed and interconnected nature of causation in complex socio-technical systems, potentially leading to inappropriate attribution of responsibility to individual agents when systemic factors actually predominate.

## The Problem of Many Hands and Topological Complexity in Responsibility Spaces

One of the foundational challenges in assigning responsibility for AI-induced incidents is what philosophers call the "problem of many hands."[1] This problem arises because multiple actors with different degrees of agency and causal involvement contribute to any AI system's outputs. A data scientist who trained the model, the engineer who deployed it, the manager who approved deployment, the organization's leadership that set strategic directions, and the human operators who use the system all bear some relationship to eventual outcomes. The question becomes: how should responsibility be distributed across these multiple parties?

The problem intensifies when we consider what might be called the "problem of many things"—the observation that AI decisions depend not only on the choices made by human agents but also on the properties of data, algorithms, computational environments, and the complex interactions between these elements.[1] A biased training dataset might be the primary culprit in a discriminatory outcome, yet this bias may have originated from data collection practices shaped by social structures rather than conscious choices by any individual. The topological structure of the space in which responsibility is distributed becomes critical: if the space is convex, we can decompose responsibility linearly and identify specific causal factors; if the space is non-convex, responsibility distributions exhibit complex interactions where the whole cannot be understood as a simple sum of its parts.

When responsibility spaces are topologically complex—exhibiting non-convexity in certain regions—the stability of responsibility assignments becomes dependent on which region of the space the AI system operates within. In some regions, small changes in conditions produce stable, predictable shifts in responsibility assignments. In other regions, the system may display bifurcation behavior, where a minor change in a control parameter suddenly produces a qualitative shift in how responsibility should be assigned.[4] For example, a small increase in algorithmic autonomy might suddenly shift from a regime where organizations are primarily responsible (because they can easily override the system) to one where the AI system's design bears significant responsibility (because overriding it becomes computationally or organizationally infeasible).

The Computational Reflective Equilibrium approach proposes that responsibility can be assigned by finding the most coherent and ethically acceptable distribution across all stakeholders, using constraint satisfaction methods to identify equilibrium points where responsibility assignments align with both intuitions and ethical principles.[4] This computational approach reveals that responsibility spaces often have multiple possible equilibrium points, and the stability of each equilibrium depends on the convexity properties in its local neighborhood. Some equilibrium points are robust—small perturbations in operating conditions leave responsibility assignments largely unchanged. Other equilibrium points are unstable—modest changes in conditions produce dramatic shifts in how responsibility should be allocated.

## Moral Judgments and Dual-Process Decision Architecture

Human moral judgment operates through distinct processing systems that activate differently depending on the type of moral dilemma being considered.[2] When people confront trolley-like dilemmas—where choosing to act produces harmful consequences that prevent greater harm—they engage controlled cognitive processes that carefully weigh consequences against moral principles. In contrast, when confronted with footbridge dilemmas—where the harmful action feels more directly violent or emotionally disturbing—automatic emotional responses dominate the reasoning process, often overriding consequentialist calculations.[2]

This dual-process architecture creates inherent instability in moral judgment systems, particularly when applied to AI decision-making. When people evaluate whether AI agents should make autonomous decisions in moral contexts, their judgments depend significantly on which processing system becomes activated. Research demonstrates that people rate AI agents' behavior as more immoral and deserving of greater blame in trolley-dilemma-type contexts (where controlled reasoning applies) than in footbridge-dilemma-type contexts (where emotional responses dominate).[2] This differential response reveals that the stability boundary of moral judgments about AI depends not only on the actual action taken but on the type of moral reasoning the situation evokes.

The implications for responsibility attribution are profound. If human moral judgment about AI systems operates through dual processes that activate context-dependently, then responsibility functions must account for how different stakeholders will interpret the same AI decision through different moral lenses.[2] A technical decision that seems ethically justified from a consequentialist perspective (maximizing expected utility) may trigger strong moral condemnation from those whose moral reasoning is driven by deontological principles (respect for rights and duties) or care ethics (prioritization of relationships and responsiveness to vulnerable parties). The stability of a responsibility attribution function depends partly on whether its convexity properties allow it to accommodate these different moral frameworks or whether they force incoherent compromises.

## The Topology of Decision Spaces and Stability Thresholds

Mathematical stability analysis, drawn from dynamical systems theory, provides crucial insights into how responsibility attribution functions determine the stability of moral judgments in AI decision spaces.[42] A system exhibits Lyapunov stability when small perturbations to the initial conditions produce small perturbations in subsequent system behavior—the system remains bounded and predictable even as conditions change. In contrast, unstable systems display sensitivity to initial conditions where minute changes can cascade into dramatically different outcomes.

When we map responsibility attribution functions onto the space of possible AI decisions and their moral evaluations, certain regions of this space exhibit Lyapunov stability while others display instability. In stable regions, modest changes in how responsibility is distributed lead to correspondingly modest shifts in how an AI system's decision is evaluated morally. The stakeholders engaging with the system can develop consistent moral judgments and maintain confidence in the responsibility assignments they make. In unstable regions, the system displays extreme sensitivity: a marginal shift in who bears responsibility (perhaps because of a slightly improved explanation of the AI's reasoning) can suddenly flip moral evaluation from "acceptable" to "unacceptable" or vice versa.

The convexity of responsibility functions critically determines where these stability thresholds occur.[4][42] When responsibility functions are strictly convex, they exhibit a property called "no arbitrage" in financial mathematics—there is a single optimal responsibility distribution that maximizes coherence and ethical acceptability. This convex structure creates a single stable equilibrium point that attracts the system's behavior. However, when responsibility functions become non-convex in certain regions (as they often do when dealing with questions about whether AI systems can bear moral responsibility), multiple local equilibria emerge. The system can settle into different responsibility distributions, each internally coherent but representing qualitatively different ethical frameworks.

The stability boundary between different equilibrium regions depends on the degree of convexity violation. At bifurcation points—where the parameter controlling convexity reaches a critical value—the system transitions from having a single stable equilibrium to having multiple stable equilibria or from stability to instability.[22] This bifurcation occurs when the mathematical properties of the responsibility function change qualitatively. For instance, if we imagine a parameter representing the degree of AI autonomy, as autonomy increases (and the parameter crosses a threshold), the responsibility function might suddenly shift from convex to non-convex in certain regions. This shift produces a bifurcation: the responsibility landscape suddenly develops new valleys and peaks that structure the equilibrium possibilities differently.

## Coherence and Reflective Equilibrium in Moral Judgment Spaces

John Rawls's method of reflective equilibrium, adapted to address AI responsibility questions, provides a philosophical framework for understanding how moral judgments achieve stability in complex systems.[43][46] Reflective equilibrium occurs when three elements align: (1) a set of considered moral judgments about specific cases, (2) a set of general moral principles that organize and systematize these judgments, and (3) theoretical considerations about the nature of morality and justice that support both the judgments and principles. When these three elements cohere, the moral agent has achieved reflective equilibrium—a state of relative stability in moral reasoning where judgments and principles mutually support one another.

The mathematical properties of responsibility functions determine whether and where reflective equilibrium can be achieved in AI decision spaces. A convex responsibility function that distributes accountability in a linear fashion may fail to achieve reflective equilibrium if it conflicts with considered moral judgments about fairness and agency. For example, if a strictly convex responsibility function assigns sole responsibility to deploying organizations regardless of how much autonomy the AI system actually possesses, this assignment might conflict with the intuition that systems with significant autonomy should bear greater moral responsibility. The convexity property prevents the function from bending to accommodate this judgment—the function is too rigid in its mathematical structure.

Conversely, non-convex responsibility functions can exhibit multiple equilibrium points corresponding to different coherent moral frameworks. One equilibrium might represent a deontological perspective emphasizing duties and rights (perhaps assigning responsibility based primarily on whether harmful actions were taken and who authorized them), while another represents a consequentialist perspective emphasizing outcomes (assigning responsibility based on who could have prevented the worst consequences and whether they acted appropriately). The stability of each equilibrium depends on the curvature of the responsibility function in its local neighborhood. If the curvature is "favorable" (the function curves inward like a bowl), the equilibrium is stable—perturbations push the system back toward the equilibrium point. If the curvature is "unfavorable" (the function curves outward like a hill), the equilibrium is unstable—even tiny perturbations can push the system away.

The concept of "negative curvature" in responsibility spaces proves particularly important.[50] When responsibility functions exhibit negative curvature, the geometry of the ethical space creates resistance to incoherence. Just as a ball placed on a saddle-shaped surface (exhibiting negative curvature) is unstable and must roll away in some direction, a system operating in a negatively curved region of responsibility space cannot maintain coherence if that coherence requires simultaneously holding contradictory positions. A responsibility function with negative curvature in the region representing "AI agents bear responsibility but are not moral agents" will resist attempts to occupy that contradictory space—reasoning will tend to collapse unless reformulated into a coherent position.

## Epistemic and Transparency Dimensions of Stability

The stability of moral judgments in AI decision spaces depends critically on whether affected parties can understand and verify responsibility assignments. The epistemic condition for responsibility requires that responsible agents know what they are doing—they must have adequate knowledge of the causal chains, consequences, and moral significance of their actions.[1] When this condition fails, the responsibility assignment itself becomes unstable because affected parties cannot accept it as justified.

Transparency and explainability function as stabilizing mechanisms in responsibility spaces.[1][3][6] When AI systems operate as "black boxes" whose reasoning cannot be articulated, the epistemic condition for responsibility breaks down. Users cannot know what the system is doing, developers cannot fully understand how their design choices manifest in system behavior, and affected individuals cannot determine who bears responsibility for decisions affecting them. The stability boundary shifts outward—moral judgments become less certain, stakeholder confidence decreases, and the responsibility function becomes prone to oscillation as different parties propose competing interpretations.[3]

Explainability reduces this instability by decreasing the dimensionality of uncertainty.[1][3] When stakeholders can understand the factors contributing to a decision, the space of possible responsibility assignments shrinks. Instead of having to account for unknown factors and hidden biases, responsibility can be assigned based on known causal chains and transparent decision-making processes. This reduction in uncertainty typically makes responsibility functions locally more convex—the landscape of possible assignments becomes smoother, with fewer competing local optima.

However, there is a deeper relationship between explainability and the convexity of responsibility functions that extends beyond mere uncertainty reduction.[1] The requirement to explain one's actions (answerability) becomes a constraint that shapes the space of acceptable responsibility distributions. If a responsibility assignment cannot be explained coherently to affected parties—if the explanation would require appealing to factors deemed inappropriate, if it would require denying obvious causal contributions, if it would contradict other responsibility assignments in the system—then that distribution occupies an unstable region of responsibility space. Only distributions that can be defended through coherent explanation remain stable against challenge.

This insight reveals why transparency requirements feature so prominently in responsible AI frameworks.[3][6][18] Transparency is not merely a technical nicety or a matter of building public trust (though it serves those functions). Transparency is a structural requirement for stability in responsibility attribution systems. The convexity and stability properties of responsibility functions fundamentally depend on whether responsible parties can articulate and defend their role in decision-making processes.

## The Problem of Distributed Agency and Non-Convex Responsibility Landscapes

Modern AI systems distribute agency across multiple actors in ways that create inherently non-convex responsibility landscapes.[3][20][27] A self-driving car's decision to brake involves the choices made by the car's designer, the training data used to optimize the braking algorithm, the deployment decisions made by the manufacturer, the regulatory environment governing autonomous vehicles, the prior decisions by passengers to trust the automation, and the unexpected circumstances that made the decision necessary. Responsibility cannot be divided linearly among these factors because they interact non-additively—the contribution of one factor depends on what other factors are doing.

This distributed agency problem manifests mathematically as non-convexity in the responsibility function because individual agent contributions don't combine linearly.[3][27] If we were to ask "how much responsibility does the designer bear?" the answer depends dramatically on how much autonomy the system actually exercises, which depends on the quality of the training data, which depends on how the system is deployed, which depends on the regulatory environment. Each dimension of responsibility is coupled to the others through complex feedback loops. Changing one dimension shifts the entire landscape of responsibility assignments.

The non-convex nature of these responsibility landscapes creates multiple possible equilibrium points and introduces stability challenges.[4] Different stakeholders, approaching the problem from different perspectives, can converge on different responsibility distributions, each of which is internally coherent but incompatible with the others. The organization responsible for deploying the AI might argue for a responsibility distribution emphasizing human oversight and user decision-making (assigning responsibility primarily to individuals who chose to use the system). The affected individual might argue for a distribution emphasizing manufacturer accountability (assigning responsibility to those who designed and deployed the system). Neither position is logically incoherent; they simply occupy different local equilibria in the responsibility landscape.

The stability boundary between these equilibria—the tipping point where one distribution becomes more compelling than another—depends on how the convexity of the responsibility function changes as parameters vary.[4] As regulatory pressure increases, oversight requirements tighten, or public concern about AI safety grows, the responsibility landscape can undergo bifurcation. A responsibility distribution that seemed stable and justified under one set of conditions suddenly becomes unstable, with the system tending toward a different equilibrium distribution.[4] Understanding these bifurcation points allows policymakers and designers to anticipate where stability challenges will emerge and to adjust system design accordingly.

## Dynamics of Moral Judgment Formation and Equilibrium Seeking

The formation of moral judgments about AI systems exhibits the dynamics of a complex system seeking equilibrium rather than the mechanics of simple logical deduction.[8][47] Individuals begin with initial moral intuitions and considered judgments. Through exposure to information, dialogue with others, reflection on principles, and engagement with theoretical considerations, they revise and refine their judgments. This process is not linear—it exhibits feedback loops, cycles of revision, and periods of instability where judgments shift rapidly as new information becomes available.

The stability of moral judgments in AI contexts depends partly on whether the information environment supports convergence toward an equilibrium or whether it generates perpetual oscillation.[2][8] When individuals encounter consistent messages about AI responsibility (from media, experts, peers, and their own experience), their judgments tend to stabilize around a particular point. When they encounter conflicting messages that pull them toward different responsibility assignments, their judgments become unstable and prone to sudden shifts.

The mathematical properties of the responsibility function determine which equilibrium points can be stable under which conditions.[8][47] A responsibility function exhibiting high convexity tends to support stable convergence—different observers, approaching the question from different directions, tend to arrive at the same responsibility assignment because there is a single clear optimum. A non-convex responsibility function supports multiple local optima, meaning different observers can stably settle on different responsibility assignments without either being objectively wrong. This latter scenario creates what we might call "responsibility pluralism"—the recognition that multiple coherent systems for assigning responsibility can coexist, each stable within its own region of the parameter space.

The practical implication is that stability of moral judgments depends on which responsibility framework different stakeholders adopt. If some observers accept a deontological framework (focusing on duties, rights, and the intrinsic wrongness of certain actions) while others adopt a consequentialist framework (focusing on maximizing good outcomes), they will generate different responsibility distributions that may be equally stable within their respective frameworks but incompatible with one another. The stability boundary between these frameworks depends on how convex the responsibility function is in that region—whether the function penalizes trying to hold both perspectives simultaneously or whether it allows for some integration of both concerns.

## Bifurcation Points and Phase Transitions in Responsibility Attribution

Bifurcation theory, which analyzes how systems undergo qualitative changes as parameters vary, provides crucial insights into when responsibility attribution becomes unstable and undergoes sudden transitions.[22] As the relevant parameters of an AI system change—increasing autonomy, expanding scope of decision-making, introducing more complex data dependencies, removing human oversight—the responsibility function's mathematical properties change. At certain critical parameter values, bifurcation occurs: the system transitions from one regime of behavior to another.

A bifurcation point in responsibility space can be understood as a "tipping point" where the previously stable responsibility assignment suddenly becomes untenable. For example, imagine a parameter representing the degree of AI system autonomy, ranging from zero (complete human control) to one (complete AI autonomy). At low autonomy values, a convex responsibility function might assign primary responsibility to human operators—they control the system sufficiently that they are responsible for its outputs. As autonomy increases, the operator's control decreases, but the function remains convex and the primary responsibility assignment shifts gradually. However, at some critical autonomy threshold (say, 0.7 or 0.8), the function may undergo bifurcation. Below this threshold, the responsibility landscape has a single clear equilibrium at "human operator is primarily responsible." Above this threshold, the landscape has a different equilibrium at "AI designer is primarily responsible." At the bifurcation point itself, both equilibria coexist with equal stability, and the system is unstable—small perturbations can shift responsibility assignment dramatically.

Practical cases illustrate how these bifurcations manifest.[4][12] A loan approval system operating with high human oversight but providing AI recommendations exhibits responsibility dynamics where humans remain the primary decision-makers despite the system's increasing accuracy. Responsibility assigns to human operators. As the system's recommendations become increasingly accurate and compelling, human operators begin deferring to the system almost automatically. At some point, this deference crosses a bifurcation threshold: the situation transitions from "humans use AI recommendations as one input among many considerations" to "humans effectively implement the AI system's suggestions without genuine independent judgment." After bifurcation, responsibility assignments shift—the system designer becomes more responsible for wrong decisions because the human overseer is no longer meaningfully exercising judgment.

The stability of responsibility assignments around bifurcation points depends on the second derivative of the responsibility function (its curvature).[22][42] In regions where the function is highly convex (curvature is strongly positive), bifurcations tend to be supercritical—the transition between regimes is relatively smooth, and the new equilibrium is stable. In regions where the function is nearly flat or exhibits negative curvature, bifurcations tend to be subcritical—the transition is sharper, and the new equilibrium may be less stable initially, making the system prone to oscillation.

## Multi-Stakeholder Moral Frameworks and Convexity Properties

Responsible AI requires engaging multiple stakeholders—developers, deploying organizations, users, affected individuals, regulators, and civil society—in shaping how responsibility is attributed.[18][25][27][30] Different stakeholder groups have different perspectives on which responsibility distributions are acceptable, creating a pluralistic moral space with multiple partially conflicting value systems.

From a mathematical perspective, multi-stakeholder responsibility spaces are necessarily non-convex. If all stakeholders agreed on a single moral framework for assigning responsibility (say, all were strict utilitarians focused on maximizing overall welfare), the responsibility function would be convex—there would be a single optimal responsibility distribution that all parties would endorse. However, because stakeholders adopt different moral frameworks—some emphasizing fairness and rights, others emphasizing efficiency and consequences, still others emphasizing relationships and care—the responsibility space becomes non-convex. Different stakeholder groups converge on different responsibility equilibria.

The stability of responsibility assignments in this multi-stakeholder context depends on whether procedural mechanisms exist for managing non-convexity.[27] Several approaches can improve stability without requiring all stakeholders to abandon their distinct moral frameworks. First, stakeholder engagement processes that explicitly surface different perspectives can help identify regions of agreement and understand the basis for disagreement.[27][30] When multiple equilibria exist, understanding what distinguishes them makes it possible to design systems that operate stably across different stakeholder perspectives. Second, procedural justice—ensuring that stakeholders feel heard and that responsibility assignments can be explained and justified to all parties—can stabilize responsibility distributions even when substantive consensus about the "right" assignment doesn't exist.[18][25] The stability comes from legitimacy of process rather than agreement on outcomes.

Third, layered governance structures that assign different aspects of responsibility to different stakeholders can increase overall system stability.[3][18][27][28] Rather than forcing stakeholders into a single responsibility distribution, responsibility can be partitioned: technical responsibility goes to developers, organizational responsibility goes to deploying institutions, regulatory compliance responsibility goes to specialized oversight bodies, and direct accountability to affected individuals goes to those implementing the system. This partitioning can make non-convex multi-stakeholder spaces locally convex—each stakeholder operates within a subspace where they can achieve clear responsible action without requiring agreement from other stakeholders about broader responsibility distribution.

## Practical Applications: Designing Stable Responsibility Attribution Systems

Understanding how convexity determines stability boundaries in moral judgment spaces has direct implications for designing AI systems that maintain ethical integrity as they operate in complex, uncertain environments. The theoretical analysis suggests several design principles that can enhance stability.

First, **enhance local convexity through transparency and explainability**.[1][3][15] By making system reasoning transparent, designers increase the convexity of responsibility functions in the neighborhood of current operating points. Stakeholders can understand the factors contributing to decisions, see how responsibility is distributed, and anticipate how responsibility assignments would shift if conditions changed. This increased local transparency creates locally convex responsibility landscapes where responsibility assignments remain relatively stable to perturbations.

Second, **identify and map bifurcation points in system operation**.[4][12][28] As AI systems are designed and deployed, relevant parameters will vary—the system's autonomy will increase, the scope of decisions will expand, oversight mechanisms will prove more or less effective. By identifying critical parameter values where bifurcation occurs, designers and deployers can implement safeguards that prevent unintended transitions between responsibility regimes. For instance, if analysis suggests that bifurcation between "human is responsible" and "system is responsible" regimes occurs around a threshold of system accuracy, humans can be alerted as the system approaches this threshold, allowing explicit re-evaluation of oversight mechanisms before automatic transition occurs.

Third, **design responsibility attribution mechanisms that are robust across multiple moral frameworks**.[23][27] Rather than assuming that a single "correct" responsibility distribution exists, acknowledge that different stakeholders operating within different moral frameworks will generate different responsibility assignments. Design systems that maintain ethical stability across this plurality. This might involve ensuring that whether stakeholders emphasize consequences or duties, whether they prioritize efficiency or fairness, the resulting responsibility distributions are sufficiently aligned that the AI system can operate with integrity.

Fourth, **establish ongoing human-in-the-loop mechanisms that maintain epistemic grounding**.[1][15][25] One of the most powerful stability mechanisms is ensuring that humans remain cognitively and informationally engaged with system operation. As systems become more capable and appear more reliable, there is a strong tendency toward automation bias—humans deferring to system judgments without genuine independent evaluation. This deference creates instability because responsibility has shifted without explicit acknowledgment. Maintaining epistemic grounding—ensuring humans understand not just what the system decided but why, and can meaningfully contest the system's reasoning—keeps responsibility assignments stable and coherent.

## Ethical Risk Cascades and Stability of Complex Systems

The stability of responsibility attribution in complex AI systems is further complicated by the way ethical risks propagate through interconnected components.[9][27] A bias in the training data (input module) can be amplified by the learning algorithm (training module), and manifest as discriminatory outputs (output module). The non-convexity of these cascading risk pathways creates instability in responsibility assignment: the responsibility landscape changes dramatically as different causal pathways become salient.

When ethical risks cascade through multiple system components, the stability boundary of responsibility attributions shifts. At low system complexity, responsibility can be assigned relatively locally—if the output is biased, look to the training data and model training. At high system complexity, with multiple cascade pathways possible, responsibility becomes more distributed and harder to localize. The convexity of the responsibility function depends on the system's topology—whether it exhibits modular structure (which creates local convexity) or deeply interconnected structure (which creates non-convexity and multiple equilibria).

Managing this complexity requires what might be called "topological awareness" in responsibility design.[56] Systems designed with clear modular boundaries that limit risk cascade pathways maintain higher local convexity in responsibility spaces. The cascading risk framework suggests that stability is enhanced when architectural choices limit how perturbations in one component propagate to others. A modular AI system where data processing, model training, and decision application are clearly separated maintains more stable responsibility distributions because each module's failure modes can be understood independently.

## Conclusion: Toward Coherent and Stable Responsibility Systems

The convexity of responsibility attribution functions determines the stability boundaries of moral judgments in AI decision spaces through several interconnected mechanisms. In convex regions of responsibility space, responsibility assignments remain stable to perturbations because there is a single clear equilibrium toward which the system gravitates. In non-convex regions, multiple equilibria exist, and small changes in conditions can cause sudden transitions from one equilibrium to another. Bifurcation points mark critical parameters where the mathematical structure of responsibility space changes qualitatively, making previously stable assignments suddenly untenable.

The stability of moral judgment systems in AI contexts therefore depends not only on the content of the moral frameworks employed but on their mathematical properties. **Transparency and explainability stabilize responsibility functions** by increasing local convexity—stakeholders can understand responsibility assignments and predict how they would shift under changed conditions.[1][3][15] **Multi-stakeholder engagement procedures stabilize systems** by explicitly acknowledging and managing non-convexity—different stakeholders can converge on responsibility assignments even when working within different moral frameworks.[27][30]

The relationship between responsibility attribution and moral judgment stability also reveals why certain governance structures prove more resilient than others.[18][25][28] Layered governance that partitions responsibility among different stakeholder groups can achieve greater overall stability than centralized governance requiring universal agreement on responsibility distributions, because it operates in regions of higher local convexity. Similarly, governance structures that remain explicitly anchored in human oversight and maintain epistemic grounding preserve the convexity properties that support stable responsibility attribution.[1][15]

As artificial intelligence systems become increasingly autonomous and consequential, the mathematical properties of the responsibility systems we design will become crucial determinants of whether ethical reasoning remains coherent and justified or whether it collapses into confusion, blame-shifting, and instability. By understanding how convexity shapes stability boundaries, by mapping bifurcation points in systems as they evolve, and by designing responsibility attribution mechanisms that maintain coherence across multiple moral frameworks, we can work toward AI systems that remain ethically stable even as they operate in complex, uncertain, and multivalent moral spaces. The path forward requires integrating insights from mathematics, philosophy, and systems design to create responsibility frameworks that are not merely coherent in principle but stable in practice.
