# Behavioral Game Theory in Investment Decision-Making: Balancing Rational Equilibrium with Loss Aversion in Prospect Theory

This comprehensive examination of behavioral game theory's role in investment decision-making reveals a fundamentally complex relationship between rational equilibrium models and the behavioral biases they attempt to incorporate. The research demonstrates that prospect theory parameterization does not simply correct for cognitive biases—rather, it creates a dynamic tension between equilibrium predictions and observable behavioral deviations, with the effect of loss aversion parameters sometimes amplifying rather than mitigating irrational decision patterns. Current modeling approaches achieve only partial success in reconciling traditional game-theoretic equilibrium concepts with the empirical reality of investor behavior, creating a landscape where different theoretical frameworks produce divergent predictions depending on their treatment of loss aversion coefficients and the endogenization of reference points. The critical finding across multiple empirical studies is that loss aversion effects are not uniform but highly dependent on parameter specification, temporal framing, and individual heterogeneity, suggesting that the balance between rational correction and bias amplification remains fundamentally unresolved in contemporary behavioral finance literature.

## The Foundations of Behavioral Game Theory in Financial Markets

Behavioral game theory emerged as a direct response to the persistent failures of traditional game-theoretic models to predict actual investor behavior, particularly regarding how individuals deviate from Nash equilibrium predictions in strategic financial settings[7]. In traditional game theory, the Nash equilibrium concept assumes that all players are fully rational, have perfect information, and make decisions that maximize their expected utility. However, experimental evidence has repeatedly demonstrated that actual players comply with Nash Equilibrium predictions only approximately 35 percent of the time, with even more striking deviations appearing when participants are asked about their beliefs regarding opponents' behavior[7]. The framework of behavioral game theory acknowledges these systematic deviations by incorporating insights from psychology and cognitive science, recognizing that investors operate under bounded rationality—they are not fully rational but attempt to make satisfactory rather than optimal decisions given their cognitive limitations.

The integration of behavioral principles into game-theoretic frameworks creates a fundamentally different analytical landscape than traditional models provide. When behavioral decision-makers interact strategically, their choices become interdependent in ways that traditional models cannot adequately capture[4]. For example, research on behavioral security games demonstrates that when defenders become more behavioral—meaning they assign lower weights to probabilities and exhibit greater sensitivity to large potential losses—they systematically concentrate their defensive investments on the highest-value assets, creating investment patterns that diverge qualitatively from rational equilibrium predictions[1]. This concentration effect illustrates a key principle: behavioral biases do not merely introduce random noise into decision-making but create systematic, directional distortions that compound when multiple behavioral agents interact within the same strategic environment.

The theoretical challenge in behavioral game theory centers on how to model the interaction between rational and behavioral players, or how to characterize equilibrium when all players behave behaviorally. Quantal Response Equilibrium (QRE) provides one approach to this problem by generalizing Nash equilibrium to accommodate probabilistic choice-making, where players select actions with probabilities proportional to expected payoffs rather than always choosing the strictly optimal action[15]. This framework acknowledges that individual deviations from optimal play are more likely when the expected payoff difference between actions is small and less likely when one action's payoff substantially exceeds another's. However, QRE still maintains the core equilibrium assumption that players' beliefs about others' choices must match the actual distribution of choices in equilibrium, which may not hold when behavioral biases systematically shape beliefs in predictable ways.

## Prospect Theory as the Foundation of Behavioral Financial Models

Prospect theory, developed by Kahneman and Tversky in 1979 and refined in 1992, fundamentally transformed how economists and finance scholars understand decision-making under risk and uncertainty[2][5]. Rather than assuming that individuals evaluate outcomes in absolute terms relative to their current wealth, prospect theory posits that people evaluate gains and losses relative to a psychological reference point, with asymmetric sensitivity where losses are felt approximately twice as acutely as equivalent gains[2]. This loss aversion mechanism operates through a value function that is concave over gains (creating risk aversion) and convex over losses (creating risk-seeking behavior), with a distinctive kink at the reference point reflecting the heightened sensitivity to losses. The mathematical representation of the prospect theory value function is expressed as \(v(x) = x^\alpha\) for gains and \(v(x) = -\lambda(-x)^\beta\) for losses, where the loss aversion coefficient \(\lambda > 1\) quantifies the relative weight placed on losses compared to gains[5].

The parameterization of prospect theory has proven remarkably influential but also remarkably contentious in empirical research. Classical laboratory experiments suggested a loss aversion coefficient of approximately 2.25, implying that losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good[5]. However, more recent empirical work examining actual investor behavior in field settings has uncovered substantial heterogeneity in these parameters across individuals and contexts. Studies of mutual fund flows, for instance, estimate loss aversion coefficients ranging from 1.45 to values as low as 1.2 or even lower when accounting for the endogenization of reference points[23]. This variation proves critical because the magnitude of the loss aversion parameter directly determines whether prospect theory models amplify or dampen the behavioral deviations observed in actual markets. When loss aversion coefficients are assumed to be high (near 2.25), prospect theory generates extreme predictions about portfolio choice, risk-taking, and asset prices; when coefficients are calibrated to empirical values closer to 1.5, the same model produces more moderate predictions that sometimes converge toward rational equilibrium benchmarks.

An often-overlooked feature of prospect theory parameterization concerns the size dependence of loss aversion—the observation that loss aversion coefficients cannot be meaningfully separated from the scale of the losses and gains being evaluated[8]. Specifically, the loss aversion parameter identified from choices involving small stakes (perhaps involving gains or losses of $10-100) may not translate directly to large financial decisions involving thousands or millions of dollars. This creates a fundamental identification problem: when empirical researchers estimate loss aversion coefficients from field data on actual investment decisions, they cannot distinguish between a change in true underlying loss aversion and a change in how loss aversion operates at different scales. The theoretical implication is profound: prospect theory as typically implemented in investment models may conflate scale effects with preference parameters, potentially leading to misspecified models where apparent loss aversion is partly an artifact of how the researcher has normalized the problem rather than a true feature of investor preferences.

## Equilibrium Analysis in Behavioral Investment Models

The incorporation of behavioral preferences into equilibrium analysis creates what might be termed a "Gordian knot" problem: traditional equilibrium concepts assume that individuals maximize well-defined utility functions and that market prices adjust until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, yet behavioral models introduce non-standard preferences, cognitive errors, and systematic biases that complicate or potentially invalidate these equilibrium conditions. Research on equilibrium analysis in behavioral growth models demonstrates that behavioral biases fundamentally alter the structure of equilibrium if and only if they change the direction of how agents respond to changes in their economic environment[6]. This principle is counterintuitively important because it implies that many behavioral biases might be largely irrelevant for understanding long-run equilibrium outcomes, mattering only when they flip the direction of the direct response to environmental shocks.

The theory of behavioral learning equilibria extends this analysis by recognizing that in dynamic environments, agents do not necessarily converge to perfect rationality even over time[22]. Instead, agents may converge to behaviorally-conditioned equilibria where expectations are formed using simplified rules that incorporate systematic biases such as overweighting recent data or underweighting base rates. These behavioral learning equilibria can exhibit qualitatively different properties than rational expectations equilibria: they may have multiple equilibria where rational models would have a unique one, or conversely, they may exhibit determinacy properties that prevent the indeterminacy problems that plague some rational expectations models. The practical implication is that behavioral models do not simply "correct" rational equilibrium by adding a bias term; rather, they fundamentally restructure the equilibrium concept itself.

A particularly revealing observation emerges from the study of behavioral game theory in Bayesian game frameworks applied to financial decision-making. When investors hold heterogeneous beliefs about whether their competitors are exhibiting overconfidence bias (or other behavioral biases), their strategic decisions become dependent not only on their own beliefs but on their perceptions of others' biases[21]. This creates what researchers term "contagion of sentiment," where modest changes in market perceptions about how widespread a behavioral bias is can cause significant shifts in equilibrium behavior. For example, if investors believe that overconfidence is becoming more prevalent in the market, they may themselves shift toward more conservative strategies even without any actual change in the prevalence of overconfidence. This phenomenon reveals that behavioral game theory does not simply balance rational equilibrium with bias correction; rather, it creates new equilibrium properties dependent on higher-order beliefs about biases, potentially amplifying behavioral distortions through belief contagion mechanisms.

## Loss Aversion Amplification Versus Bias Correction in Empirical Markets

The empirical evidence reveals a deeply troubling paradox for behavioral finance: loss aversion parameters estimated from anticipated decision-making and choice behavior are substantially larger than loss aversion parameters inferred from how investors actually experience realized outcomes. Research on the "financial loss aversion illusion" demonstrates that investors report loss aversion coefficients of approximately 2.2 when evaluating expected returns—meaning they claim to weight losses twice as heavily as gains—yet when these same investors later evaluate their actual portfolio performance, the apparent loss aversion coefficient drops to approximately 1.2 and becomes statistically indistinguishable from one (loss neutrality)[14][11]. This dramatic gap suggests that investors do not experience losses as acutely as they anticipate they will, creating what researchers term an "affective forecasting error" where the anticipated emotional pain of losses exceeds the actual experienced pain.

This finding has profound implications for whether prospect theory parameterization amplifies or corrects for behavioral biases in investment decision-making. If loss aversion primarily reflects an affective forecasting error—a failure to accurately predict one's emotional response to outcomes—then using high loss aversion coefficients in investment models creates amplified predictions of suboptimal behavior relative to what investors would actually do if they could accurately predict their own emotional responses. In this scenario, prospect theory with standard parameterization does not correct for behavioral bias but rather amplifies it by incorporating the error structure of human forecasting into the model. Conversely, if loss aversion is a genuine preference feature that emerges when investors actively contemplate potential losses, then models using lower loss aversion coefficients inferred from realized experience may understate the biases that actually drive investment behavior, failing to correct adequately for the bias that matters during the decision-making process.

The heterogeneity of loss aversion effects across investor populations provides additional evidence that the balance between correction and amplification varies substantially across contexts. Studies examining mutual fund flows find that loss aversion operates more strongly for retail investors in smaller funds than for institutional investors managing larger portfolios[23]. Financial sophistication, measured through variables such as investment experience and financial literacy, consistently predicts lower effective loss aversion coefficients, suggesting that more sophisticated investors either truly have lower loss aversion or have better mechanisms for managing it[14]. Age and initial wealth also influence loss aversion: older investors and those with greater initial capital endowments exhibit stronger myopic loss aversion, paradoxically being more likely to evaluate their portfolios frequently and thus more vulnerable to the pattern of frequent small losses exceeding frequent small gains in magnitude[27].

The disposition effect—investors' tendency to sell winners more readily than losers—demonstrates how prospect theory parameterization can either amplify or correct behavioral bias depending on how the model is specified. When prospect theory is applied with conventional parameter values (loss aversion coefficient of approximately 2.25, risk aversion parameters near 0.88), the model predicts that investors should almost never voluntarily sell positions in gains unless compelled by external factors, yet this prediction starkly contradicts observed behavior where investors regularly sell appreciated positions[43]. This failure suggests that either the standard prospect theory parameterization is substantially incorrect, or that prospect theory with standard parameters overparameterizes loss aversion, predicting behavior more extreme than investors actually exhibit. However, research on how financial advisors can mitigate the disposition effect by manipulating information about paper gains and losses reveals that making position costs salient actually increases the disposition effect—investors become more loss-averse when reminded of their purchase prices—suggesting that loss aversion, once activated, does indeed amplify according to prospect theory principles[60].

## The Role of Reference Point Endogenization and Dynamic Adjustment

A crucial but often-overlooked factor in determining whether behavioral models amplify or correct loss aversion effects is how reference points are specified and whether they adjust dynamically over time. Traditional static prospect theory models treat the reference point as exogenously fixed, typically at the current wealth level or perhaps at recent historical wealth. However, research on reference point endogenization demonstrates that when investors can mentally adjust their reference point—adapting their aspiration level based on recent experience and new information—the implications for loss aversion and risk-taking become qualitatively different[44]. Specifically, when reference points endogenize, loss aversion becomes less consequential for portfolio choice because the locations and slopes of the value function around the reference point effectively adjust as the reference point itself moves.

The dynamic aspect of reference point adjustment proves particularly important in understanding whether behavioral models amplify or correct loss aversion effects in real-world contexts. When investors experience losses and fail to psychologically accept these losses as permanent, they delay painful reductions in consumption and maintain aspirational reference points higher than current wealth, creating a distinct risk-return profile where investors accept greater portfolio risk to try to recover to their previous reference point[47]. This behavior—attempting to reach a target and accepting greater risk when below it—can appear as amplified loss aversion in static models, yet it reflects a dynamic optimization process where time-varying reference points and different loss aversion in different regions create apparent amplification that is actually endogenous to the solution of the investor's optimization problem. The welfare implications are substantial: investors employing such dynamic loss aversion strategies may suffer welfare losses exceeding 10 percent compared to constant relative risk aversion strategies, suggesting that the behavioral model capturing dynamic reference point adjustment predicts more substantial behavioral costs than simpler static models.

The temporal framing of decisions significantly affects how loss aversion operates in behavioral models. Classical behavioral finance research demonstrated that myopic loss aversion—the tendency to evaluate investment performance frequently and exhibit heightened loss aversion in shorter evaluation periods—substantially reduces investors' willingness to hold risky assets[27][30]. However, more recent experimental work reveals that this effect is not simply an artifact of investor irrationality but reflects rational optimization under the constraint that investors evaluate performance frequently. When investors are constrained to evaluate portfolios quarterly rather than daily, their effective risk tolerance increases substantially, not because their loss aversion changes but because the shorter evaluation windows reduce the frequency of small losses that collectively outweigh small gains. This finding suggests that models incorporating myopic loss aversion with standard parameters may correctly predict behavior—not by amplifying loss aversion but by incorporating the legitimate temporal structure of actual investor decision-making.

## Heterogeneous Agent Models and Behavioral Equilibrium Concepts

The evolution toward heterogeneous agent models in behavioral finance reflects an increasingly sophisticated recognition that a single behavioral parameter cannot adequately characterize how loss aversion affects market outcomes when different agents have different behavioral characteristics or when behavioral characteristics themselves vary across time and states of the world. Asset pricing models with heterogeneous agents incorporating both fundamentalist investors (who believe prices will revert to fundamental values) and trend-following investors (who extrapolate recent price movements) demonstrate that behavioral equilibrium emerges through the evolutionary dynamics of switching between strategies[26][59]. In these models, when trend-following investors dominate the market, prices can deviate substantially from fundamentals, yet this deviation is not simply an amplification of loss aversion but rather reflects an equilibrium where agents optimally choose between different forecasting strategies based on recent performance.

The behavioral equilibrium framework, developed in evolutionary asset pricing models, explicitly departs from requiring beliefs to match actual choice distributions—the core equilibrium assumption in traditional QRE models. Instead, behavioral equilibrium models define equilibrium through the market-clearing asset prices that emerge when agents implement behaviorally-specified trading strategies[48]. This framework proves particularly useful for understanding whether behavioral models amplify or correct loss aversion effects because the equilibrium asset prices themselves depend on the proportion of agents using different behavioral strategies. When loss-averse agents constitute a larger share of market wealth, asset prices may reflect greater amplification of loss aversion effects; conversely, when sophisticated arbitrageurs and rational investors constitute a larger share of the market, behavioral deviations may be substantially corrected through market forces.

However, empirical evidence on how well arbitrage mechanisms correct behavioral biases is decidedly mixed. Research on crowded trades—instances where many institutional investors have converged on similar positions—reveals that crowding itself amplifies the effects of behavioral biases by creating additional risks to liquidity, fire sales, and crash risk that exist separately from any individual investor's loss aversion[57]. When many investors become loss-averse and simultaneously attempt to de-risk by selling similar positions, the collective effect amplifies beyond what individual loss aversion parameters would predict. This amplification mechanism arises from the interaction of loss aversion at the individual level with congestion in the market for assets, creating aggregate effects where the whole is substantially worse than the sum of its parts. The implication for behavioral game theory is that equilibrium analysis must account not only for how individual loss aversion shapes individual choices but for how the aggregate of such choices creates market-level congestion effects that further amplify behavioral distortions.

## Cognitive Biases Beyond Loss Aversion: Probability Weighting and Belief Formation

While loss aversion has received the most attention in discussions of behavioral game theory and prospect theory, the full parameterization of prospect theory includes several other critical components that shape how models balance rational equilibrium with bias correction. Probability weighting—the observation that individuals do not assign decision weights to probabilities in accordance with their objective values—creates a non-linear transformation where small probabilities are overweighted and large probabilities are underweighted[2]. This feature of prospect theory proves particularly important in understanding investment behavior because it can amplify behavioral distortions beyond what loss aversion alone would predict. For instance, investors may dramatically overweight the small probability of catastrophic losses, leading to excessive avoidance of risky assets even at extremely favorable expected value terms.

Recent research on belief updating biases reveals that loss aversion cannot be properly understood in isolation from how investors form and update beliefs about future returns[32]. When investors exhibit base rate neglect—a tendency to underweight historical probability distributions in favor of specific information about recent outcomes—the effective loss aversion that manifests in their behavior can appear amplified relative to their true loss aversion parameters because they are simultaneously overweighting tail risks and underweighting the base rate at which such risks historically occur. This interaction between loss aversion and probability weighting can create compound distortions where a behavioral model incorporating only loss aversion would substantially underpredict the magnitude of behavioral deviations observed in actual markets. Conversely, models incorporating both loss aversion and probability weighting can sometimes overpredict behavioral deviations when these biases work in opposing directions.

The role of memory and experienced-based recall in forming investment expectations creates another layer of complexity in understanding whether behavioral models amplify or correct loss aversion[35]. Research on investor memory demonstrates that when investors form expectations about future market returns, they rely heavily on memories of past experiences that are selectively retrieved through processes of cued recall, where recent market movements cue the recall of similarly-valenced past episodes. When the market has recently risen, investors recall periods of rising markets and form optimistic expectations about future returns; when the market has recently fallen, they recall falling markets and form pessimistic expectations. This memory-based expectation formation can amplify loss aversion effects because recent losses activate memories of previous bad market periods, making the distribution of future outcomes appear riskier and more loss-prone than historical frequencies would suggest. In this mechanism, loss aversion is amplified not because the true loss aversion parameter has changed but because the perceived distribution of future outcomes has shifted toward appearing more loss-prone.

## The Policy Implications of Bias Amplification Versus Correction

The distinction between behavioral models that amplify loss aversion effects and those that correct for them carries profound implications for financial regulation and investor protection policy. If prospect theory with standard parameterization amplifies loss aversion by incorporating affective forecasting errors, then policies designed to bring investor behavior into alignment with prospect theory predictions would be actively harmful, pushing investors toward more conservative portfolios and lower returns than they would ultimately prefer if they could accurately predict their own emotional responses. Conversely, if loss aversion reflects genuine preferences that should be respected, then behavioral models that move toward lower loss aversion coefficients may be failing to provide adequate protection for investors who face genuine risks of catastrophic outcomes.

The evidence on financial advice and disclosure practices reveals that information architecture substantially affects how investors respond to loss aversion framing[60]. When financial advisors provide information highlighting paper losses on positions—making the loss aspect of a position more cognitively salient—investors increase their loss aversion and become more reluctant to realize losses, amplifying the disposition effect. However, when the same advisors provide information that emphasizes other decision-relevant factors and de-emphasizes position-level losses, investors' disposition effect substantially declines, with consequences for portfolio returns increasing by approximately 20 basis points per month. This evidence suggests that loss aversion, at least in this context, can be successfully corrected through information architecture that manages the salience of gains and losses without requiring investors to overcome their fundamental psychological nature.

Market dynamics and investor sentiment variables also reveal the complex relationship between behavioral preferences and equilibrium outcomes. Research on how behavioral heterogeneity affects stock price dynamics demonstrates that the proportion of investors using different behavioral strategies dynamically adjusts based on recent performance, creating evolutionary dynamics where strategies that performed well in recent periods attract more capital and thus become more prevalent[56]. This switching mechanism creates multiple equilibria and the possibility of bifurcations where small changes in parameters can cause dramatic shifts in behavior. When loss-averse investors dominate the market, stock prices cluster at lower valuations with higher risk premiums; when overconfident and trend-following investors dominate, prices can exhibit bubble dynamics. Neither equilibrium "corrects" for the behavioral biases; rather, the market prices reflect the aggregate of the behavioral agents present.

## Unresolved Tensions and Future Directions

The fundamental unresolved tension in behavioral game theory applied to investment decision-making centers on whether loss aversion represents a fundamental preference that should be incorporated into models of optimal behavior or a bias to be corrected through better information and decision-support systems. The empirical evidence supports neither position fully: loss aversion clearly influences actual investor behavior, yet investors often acknowledge retrospectively that they overestimated their loss aversion ex ante. Prospect theory parameterization has proven useful for predicting certain behavioral patterns—such as the disposition effect and the equity premium puzzle—yet fails spectacularly in other contexts, such as explaining why investors hold such low equity allocations when loss aversion coefficients would not rationally justify such extreme avoidance.

The gap between academic model parameterization and field evidence remains substantial. Experimental studies using student participants and modest financial stakes typically estimate loss aversion coefficients near 2.25; studies of actual investor behavior with real money and higher stakes typically estimate coefficients substantially lower, ranging from 1.2 to 1.5; yet theoretical models designed to explain aggregate market phenomena often assume even higher loss aversion to generate predictions consistent with observed returns[38][41]. This pattern suggests fundamental misalignment between the parameterization choices made in different contexts and the underlying phenomena being modeled.

Behavioral game theory's treatment of strategic interactions introduces additional complications beyond individual loss aversion parameters. When multiple behavioral agents interact strategically, the equilibrium outcomes depend not only on how loss-averse each agent is individually but on how each agent's behavior is shaped by beliefs about others' loss aversion and predictions about how others will respond to perceived losses[21]. These higher-order effects can sometimes amplify behavioral distortions through belief contagion but can also create self-correcting mechanisms where overconfidence about others' loss aversion leads to aggressive bidding that converges toward rational equilibrium. The current state of knowledge does not permit a definitive answer to whether such mechanisms typically amplify or correct loss aversion effects in most financial settings.

## Conclusion: Toward Integrated Framework

The evidence examined throughout this analysis reveals that behavioral game theory models in investment decision-making do not simply balance rational equilibrium with bias correction in a straightforward manner. Rather, they create a complex landscape where different theoretical choices regarding loss aversion parameterization, reference point specification, and temporal framing produce fundamentally different predictions about whether behavioral models amplify or correct for loss aversion effects. Standard prospect theory parameterization with loss aversion coefficients near 2.25 appears to amplify behavioral biases by incorporating affective forecasting errors, generating predictions more extreme than investors' actual behavior reflects. Empirically-calibrated models using lower loss aversion coefficients better match realized investor behavior, suggesting that loss aversion manifests less severely in actual experience than in anticipated outcomes.

The integration of behavioral game theory with heterogeneous agent models and evolutionary dynamics adds important nuance to the amplification-versus-correction question. Market equilibria emerge from the interaction of investors with heterogeneous behavioral characteristics and beliefs about others' behavioral characteristics, creating multiple potential equilibria where behavioral effects can be amplified through belief contagion or partially corrected through the market selection of successful strategies. Policy interventions designed to correct for behavioral biases must therefore account not only for individual loss aversion but for how behavioral heterogeneity creates emergent market-level phenomena through strategic interaction.

Future developments in behavioral game theory should move toward tighter integration between individual-level laboratory evidence on loss aversion, field-based estimates of actual loss aversion parameters in financial decision-making, and structural models of how these individual parameters aggregate to influence equilibrium asset prices and returns. The field would benefit from explicit recognition that loss aversion is not a single parameter but a complex phenomenon that operates differently in different contexts, with different temporal frames, and for different investor populations. Rather than seeking a universal loss aversion coefficient, theoretical work should focus on understanding how the contextual factors that drive variation in loss aversion across settings also drive variation in how loss aversion operates in strategic equilibrium. Only through such integration can behavioral finance move beyond the current state where different theoretical frameworks yield contradictory predictions about whether behavioral models amplify or correct the role of loss aversion in investment decision-making.
