# Balancing Real-Time Flight Control and Global Path Planning Optimization in Autonomous Drone Navigation Under Sensor Fusion Constraints

Autonomous drone navigation systems face a fundamental challenge that represents one of the most critical tensions in robotics and aerospace engineering: the need to simultaneously achieve optimal global path planning while maintaining real-time responsiveness of flight control systems, all while integrating data from multiple sensors with inherent uncertainties and limitations. This report examines how contemporary autonomous aerial systems navigate this complex optimization landscape, analyzing the architectural solutions, algorithmic approaches, and sensor fusion methodologies that enable practical autonomous flight in diverse operational environments. The challenge is particularly acute because global path optimization typically requires extensive computation across large solution spaces, while flight control systems must operate at high frequencies (often 100-400 Hz) with strict latency constraints to maintain stable, safe flight. Sensor fusion further complicates this balance by introducing measurement uncertainties, computational overhead, and the need for real-time data integration across heterogeneous sensor modalities. This report synthesizes current research and practical implementations to demonstrate how hierarchical planning architectures, model predictive control frameworks, and intelligent sensor fusion strategies create systems capable of achieving both responsiveness and optimality.

## Hierarchical Decomposition: The Fundamental Solution Architecture

The dominant approach to resolving the tension between real-time control responsiveness and global path planning optimality involves decomposing the navigation problem into hierarchical layers, each operating at different temporal and spatial scales[3][11][8]. This architectural pattern has emerged across numerous autonomous vehicle implementations and represents a fundamental principle in how modern autonomous drones balance competing objectives. The highest level of the hierarchy performs global route planning, considering mission-level objectives, large-scale terrain features, and strategic waypoints while deliberately ignoring fine-scale obstacles that would create computational intractability[3][11]. At this level, algorithms like A* search with heuristic guidance, Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT), and Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM) can optimize for distance, fuel consumption, flight time, and other mission-critical metrics without being constrained by the real-time requirements that plague lower-level controllers[3].

Below the global planning layer, intermediate levels implement local corridor generation and tactical replanning mechanisms that refine the global solution within time-bounded computation windows[3][8]. These middle-layer planners convert the global route into detailed waypoint sequences and define admissible flight corridors—three-dimensional volumes within which the lowest-level controller can operate safely while maintaining flexibility to respond to real-time environmental conditions[3]. The key insight enabling this decomposition is that global reoptimization need not occur at control loop frequencies; instead, replanning can be triggered by specific events such as significant path tracking errors, newly detected obstacles that violate corridor constraints, or changing environmental conditions that render the previous global plan suboptimal[3][11][14]. This event-driven replanning approach enables the hierarchical architecture to maintain global optimality considerations without creating computational bottlenecks in the real-time control loop.

The lowest level of the hierarchy implements the actual flight control system, operating at frequencies measured in hundreds of hertz to maintain aircraft stability and track reference trajectories generated by higher levels[7][10][25]. These real-time controllers operate within strict latency budgets—typically 10-50 milliseconds depending on aircraft dynamics—and cannot afford to wait for optimized solutions from global planners[7][10]. Instead, they implement feedback control laws that track locally optimal trajectories, compensate for disturbances detected by onboard sensors, and maintain safety margins to ensure collision avoidance even if higher-level planning assumptions prove violated. The hierarchical approach thus enables both responsiveness (through fast, bounded-complexity local control) and optimality (through periodic global optimization constrained only by the requirement that replanning triggers are infrequent enough not to disrupt real-time control).

Critically, the coordination between hierarchical levels determines whether the overall system achieves genuine optimality or degrades toward suboptimal performance through poor layer integration[8][14]. Recent implementations employ global-local coordination strategies that define replanning triggers based on deviation magnitudes, remaining energy margins, and mission timeline constraints[11]. Rather than naive event-triggered replanning where any environmental change triggers expensive reoptimization, sophisticated systems implement thresholding mechanisms where only deviations exceeding specified tolerances justify replanning overhead[11]. For unmanned surface vehicles operating in dynamic ocean environments, this coordination is achieved through seamless bridging between global trajectory optimization and local reactive collision avoidance using transition Clothoid paths that maintain continuity while softening replanning time restrictions[8]. This approach demonstrates that optimal integration across hierarchical levels requires explicit design of the coupling mechanisms, not merely independent optimization at each level.

## Real-Time Model Predictive Control: Embedding Optimization in Fast Control Loops

While hierarchical decomposition handles the global-versus-local balance, the actual real-time control layer itself must balance responsiveness with optimality through carefully designed control algorithms. Model Predictive Control (MPC) has emerged as the dominant approach for embedding trajectory optimization within real-time control loops, enabling drones to simultaneously track reference trajectories generated by global planners while adapting to real-time disturbances and environmental changes[7][10][36]. Model Predictive Control formulates trajectory tracking as a receding-horizon optimal control problem where, at each control update interval (typically 10-50 milliseconds), the controller solves a finite-horizon optimization problem using current state estimates and system dynamics models[7][10]. The first control input from this optimization is applied immediately, and the optimization is resolved at the next time step using updated state information. This approach enables sophisticated disturbance rejection, constraint handling, and performance optimization within the strict real-time latency requirements of flight control.

The power of MPC lies in its ability to explicitly handle system dynamics, control constraints, and performance objectives within a unified optimization framework[7][10]. Rather than sequential decoupling where path planning generates desired waypoints and control systems attempt to track them through simple proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control, MPC jointly optimizes the trajectory and control inputs to satisfy aircraft dynamics constraints while minimizing a cost function encompassing trajectory tracking error, control effort, and secondary objectives such as energy efficiency[7][10]. For quadrotor aircraft, this enables the controller to generate smooth, feasible trajectories that respect the aircraft's nonlinear dynamics, acceleration limits, and attitude constraints while precisely tracking reference paths despite environmental disturbances. Field testing of MPC controllers on 25-kilogram unmanned aircraft has demonstrated root mean square tracking errors consistently between 8 and 20 centimeters across figure-eight, curved, and wind-disturbed trajectories during autonomous operations, confirming that real-time MPC enables practical, high-precision trajectory tracking[7].

The computational challenge of implementing MPC in real-time remains substantial, particularly for larger aircraft with longer prediction horizons necessary to capture their slower dynamics[10]. To address this challenge, contemporary implementations employ several strategies that reduce computation time while maintaining control performance. One effective approach uses Laguerre function approximations to reduce the number of decision variables in the optimal control problem, enabling real-time implementation on standard embedded processors[10]. Rather than optimizing control inputs at every time step within the prediction horizon, Laguerre functions represent the entire control trajectory using fewer basis functions, dramatically reducing computational burden while preserving the ability to generate smooth, flyable trajectories[10]. Another strategy employs gain-scheduling, where linear MPC models appropriate to different flight regimes (such as climb, cruise, and descent phases) are precomputed offline, and the controller switches between these models during flight based on current conditions[10]. This enables the computational benefits of linear MPC while implicitly accounting for the nonlinear dynamics that dominate at flight envelope boundaries.

Wind disturbance compensation represents a critical example of how MPC can balance real-time responsiveness with optimality in practical environments[33][36]. Wind is the primary challenge for low-speed fixed-wing unmanned aerial vehicles attempting to follow predefined flight paths, causing apparent deviations between airspeed and ground speed that can consume 20-50 percent of the vehicle's airspeed in small systems[33]. Rather than implementing purely reactive compensation based on measured wind disturbances, sophisticated MPC controllers incorporate wind preview information obtained from remote sensing (such as ground-based LiDAR) into the optimization horizon[36]. This forward-looking approach enables proactive compensation rather than delayed feedback control, achieving 80 percent improvements in position-holding performance compared to purely feedback-based MPC disturbance rejection[36]. The controller utilizes remote wind measurements to predict the disturbance profile over its prediction horizon and computes optimal control adjustments preemptively. This demonstrates how MPC can naturally integrate multiple information sources and planning horizons to achieve performance unattainable through sequential control approaches.

## Sensor Fusion: Foundation and Constraint for Both Planning and Control

Sensor fusion represents both an essential foundation for autonomous navigation and a source of significant computational and architectural constraints that influence the real-time control-versus-global-optimization balance[2][5][9][12][15][27]. All navigation and control decisions—whether in global path planning, local replanning, or real-time flight control—depend fundamentally on accurate state estimates (position, velocity, orientation) derived from onboard sensors[2][5][9]. However, individual sensors provide incomplete, noisy, and often conflicting information: GPS provides absolute position but with 5-10 meter errors and is unavailable indoors or in congested urban canyons; inertial measurement units (IMUs) provide attitude and short-term acceleration data but accumulate drift errors that grow linearly with time; cameras enable visual odometry and obstacle detection but struggle in low-light conditions and require substantial processing; LiDAR provides precise 3D environmental mapping but generates massive data streams (potentially 2 million points per second) that challenge onboard processors[2][5][9][27][53].

The Kalman filter, in its various forms, provides the mathematical foundation for fusing these heterogeneous sensor streams into coherent state estimates[9][15][18]. The standard linear Kalman filter optimally estimates the hidden state of linear systems with Gaussian noise by recursively updating state predictions with new measurements, accounting for the uncertainties in both the system model and sensor measurements[9][15]. The filter operates in two steps: a prediction step where the system dynamics model is used to project the state forward in time, and an update step where incoming sensor measurements correct this prediction[15]. By explicitly modeling uncertainties through covariance matrices, the Kalman filter computes how much to trust each measurement relative to the predicted state—measurements from reliable sensors or states with low prediction uncertainty receive high weight, while uncertain sensors are weighted accordingly[9][15].

However, drone navigation systems face nonlinear dynamics that violate the Kalman filter's linearity assumptions[15][18]. Aircraft orientation depends nonlinearly on angular velocities (through quaternion or Euler angle integrations), acceleration measurements depend nonlinearly on orientation due to gravity effects, and visual-based position estimates depend nonlinearly on camera parameters and 3D scene geometry[15][18]. The Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) addresses nonlinearity by linearizing the nonlinear equations around the current state estimate using Jacobian matrices, but this linearization can degrade performance when the system exhibits high nonlinearity or operates far from the linearization point[15][18]. The Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) provides a more robust alternative by using a deterministic sampling approach—it selects a set of representative "sigma points" from the state distribution, propagates these through the nonlinear functions, and recovers the posterior distribution through a weighted combination[15]. This approach more accurately handles nonlinear transformations of Gaussian distributions and typically produces better estimates than EKF for highly nonlinear systems like quadrotor aircraft[15][18].

The computational demands of sensor fusion directly impact the real-time control-versus-global-optimization balance because sensor fusion algorithms consume significant processor resources that might otherwise be available for path planning or control computation[9][31][34]. Real-time sensor fusion requires executing filter updates at 50-100 Hz or higher to maintain state estimate accuracy and responsiveness to changing environments[9]. For a typical autonomous aircraft with multiple sensor streams (GPS at 1-10 Hz, IMU at 200 Hz, camera vision at 30 Hz, LiDAR at 10 Hz), the fusion algorithm must efficiently consume and process data from heterogeneous sources arriving at different rates and with different latencies[2][5]. Mid-level sensor fusion architectures address this computational challenge by extracting higher-level features from raw sensor data before fusion—rather than fusing raw GPS signals, camera pixels, and LiDAR point clouds, the system first converts these into consistent representations (such as position uncertainty estimates, obstacle maps, and velocity estimates), then fuses these intermediate features[5]. This approach reduces computational burden while maintaining the ability to combine complementary sensor information.

Multi-sensor fusion architectures for GPS-denied environments (such as indoor flight, dense urban canyons, or GPS-jammed scenarios) demonstrate how sensor constraints influence the entire navigation system design[2]. In these environments, inertial sensors provide the primary state propagation mechanism, but IMU drift—small biases in accelerometers and gyroscopes that accumulate into large position and orientation errors—represents the fundamental challenge[2][26]. A high-quality IMU might accumulate 100 meters of position error over just three minutes of GPS-denied flight, corresponding to a drift rate of 33-35 meters per minute[54]. To combat this drift, sophisticated systems fuse IMU data with visual odometry, LiDAR-based simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), or landmark-based corrections[2][26][27]. Visual odometry systems track distinctive features across consecutive image frames to estimate the camera's motion, providing relative position updates that constrain IMU drift without requiring absolute position references[2]. LiDAR-based SLAM builds maps while simultaneously estimating the drone's position within those maps, using loop closure detection (recognizing when the vehicle has returned to a previously mapped location) to correct accumulated drift[27].

These GPS-denied navigation approaches create significant computational challenges because optical flow processing and SLAM algorithms are computationally intensive[27][30][31]. A comprehensive SLAM system combining LiDAR point cloud processing, feature extraction, loop closure detection, and pose graph optimization can easily consume 50-70 percent of a typical embedded processor's computational resources[27][31]. This reduces the available compute budget for real-time flight control and path planning. Consequently, system architects must carefully allocate computational resources: some autonomous systems implement SLAM at reduced frame rates (10-20 Hz) to free processor time for control and planning, accepting slightly degraded state estimates; others employ specialized hardware (field-programmable gate arrays, graphics processing units) to parallelize sensor fusion and control without sacrificing responsiveness[31]. The Visual Navigation System (VNS) approach addresses this challenge by using onboard cameras to create internal maps during flight, enabling drift compensation from visual odometry even in completely unknown terrain, achieving approximately 10 meters of drift per minute compared to 33-35 meters per minute without vision correction[54].

Landmark-based drift compensation algorithms demonstrate an elegant approach to managing the computational demands of sensor fusion while maintaining navigation accuracy[26]. Rather than attempting to correct drift continuously as it accumulates, these systems detect landmarks (distinctive environmental features such as building corners, unique rock formations, or artificial markers) as the drone traverses its path[26]. When the drone revisits a previously detected landmark, the associated drift between the two landmark observations can be computed and used to correct the accumulated yaw error in post-processing[26]. This approach dramatically reduces computational overhead by decoupling drift detection (performed infrequently whenever landmarks are revisited) from the real-time state estimation filter. A gyroscope bias of 0.1 degrees per second causes a 360-degree heading error after just one hour, making early drift observations crucial; landmark-based correction captures these observations opportunistically without burdening the real-time control loop[26].

## Reactive Local Planning and Real-Time Obstacle Avoidance

Despite sophisticated global planning and sensor fusion, real-world autonomous flight encounters obstacles and environmental features not captured in pre-flight mission planning. Reactive local planning algorithms enable drones to respond to these real-time discoveries within the strict computational latency constraints of flight control—typically 50-200 milliseconds[3][11][19][20]. These algorithms accept pre-computed global plans and dynamically refine them based on sensor-detected obstacles, generating collision-free trajectories within the computation budget available at each control cycle.

The Dynamic Window Approach represents a classic reactive obstacle avoidance method that remains widely used in modern systems[19][22]. The algorithm operates by discretizing the velocity space (all reachable velocity combinations within the next control interval given the vehicle's acceleration constraints) and evaluating each velocity combination using an objective function that combines three factors: alignment with the goal direction (driving progress toward the objective), distance to nearest obstacle (avoiding collisions), and smoothness of trajectory (minimizing jerky motions)[19][22]. This approach explicitly handles vehicle dynamics by only considering velocities that can actually be achieved given current motion constraints, eliminating the risk of commanding infeasible maneuvers. The evaluation occurs at 10-50 Hz, enabling rapid response to detected obstacles while respecting aircraft dynamics. For mobile robots executing high-velocity goal-directed motion in unknown environments, the Dynamic Window Approach enables robust collision avoidance without global replanning, though it suffers from the classic local minima problem where the robot becomes stuck between obstacles that create competing attractive and repulsive forces.

Virtual potential fields provide an alternative reactive navigation approach that intuively models obstacles as repulsive forces and goals as attractive forces[20][23]. The potential field is a scalar function of configuration space where the goal is at the global minimum potential and obstacles create local minima or saddle points that repel the vehicle[20]. The control law applies forces equal to the negative gradient of the potential, pushing the vehicle toward the goal while repelling from obstacles. Navigation functions represent a specialized class of potential functions that guarantee no local minima for specific system classes, enabling global convergence to goals without the local minima problem that plagues general artificial potential fields[20]. However, navigation functions require explicit computation of C-space obstacles (the set of robot configurations that cause collision), which becomes computationally expensive for high-dimensional systems like aerial vehicles with attitude constraints.

The Artificial Potential Field approach elegantly handles real-time constraints by computing forces based solely on current configuration—no optimization or search is required, only gradient evaluation. This enables evaluation at arbitrary frequencies (50-100 Hz or higher) without increasing computational burden substantially. The cost is reduced path quality and potential local minima; the benefit is guaranteed real-time responsiveness. Consequently, hybrid systems often employ artificial potential fields for local reactive control while using global planners (which might require seconds of computation) for mission-level path optimization.

More sophisticated reactive planning approaches employ receding-horizon optimization within real-time constraints[14][17]. Receding-horizon or model predictive control-based local planners repeatedly solve finite-horizon optimal trajectory generation problems at frequencies of 10-30 Hz, generating collision-free trajectories that balance obstacle avoidance (through constraint satisfaction) with reaching goal positions (through the optimization objective)[14][17]. By limiting the prediction horizon and discretizing the state-control space, these methods can solve nonconvex trajectory optimization problems in tens of milliseconds—fast enough to enable real-time replanning as new obstacles are detected[17]. The power of receding-horizon planning is that it provides locally optimal solutions within a bounded time budget, rather than either fully reactive methods (which lack optimality) or complete global optimization (which requires excessive computation).

Collision avoidance in multi-agent scenarios introduces additional complexity because the obstacle positions are not fixed but change based on other agents' motions[21][38][40]. Velocity obstacle methods address this by computing feasible velocity ranges that guarantee collision avoidance given predicted obstacle motions. The key insight is that collisions occur in velocity space (not just configuration space) when the relative velocity vector points toward the relative position vector[21]. By computing the set of relative velocities that lead to collision and identifying the complement (feasible velocities), the system can evaluate which control inputs guarantee safety even for moving obstacles[21]. This approach enables collision avoidance with moving vehicles, pedestrians, and dynamic obstacles detected during flight.

## Multi-Objective Optimization and Cost Function Design

Autonomous drone missions typically involve competing objectives that cannot be simultaneously optimized: minimizing flight distance conflicts with minimizing fuel consumption (shorter paths might require aggressive maneuvers with high energy cost), minimizing threat exposure (navigating around enemy positions) conflicts with minimizing flight time, and achieving smooth trajectories conflicts with tight obstacle clearance[39][42][43]. Balancing these conflicting objectives within the path planning framework influences how well the system can respond to real-time changes while maintaining optimality with respect to mission priorities.

Pareto optimization provides a principled approach to multi-objective path planning by computing sets of non-dominated solutions where improving one objective necessarily degrades another[43][46]. Rather than forcing planners to choose weights for a single combined cost function (which often leads to unintuitive behavior when objective scales differ vastly), Pareto approaches identify all solutions on the Pareto front—solutions such that no other feasible solution is better in all objectives simultaneously[43]. The A*-Pareto-Optimality (A*-PO) algorithm incorporates Pareto optimality into every step of A* search, evaluating both the path cost to reach each configuration and the estimated remaining cost to the goal in each objective dimension[43]. This enables planners to explore the solution space efficiently while preserving the option to trade between objectives based on mission priorities or real-time environmental changes.

The challenge of weight selection in multi-objective optimization is substantial: traditional weighted-sum approaches combine multiple objectives as \(J = w_1 J_1 + w_2 J_2 + \ldots + w_n J_n\)[39] where \(w_i\) are weights chosen to reflect objective priorities. However, the scale of each objective influences the optimization profoundly—if fuel consumption is measured in units (potentially 0-100) while threat exposure is measured in kilometers (potentially 0-1000), the weighted-sum approach is numerically dominated by threat exposure even if fuel consumption should have higher priority[39]. Weighted Chebyshev distance approaches address this by optimizing the worst relative deviation from ideal values across all objectives, preventing any single objective from dominating the solution[39]. Cost function design for path planning must therefore explicitly consider both the physical meaning of each objective and how different weight choices influence the optimization landscape.

For medical applications of autonomous drones (such as emergency response or autonomous delivery to remote patients), cost function design must balance mission success probability against efficiency metrics[39]. An aggressive path that prioritizes reaching the destination quickly might navigate dangerously close to obstacles, increasing crash probability. A conservative path that maximizes safety through large obstacle clearances might fail to reach the destination before a patient's condition deteriorates. The weighting between trajectory smoothness (driving progress toward the goal), obstacle avoidance (maintaining safety margins), and heuristic costs (estimating remaining distance to goal) must be set carefully, often through extensive simulation and field testing to understand how different weight combinations influence mission success rates under various environmental conditions.

## Wind Disturbance and Environmental Uncertainty

Environmental disturbances, particularly wind for fixed-wing aircraft, represent sources of uncertainty that must be handled throughout the navigation hierarchy, from global path planning through real-time control[33][36]. Wind dynamics couple position control with attitude control (the aircraft must tilt to counteract wind rather than maintain level flight) and create nonlinear interactions in trajectory tracking[33]. Small unmanned aircraft typically fly at speeds of 20-30 meters per second, but wind can reach 5-15 meters per second, making wind speed 20-50 percent of the aircraft's airspeed[33]. This creates a fundamental problem: the path planner might optimize a globally efficient route without accounting for wind effects, but during execution, the aircraft must deviate substantially from this path to compensate for wind, potentially violating obstacle clearances designed without wind consideration.

Sophisticated path planning approaches incorporate wind disturbance compensation directly into trajectory design rather than treating wind as a control problem to be handled reactively[33]. Wind disturbance-compensated path-following control strategies estimate wind disturbance and incorporate this estimate into the guidance vector field that provides desired airspeed direction commands to the aircraft's inner control loop[33]. The approach employs a disturbance observer that estimates the composite wind disturbance from tracking errors, then uses this estimate to compensate the nominal guidance law[33]. By scaling the compensation magnitude based on the current wind magnitude, the system handles extreme wind conditions (where wind speed exceeds aircraft airspeed) by recognizing when complete path following is infeasible and instead commanding the aircraft to minimize cross-track error rather than maintaining the exact planned track[33]. This demonstrates how planning and control must jointly account for environmental constraints that exceed the vehicle's capabilities.

Wind preview-based model predictive control represents an advanced approach to disturbance rejection that achieves substantial performance improvements through feedforward compensation[36]. Rather than relying solely on feedback control (detecting wind effects through position tracking errors and reacting), wind preview systems use remote sensing (such as ground-based LiDAR) to measure wind velocities ahead of the aircraft's flight path[36]. This dramatic performance improvement demonstrates the value of forward-looking optimization approaches that access future environmental information.

## Energy-Optimal Trajectory Planning

Beyond real-time responsiveness, autonomous drone missions must consider energy efficiency because battery capacity determines achievable mission duration and range[32][35]. Energy-optimal trajectory planning formulates path planning as a multi-objective optimization problem where fuel (or electrical energy) consumption is minimized subject to constraints on path feasibility, obstacle avoidance, and reaching the mission goal[32][35]. This contrasts with classical path planning which minimizes distance or flight time—shorter paths do not always minimize energy because aggressive maneuvers required for sharp turns or altitude changes can consume substantial energy despite reducing distance[32].

Energy consumption in flight depends on thrust generation, which varies nonlinearly with aircraft velocity and altitude. Aircraft operate most efficiently at specific velocities and altitudes where aerodynamic drag is minimized[32][35]. Consequently, energy-optimal trajectories avoid aggressive maneuvering, preferring smooth curves and constant-altitude cruise segments[32]. Multi-point trajectory optimization for minimum-fuel UAV routing constructs optimization problems where the flight path is parameterized as waypoints connected by trajectories, and the optimization adjusts waypoint positions and aircraft velocity between them to minimize total fuel consumption while respecting obstacle avoidance and other constraints[35]. This approach recognizes that optimal paths are often quite different from shortest paths—a path that maintains constant, efficient cruise speed might be longer in distance but substantially shorter in energy expenditure.

The integration of energy-optimal planning with real-time control creates an additional tension: globally energy-optimal paths often require substantial advance planning and computation, yet real-time environmental changes might necessitate deviations from the energy-optimal path. Intelligent systems balance these by computing energy-optimal reference paths during mission planning, then allowing real-time controllers to deviate within specified energy budgets[32]. If a global energy budget is sufficient to reach the destination despite deviations required for obstacle avoidance, the controller maintains the energy-optimal reference path. If energy consumption is approaching limits due to environmental challenges (strong headwinds, dense obstacle fields requiring slow speeds), the system can adaptively reweight the path planning objectives, accepting some energy inefficiency in exchange for guaranteed task completion.

## Decentralized and Multi-Agent Coordination

Autonomous missions increasingly involve multiple cooperating drones that must coordinate their trajectories to achieve collaborative objectives while avoiding collisions with each other[14][37][40][45]. Decentralized approaches to multi-agent path planning distribute planning computation across individual agents, with each agent planning its own trajectory while communicating trajectory intentions to neighbors[14][40]. This approach avoids centralizing all computation at a ground station (which creates communication bottlenecks and single points of failure) while still enabling coordination.

Decentralized receding-horizon planning combines deliberate (planned) motion strategies with reactive collision avoidance to enable coordinated multi-agent interception and formation maintenance[14]. Each agent periodically broadcasts its planned trajectory over the next planning horizon, enabling neighbors to predict collision risks. When collision risks are detected, reactive local planners modify trajectories to avoid the predicted conflicts while attempting to maintain progress toward mission objectives[14]. The receding-horizon approach enables fast replanning at frequencies of 5-10 Hz—fast enough to respond to coordination failures or newly detected obstacles, yet slow enough that communication and computation remain manageable.

Recent work on multi-agent path planning using Gaussian Belief Propagation with path integration demonstrates integration of global and local planning for multi-agent scenarios[40]. Global planners (using RRT or structured lane-based approaches) generate coarse paths, and a tracking factor ensures agents maintain adherence to these global paths while coordinating with neighbors through local belief propagation[40]. The integration reduces path deviation by 28 percent in single-agent scenarios and 16 percent in multi-agent scenarios compared to naive local planning without global path awareness[40]. This demonstrates that even in decentralized systems, global path information provides substantial performance benefits, motivating the preservation of hierarchical planning structures even in multi-agent scenarios.

## Conclusion: Synthesis and Future Directions

The challenge of balancing real-time flight control responsiveness with global path planning optimality under sensor fusion constraints has been addressed through a sophisticated ecosystem of complementary architectural and algorithmic approaches that have matured over the past decade. Hierarchical planning architectures decompose the navigation problem across multiple temporal and spatial scales, enabling global optimization at frequencies of 0.1-1 Hz and local real-time control at 50-400 Hz, with intermediate replanning at 5-20 Hz triggered by significant environmental changes rather than occurring continuously. Model Predictive Control has emerged as the dominant real-time control methodology because it embeds trajectory optimization within control loops, enabling drones to simultaneously track global plans while adapting to real-time disturbances and constraints. Sensor fusion through Kalman filtering (in its linear, extended, and unscented variants) provides the foundation for all navigation decisions, though the computational demands of multi-sensor fusion remain a significant constraint on overall system responsiveness, requiring careful architectural choices about which computations execute in real-time versus offline post-processing modes.

Reactive obstacle avoidance algorithms (spanning from simple Dynamic Window approaches to sophisticated receding-horizon planning) ensure that real-time environmental discoveries do not prevent safe flight, though at the cost of deviating from globally optimal paths. Multi-objective optimization approaches (including Pareto optimization and weighted Chebyshev methods) enable planning systems to balance competing mission objectives, though weight selection remains partially an art requiring extensive tuning and testing. Emerging integration of foundation models with classical planning algorithms may enhance semantic reasoning within path planning, though preliminary evidence suggests these models remain most useful at high-level mission interpretation rather than real-time spatial planning.

Advanced approaches to specific environmental challenges—wind preview-based MPC for disturbance rejection, energy-optimal trajectory planning for extended mission duration, and decentralized coordination mechanisms for multi-agent operations—demonstrate how the fundamental planning-control balance operates across diverse operational domains. Throughout these advances, a unifying principle emerges: the most effective autonomous systems do not choose between real-time responsiveness and global optimality, but rather architect their decision-making across multiple hierarchical levels operating at appropriate temporal and spatial scales, enabling both responsiveness (through fast, local reactive control) and optimality (through periodic global optimization constrained by event-driven replanning triggers). Future advances will likely focus on further automation of the architectural design process itself, using learning-based approaches to dynamically allocate computational resources and automatically tune the weights and triggers that coordinate across hierarchical levels based on mission-specific requirements and observed environmental conditions. The convergence of classical planning algorithms with modern machine learning, combined with increasingly capable embedded processors and sophisticated sensor fusion frameworks, promises to enable autonomous systems that seamlessly integrate millisecond-scale flight control responsiveness with optimizations spanning mission-duration timescales, fundamentally advancing the capability and reliability of autonomous flight operations.
