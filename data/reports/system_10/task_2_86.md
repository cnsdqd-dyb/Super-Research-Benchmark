# Data Quality and Attribution Uncertainty in Climate Agreement Verification Using Remote Sensing

Climate verification systems fundamentally depend on reliable, transparent data to ensure environmental integrity and build market confidence in emissions reductions and carbon removals. Remote sensing technologies have emerged as powerful tools for measuring forest carbon stocks, tracking deforestation, and monitoring other climate-relevant variables at scales previously impossible through ground-based methods alone. However, the integration of satellite data into international climate verification frameworks under the Paris Agreement reveals significant challenges in managing data quality and quantifying the uncertainties inherent in attributing emissions reductions to specific projects or policies. This report examines how data quality and attribution uncertainty are currently handled in climate agreement verification using remote sensing, synthesizing recent developments in technology, methodology, and governance while identifying critical gaps that must be addressed to ensure credible climate accounting.

## Remote Sensing Technologies and Their Application to Climate Verification

Remote sensing has revolutionized the landscape of environmental monitoring by enabling continuous, scalable observation of Earth's surface without direct ground intervention. Technologies including satellite imagery, Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), radar, and specialized sensors have created unprecedented opportunities to assess forest structure, detect deforestation, monitor carbon stocks, and verify climate commitments at multiple scales. The fundamental advantage of remote sensing lies in its ability to provide consistent, non-intrusive measurements across vast or inaccessible areas where traditional field-based monitoring would be prohibitively expensive or logistically impossible.[1][2]

Satellite platforms like Sentinel-2 from the European Union's Copernicus program and Landsat operated by NASA and USGS provide multispectral imagery that tracks changes in land cover, vegetation patterns, water bodies, and atmospheric conditions with increasing temporal frequency and spatial resolution.[2] For carbon verification specifically, LiDAR-based measurements of forest canopy height have emerged as particularly valuable tools, as they enable direct estimation of above-ground biomass and carbon stocks across entire landscapes.[16] Meta has released an open-source Canopy Height Map based on advanced machine learning analysis of satellite imagery, representing an important effort to democratize access to high-resolution forest structure data that was previously available only through expensive commercial acquisition.[16] When combined with field measurements and sophisticated software models, remote sensing can determine with far greater accuracy where forests exist, how much carbon they contain, and how these conditions change over time.[1]

However, the application of these technologies to climate verification must navigate complex tradeoffs between spatial resolution, temporal frequency, cost, and the inherent limitations of space-based observation. Ground-based measurements will always remain necessary because remote sensing alone cannot provide the complete picture needed for rigorous carbon accounting.[1] The integration of satellite data with field measurements creates a hybrid approach that leverages the scalability of remote sensing while grounding estimates in field-verified reality. This layered approach has become the industry standard for forest carbon projects seeking verification under major carbon standards, yet the methodologies for combining these data sources and quantifying the resulting uncertainties remain inconsistently applied across the sector.

## Uncertainty Quantification in Satellite-Based Climate Variables

Understanding and properly characterizing uncertainty represents one of the most critical and challenging aspects of using satellite data for climate verification. Uncertainties in satellite observations emerge from multiple sources throughout the data processing pipeline, and these uncertainties propagate and compound as raw satellite data are transformed into climate-relevant information products.[3][6][21] The scientific community distinguishes between two fundamental categories of uncertainty that manifest differently in satellite data: aleatoric uncertainty, which arises from inherent randomness and measurement noise that cannot be eliminated, and epistemic uncertainty, which stems from inadequate models, insufficient training data, or incomplete understanding of processes.[3][21]

Aleatoric uncertainties in satellite observations include sensor noise, inherent variability in measurement systems, and errors caused by challenging observing environments such as cloud cover or atmospheric conditions.[3][21] These uncertainties are irreducible in the sense that they represent fundamental physical limitations of the measurement process. For example, optical sensors cannot penetrate clouds, and this physical constraint creates unavoidable gaps and biases in optical satellite imagery, particularly in tropical regions with persistent cloud cover.[34] Epistemic uncertainties, by contrast, result from limitations in retrieval algorithms, model assumptions, inadequate training datasets, or insufficient knowledge about the processes being observed.[3][21] These uncertainties can theoretically be reduced through improved methodology, better calibration, more training data, or enhanced understanding of physical processes, though the practical limits of improvement remain uncertain.

The propagation of uncertainty through satellite data processing pipelines creates a fundamental challenge for climate verification.[6][44] Raw satellite data at the Level 0 stage contain primary aleatoric uncertainties from sensor noise and real-world variability. These uncertainties propagate to Level 1 calibrated radiances, where additional uncertainty emerges from calibration models and processing strategies that introduce epistemic components.[3][44] As data move through Level 2 geophysical retrievals and Level 3 gridded products, uncertainties compound because multiple data sources and models are combined, each potentially introducing additional error sources.[3][6][44] By the time satellite data are transformed into decision-relevant climate information at higher processing levels, the total uncertainty may be substantially larger than uncertainties at lower levels, yet this information is often not transparently communicated to end users.[6][44]

A critical finding from recent research demonstrates that the spatio-temporal correlation structure of errors matters profoundly for climate applications but is frequently underestimated or ignored.[3][6][44] Satellite observation errors are not randomly distributed in space and time; instead, they often exhibit coherent spatial and temporal patterns that result from systematic biases in calibration, retrieval algorithms, or environmental conditions.[3][44] Failing to account for these correlations leads to substantial underestimation of total uncertainty, creating false confidence in satellite-based climate estimates.[6][44] For instance, if a systematic calibration bias affects an entire satellite mission, the correlated nature of that error means that averaging multiple observations does not reduce uncertainty as quickly as assumed by standard statistical approaches.[44] This has profound implications for trend detection and for assessing the significance of observed changes in climate variables.

Recent advances in uncertainty quantification for satellite-based essential climate variables demonstrate the potential for improved confidence in satellite data. A lightweight machine learning framework developed for satellite-based carbon dioxide retrievals exemplifies progress in this direction by enabling probabilistic predictions that simultaneously estimate both values and associated uncertainty.[14][45] The framework uses neural networks modified to predict mean and variance of retrievals, employing likelihood-based learning and ensemble techniques to generate well-calibrated uncertainty estimates that capture both measurement noise and model uncertainty.[14][45] Validation against operational products from NASA's Orbiting Carbon Observatory-2 mission showed that over 99 percent of reference values fell within predicted uncertainty bounds, while achieving retrieval speeds thousands of times faster than traditional physics-based algorithms.[14][45] This breakthrough suggests that combining machine learning speed with rigorous uncertainty quantification may help resolve longstanding tensions between computational efficiency and scientific rigor in satellite data processing.

## Monitoring, Reporting, and Verification Frameworks Under the Paris Agreement

The Paris Agreement established an ambitious framework for global climate action through nationally determined contributions (NDCs) submitted by countries, with verification conducted through the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) which requires biennial transparency reports (BTRs) beginning in 2024.[7][51][54] These reports must include national greenhouse gas inventories, information on NDC implementation progress, adaptation actions, and support provided or received.[7][51][54] At its foundation, this global accountability system depends on measurement, monitoring, reporting, and verification (MMRV) of greenhouse gas emissions and removals, which represents the critical infrastructure through which climate pledges are translated into verifiable reality.[7][10][11]

The technical architecture for MMRV under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, which enables international carbon credit trading and cooperative approaches, has been developed through the International Initiative for Development of Article 6 Methodology Tools (II-AMT).[10][11] This framework builds on experience from the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) under the Kyoto Protocol while establishing updated requirements aligned with Paris Agreement principles.[10][11] The Article 6 MRV tool specifies that monitoring methodologies must ensure collection and archiving of all relevant data necessary for estimating emissions and removals within defined project boundaries, determination of accurate baselines against which reductions are measured, identification of all potential sources of error, and comprehensive documentation of all calculation steps.[10][11]

A central requirement of the MMRV framework is that accuracy must be demonstrated and uncertainty must be quantified and reported as part of the verification process.[10][11] The modalities and procedures specify that measurement methods must satisfy maximum uncertainty requirements, with different tiers of methodology requiring different levels of accuracy.[10][11] Tier 1 methods employ default emission factors from IPCC guidelines but rely on simpler activity data collection and inherently carry larger uncertainties.[10][25][28] Tier 2 methods use country-specific emission factors developed from national data, reducing uncertainty compared to Tier 1 approaches while requiring more sophisticated data collection and analysis.[10][25][28] Tier 3 methods employ highly specific models or measurements tailored to individual projects or countries, potentially achieving much lower uncertainty but at substantially higher cost and complexity.[10][25][28]

For forest carbon projects specifically, remote sensing-based monitoring has become increasingly central to MMRV verification, as it enables "wall-to-wall" assessment of forest conditions across entire project areas rather than relying solely on scattered field plots.[16][27] However, remote sensing adoption for MMRV is not without significant challenges, and the rapid evolution of remote sensing technologies has outpaced standardization efforts within the carbon verification community.[1][16][27] Different carbon registries, methodologies, and project developers employ remote sensing data and analysis methods with varying levels of rigor, consistency, and transparency regarding uncertainty quantification.[1][16]

## Data Quality Assessment and Validation Protocols for Satellite Observations

Systematic validation of satellite products represents an essential prerequisite for their use in climate verification applications, yet validation approaches remain inconsistent across different satellite missions, data products, and scientific communities.[17][22][26][29][50] The fundamental principle underlying validation is straightforward: satellite product accuracy must be assessed through comparison with independent reference data that is inherently more accurate than the product being evaluated.[26][29][50] However, the practical implementation of this principle encounters numerous complications that reflect deep challenges in Earth observation metrology and climate science.

The challenge of acquiring appropriate validation reference data demonstrates the practical tension between what validation requires and what is feasible to obtain. Validation reference data should be spatially and temporally coincident with satellite observations, independent from the retrieval algorithm being tested, and more accurate than the satellite product itself.[26][29] Ground-based measurements from networks of field sites theoretically satisfy these requirements but are limited by sparse spatial coverage, high cost, and logistical constraints that prevent systematic sampling across the full range of conditions relevant to satellite products.[26][29][50] High-resolution satellite data can serve as validation reference for moderate-resolution products, but this approach itself introduces uncertainty about whether the higher-resolution data truly represent ground truth or simply contain different measurement uncertainties.[26][29]

For forest biomass estimation, integration of field inventory data with LiDAR-derived maps reveals the complexities and inconsistencies that arise when comparing satellite products with ground measurements.[32] A case study in Maryland demonstrated substantial discrepancies between LiDAR-derived biomass estimates and field measurements from the Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) program, with differences in some counties exceeding uncertainty bounds.[32] These discrepancies stemmed from multiple sources including allometric model selection uncertainty, differences in how forest versus non-forest areas were defined and sampled, geolocation errors in field plots, and fundamental differences between the assumptions of the two measurement approaches.[32] The study concluded that despite these challenges, integration of field data with LiDAR products remains valuable for validation purposes but requires enhanced field sampling protocols and careful attention to systematic uncertainties.[32]

A rigorous approach to satellite data quality assessment involves multiple independent validation metrics applied across diverse geographic locations and environmental conditions. The GHG Protocol recommends that validation metrics should include bias (systematic error), mean absolute error (which is less sensitive to outliers than root mean square error), coefficient of variation for temporal stability, and "percent wins" for point-by-point accuracy comparisons.[50] These multi-metric approaches acknowledge that no single validation statistic adequately characterizes satellite product performance across all use cases or environmental conditions.[50] Validation must assess not only average accuracy but also systematic biases that could preferentially over- or under-estimate emissions in particular regions or under particular conditions.[50]

The Sentinel-2 multispectral imager illustrates current state-of-practice in satellite data quality reporting. Its Level 2A bottom-of-atmosphere reflectance products achieve the specified uncertainty goal of ±5% of reference reflectance plus 0.005 for approximately 79 percent of retrieved values, indicating that roughly 1 in 5 pixels exceed the specified uncertainty threshold.[39] Water vapor retrievals meet uncertainty goals 96 percent of the time, while aerosol optical thickness retrievals meet goals 25-63 percent of the time depending on climate zone, with systematic underestimation of higher aerosol loadings.[39] These performance metrics, while reasonably good by industry standards, reveal that significant fractions of satellite retrievals fall outside specified uncertainty tolerances, and systematic biases vary by geographic region and environmental conditions.[39] Such variability in performance must be considered when applying satellite data to climate verification in different regions or ecosystems.

## Attribution Uncertainty and the Challenge of Baseline and Additionality Assessment

Perhaps the most profound challenge for climate agreement verification using remote sensing lies not in measuring current conditions but in determining what would have happened in the absence of an intervention—the counterfactual baseline scenario.[15][20][23][41] Attributing observed emissions reductions to specific projects or policies requires establishing what "business as usual" emissions would have been, then demonstrating that measured emissions reductions exceed this baseline.[15][20][23][41] This attribution problem introduces epistemic uncertainty that cannot be eliminated through better measurement technology alone; instead, it requires carefully constructed causal models and rigorous comparison methodologies.

The concept of additionality, which requires that project emissions reductions must be genuinely additional to what would have occurred anyway, has been subject to substantial scrutiny and debate within the carbon offset community.[15][20][23][41] Approximately 87 percent of carbon offsets used by major companies have been rated as "high risk" of not providing real and additional emissions reductions according to a 2024 study in Nature Communications.[1] Similarly, a 2023 investigation by the Guardian, Die Zeit, and SourceMaterial determined that 90 percent of rainforest carbon offsets by the world's largest certifier, Verra, were "worthless," suggesting massive systemic failures in additionality verification.[1] These findings have prompted intense scrutiny of how additionality is determined and what baseline assumptions underlie offset credit issuance.

The fundamental difficulty stems from the inherent challenge of constructing accurate baseline scenarios. Determining what would have happened in the absence of a project involves predicting future conditions and activities, which introduces uncertainty from multiple sources including economic trends, technological advancement, policy changes, and behavioral responses.[15][20][23] Different methodologies for baseline construction can yield vastly different estimates of project additionality, yet the choice of methodology often reflects subjective judgment about which scenario represents most likely "business as usual."[15][20][23] This methodological flexibility creates opportunities for bias, whether intentional or unintentional, in overestimating baseline emissions and therefore overstating project additionality.[20][23]

The terminology used to describe baselines significantly influences how we understand additionality verification. The term "counterfactual" has been widely applied to baseline scenarios, implying something contrary to facts or not grounded in evidence.[23] However, this framing is potentially misleading, as rigorous baselines need not be purely speculative; instead, they can be grounded in empirical observations of comparable situations and causal inference techniques that draw on historical data from similar contexts.[23] Rather than relying on counterfactuals in the strict sense, additionality assessment can employ predicted baselines developed through rigorous comparison of project areas with similar regions where projects were not implemented, allowing for data-driven baseline construction.[23] Despite these possibilities for evidence-based approaches, the practice of additionality verification remains substantially subjective, with considerable variability in how different projects and verifiers assess whether emissions reductions are truly additional.

The challenge of leakage—the possibility that conservation efforts in one area simply displace emissions to another location—represents another critical attribution uncertainty.[38][41] Conservation projects that successfully reduce deforestation in one jurisdiction may inadvertently increase pressure on forests in adjacent regions if underlying drivers of deforestation (such as commodity demand or agricultural expansion) are not fundamentally addressed.[38][41] Satellite-based monitoring across broad geographic areas can help detect and quantify leakage by analyzing deforestation patterns in regions adjacent to project areas, but attributing observed deforestation to project-induced displacement versus attributing it to independent drivers remains highly uncertain.[38][41] The Amazon basin provides instructive examples, as strict conservation policies in one country have been associated with increased deforestation in neighboring areas, though definitively attributing this leakage to specific policies requires careful counter-factual analysis.[38][41]

## The Critical Gaps in Remote Sensing Standardization for Climate Verification

Despite the substantial promise of remote sensing technologies for climate verification, the carbon verification community faces significant challenges in implementing consistent, standardized approaches to measurement, monitoring, reporting, and verification. A comprehensive assessment commissioned by Meta and conducted by Carbon Direct examined the landscape of remote sensing applications to forest carbon projects through interviews with nearly 40 forest carbon project developers, carbon credit registries, due diligence providers, and landowners.[1][16] The research identified that while remote sensing stakeholder communities are highly aligned on working toward common goals, three major barriers consistently emerge: lack of clear standards for remote sensing methods, limited technical knowledge among project developers, and difficulty accessing data that is hard to obtain or prohibitively expensive to acquire.[1][16]

The lack of industry-wide standardization for remote sensing measurement, monitoring, reporting, and verification represents perhaps the most critical impediment to scaling forest carbon projects with credible verification.[1][16] Different methodologies, data sources, and analytical approaches create uncertainty about whether remote sensing estimates are comparable across projects or regions. Standardization would require defining acceptable workflows for using remote sensing data, determining geographic regions in which particular datasets and models are applicable, establishing consistent approaches to quantifying and reporting data uncertainty, and folding uncertainty estimates into how carbon credits are issued.[1][16] Additionally, the carbon credit industry requires global benchmark datasets against which individual project progress can be calculated, and centralized data portals that make relevant datasets accessible to all stakeholders.[1][16]

The research identified that a dedicated forest carbon measurement, monitoring, reporting, and verification consortium would be valuable for establishing these crucial standards.[1][16] Such a consortium would ideally be a trusted, impartial entity with strong scientific expertise capable of facilitating informed discussion about standardization. However, no specific organization was identified as having the authority, expertise, and stakeholder trust necessary to assume this leadership role.[1] The governance gap reflects a broader challenge in the climate verification landscape: while the technical capacity exists to implement rigorous remote sensing-based verification, the institutional infrastructure to establish and enforce consistent standards across independent verifiers, project developers, and registries remains underdeveloped.

The cost of acquiring remote sensing data, particularly LiDAR and very high-resolution satellite imagery, presents another substantial barrier to widespread adoption. While Meta's release of its open-source Canopy Height Map represents an important effort to democratize access to high-resolution forest structure data, this dataset does not provide temporal series necessary for dynamic baseline development, and local testing and validation remains necessary for each project area.[16] Some degree of direct field measurement and data gathering will always be necessary, meaning that cost cannot be eliminated through technology alone.[1] The expense of data acquisition creates an equity concern: well-funded conservation organizations and large carbon project developers can afford sophisticated remote sensing analysis, while smaller projects and projects in lower-income regions may lack resources for comparable technological solutions.

## Calibration, Harmonization, and Cross-Mission Consistency Challenges

Climate verification systems that track progress toward long-term emission reduction targets require continuous, consistent satellite observations spanning decades. However, satellite missions have finite operational lifespans, technological capabilities improve over time, and different satellite systems employ different measurement approaches. Creating coherent climate records from these heterogeneous data sources requires sophisticated calibration and harmonization approaches that themselves introduce additional uncertainties that must be quantified and reported.

The calibration of satellite instruments to absolute standards represents a foundational requirement for climate data records but remains a significant technical and organizational challenge.[19][22][49] Satellite instruments used for weather forecasting and short-term environmental monitoring are often not calibrated to the exacting standards required for detecting long-term climate change signals, which may be only 1/10th or 1/50th the magnitude of weather variations.[19] Traditional calibration approaches rely on on-board calibration sources or comparison with reference standards, but these methods degrade over time as satellite sensors age and lose sensitivity.[22][49] Achieving SI (International System of Units) traceability—establishing documented links between satellite measurements and absolute physical standards—remains a goal rather than universal practice across the satellite community.[19][49]

Multi-mission approaches to climate data records require systematic cross-calibration of different satellites to ensure that measurements from different instruments can be coherently combined into unified time series.[47][55] The multi-mission altimetry approach used for global sea level monitoring exemplifies both the necessity and complexity of this approach. Combining observations from multiple radar altimetry missions spanning more than three decades requires identifying and correcting systematic biases between missions, accounting for instrumental drifts, and maintaining consistent orbit solutions.[47][55] Radial errors between missions are estimated through analysis of crossover points where satellite ground tracks intersect, but these corrections themselves carry uncertainty that must be characterized and propagated through to final products.[47][55]

The uncertainty characterization for satellite altimetry sea level records demonstrates the comprehensive approach needed for rigorous climate verification. A complete error budget accounts for multiple error types including wet tropospheric correction (typically the largest source, explaining ~80% of trend variability), orbit determination errors, inter-calibration biases, and measurement noise, each with different spatio-temporal correlation structures.[47] The total error variance-covariance matrix enables derivation of 90 percent confidence envelopes around measurements and estimation of trend and acceleration uncertainties over any time period of 5 years or longer.[47] This level of rigor in uncertainty characterization remains rare across satellite applications to climate monitoring, yet it represents what credible climate verification should achieve.

## Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Novel Uncertainty Quantification Approaches

Artificial intelligence and machine learning are increasingly applied to satellite data processing for climate applications, offering potential for improved speed and capability but introducing new challenges for uncertainty quantification. Deep learning models can be trained to estimate forest biomass, detect land-use changes, quantify atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations, and perform numerous other tasks relevant to climate verification.[5][14][21] However, traditional machine learning approaches typically produce single-point predictions without providing uncertainty information, creating a fundamental mismatch between what scientists need (both predictions and confidence intervals) and what algorithms produce.[5][14][21]

The challenge of training machine learning models on geographic domains where labeled training data do not exist remains a significant limitation to global application of remote sensing approaches. Duke University researchers are investigating techniques to overcome this challenge through synthetic data generation—artificially created images that mimic particular regions—and self-supervised learning approaches that do not require labeled imagery data.[5] Self-supervised learning techniques that enabled recent advances in large language models are being adapted for satellite imagery analysis, showing considerable promise for reducing the need for costly labeled training datasets in every geographic region of interest.[5]

Recent breakthroughs in probabilistic machine learning frameworks demonstrate the possibility of achieving both computational efficiency and rigorous uncertainty quantification. A framework developed for satellite-based carbon dioxide retrievals integrates likelihood-based learning with snapshot ensemble modeling, enabling neural networks to simultaneously estimate both retrieval values and associated uncertainties.[14][45][48] When validated against OCO-2 satellite observations spanning 2017-2024, the approach produced retrieval speeds in milliseconds compared to minutes for traditional physics-based algorithms, while maintaining 99 percent consistency with reference uncertainty bounds.[14][45] This advance suggests that the apparent tradeoff between machine learning speed and scientific rigor regarding uncertainty may be surmountable through careful algorithm design.

However, substantial challenges remain in ensuring that machine learning-based approaches properly capture and communicate all sources of uncertainty, particularly those related to model inadequacy and domain shift. Deep learning models trained on specific geographic or temporal domains may perform poorly when applied to different conditions, yet this model uncertainty is difficult to quantify through standard statistical approaches.[3][21] The lack of ground truth for validation poses a fundamental barrier to uncertainty calibration for many satellite-derived climate variables, particularly in remote or understudied regions.[3][21] Furthermore, the question of whether purely statistical deep learning paradigms are sufficient to quantify uncertainties related to physical domain knowledge—especially the "unknown unknowns" of total uncertainty—remains philosophically and practically unresolved.[3][21]

## Regional Variation in Data Quality and Coverage Bias in Satellite Observations

Satellite Earth observation systems do not provide uniform global coverage; instead, systematic biases in data availability exist that reflect both physical factors (orbital characteristics of satellite constellations) and economic factors (commercial demand for satellite imagery in wealthy regions).[43] A recent analysis of high-resolution satellite imagery from major constellations reveals that locations farther from the equator are generally revisited more frequently, with polar regions receiving approximately 1,000 revisits per 30 days compared to approximately 80 revisits for equatorial regions.[43] This disparity results directly from the orbital dynamics of sun-synchronous orbits employed by most satellite systems, creating an inherent geographic bias in observation frequency.[43]

Beyond orbital factors, socio-economic status significantly influences availability of historic satellite imagery, with higher-resolution imagery more frequently available for wealthier regions and lower-resolution imagery predominating in lower-income regions.[43] Analysis of metadata from major satellite image archives (Up42, Maxar, Planet) for 2017-2023 demonstrates that population density, regional income levels, and subnational location substantially influence whether high-resolution imagery has been commercially tasked in particular locations.[43] This creates a concerning disparity: regions with the highest climate vulnerability and the greatest need for verification of conservation and climate adaptation investments may have the least access to high-quality satellite observations needed for rigorous monitoring and verification.[43]

Cloud cover represents another geographic source of uncertainty in optical satellite observations, with tropics experiencing persistent cloud cover that prevents optical sensors from observing surface conditions with the frequency available in more temperate zones.[34][31] Vegetation indices derived from multispectral satellites experience systematic biases when cloud conditions differ between calibration measurements and actual observations, with cloud-induced biases in the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) varying by environmental conditions and spectral bands used.[31] These systematic regional variations in measurement quality and availability must be considered when designing climate verification systems that aspire to equitable, comparable verification globally.

## Harmonizing MMRV Requirements Across Paris Agreement Reporting

The Enhanced Transparency Framework requires countries to provide biennial transparency reports on progress toward nationally determined contributions, creating substantial demand for rigorous, consistent, and transparent greenhouse gas accounting methodologies.[7][51][54] The Katowice Rulebook established that all parties must use 2006 IPCC Guidelines as the mandatory basis for greenhouse gas inventory compilation beginning in 2024, ensuring some degree of methodological harmonization.[7] However, flexibility in how these guidelines are applied, coupled with substantial variation in national technical capacity, creates potential for inconsistency in inventory quality and uncertainty characterization.

Effective use of remote sensing for NDC verification requires alignment between what remote sensing can reliably measure and what inventory methodologies require for tracking progress. The USDA's Federal Strategy to Advance Greenhouse Gas Emissions Verification in agriculture and forestry illustrates ongoing efforts to integrate Earth observation data into national GHG quantification frameworks.[30] Priorities include improving estimates of forest carbon stocks through integrated use of field measurements and remote sensing, establishing soil carbon monitoring networks using direct measurements verified through remote sensing, improving tracking of wildfire emissions through satellite monitoring, and accelerating adoption of applications that support climate-smart agriculture and forestry.[30]

However, gaps remain between what remote sensing can provide and what verification systems require. Remote sensing excels at detecting changes in land cover and tracking deforestation at landscape scales, but determining the underlying causes of those changes—and therefore attributing emissions reductions to specific policies or projects—requires integration of remote sensing with economic, institutional, and behavioral data that satellites cannot provide.[30] Attribution of emissions reductions to particular interventions ultimately depends on causal reasoning that goes beyond what measurement technology alone can accomplish, no matter how sophisticated that technology becomes.

## Recommendations for Advancing Data Quality and Uncertainty Handling in Climate Verification

Addressing the identified gaps in data quality and attribution uncertainty handling for climate agreement verification using remote sensing requires coordinated action across multiple dimensions. First, establishing the proposed forest carbon MMRV consortium with appropriate governance, scientific authority, and stakeholder representation could provide the institutional foundation for standardization.[1][16] This consortium should develop explicitly standardized workflows for using remote sensing in MMRV, define geographic domains in which particular datasets and models are appropriate, establish consistent protocols for quantifying and reporting data uncertainty, and create benchmarking datasets enabling comparison across projects and regions.[1][16] The consortium would ideally operate with transparent, metrological principles ensuring that uncertainty information is not merely acknowledged but actively integrated into carbon credit issuance and verification decisions.

Second, investment in developing "benchmark missions" with instruments calibrated to absolute standards and SI-traceable measurements could provide the foundation for improved consistency across Earth observation systems.[19][49] These benchmark instruments would serve as calibration references for other satellite sensors, reducing the complexity and uncertainty of multi-mission calibration efforts.[19][49] While the cost of such benchmark missions is substantial, the scientific and policy value of coherent, long-term climate records spans all Earth observation applications, providing justification for this investment as climate monitoring infrastructure analogous to ground-based weather monitoring networks.

Third, prioritizing data accessibility and cost reduction for remote sensing products is essential to ensure equitable access to verification capabilities across regions and project developers. Meta's release of the global Canopy Height Map represents a model for openly available, high-quality remote sensing products; similar efforts to produce and share other essential climate variables at no cost would democratize access to verification technologies. Regional satellite programs, particularly in tropical regions vulnerable to deforestation, could be supported to develop in-country remote sensing capabilities that enable verification independent of expensive external data acquisition.

Fourth, strengthening the connection between uncertainty quantification in satellite data and carbon credit issuance would ensure that uncertainty does not represent a hidden cost absorbed by buyers or a manipulation opportunity for project developers. Establishing maximum acceptable uncertainty thresholds for different credit types, requiring that credit issuance be explicitly discounted for documented uncertainties, and making uncertainty information transparent to all market participants could substantially improve offset quality.[1][16]

Finally, advancing the science of attribution and causal inference for climate verification requires investment in methodologies that can rigorously determine what would have happened in the absence of projects or policies. This involves developing consistent baseline methodology standards, incorporating rigorous comparison group designs where possible, and explicitly quantifying and communicating the uncertainty inherent in counterfactual scenarios.[23][41] Statistical approaches such as conformal prediction sets that provide rigorous uncertainty quantification for climate projections offer potential methodological advances that could be adapted to baseline and additionality assessment.[33]

## Conclusion

Data quality and attribution uncertainty represent complementary but distinct challenges for climate agreement verification using remote sensing. Remote sensing technologies have genuinely transformed the landscape of environmental monitoring, enabling continuous, scalable observation of forest conditions, deforestation, and other climate-relevant variables at unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution. These capabilities are essential for implementing the Paris Agreement's ambitious verification requirements and enabling robust carbon credit markets. However, the substantial promise of remote sensing must be tempered by honest assessment of remaining limitations and challenges.

On the data quality dimension, satellite measurements carry irreducible uncertainties from sensor noise and measurement limitations alongside reducible epistemic uncertainties from algorithmic choices and insufficient calibration. These uncertainties propagate and compound through data processing pipelines in ways that are frequently underestimated or inadequately communicated to end users. Spatio-temporal correlations in measurement errors mean that simple averaging does not reduce uncertainty as efficiently as assumed, and regional variation in cloud cover, orbital characteristics, and data availability create systematic biases in what can be observed globally. Rigorous validation of satellite products through independent reference data remains limited by the practical challenges of obtaining appropriate validation datasets, and performance metrics demonstrate that satellite retrievals frequently exceed specified uncertainty thresholds, particularly in challenging environments.

On the attribution dimension, the fundamental challenge of determining what would have happened in the absence of an intervention remains stubbornly resistant to purely technological solutions. Satellite remote sensing cannot directly measure counterfactual baselines or quantify the causal effects of specific policies and projects; instead, these require carefully constructed models that inevitably embody subjective assumptions and judgments. The substantial failure of past offset verification systems to ensure additionality, with recent studies finding 87-90 percent of offsets at high risk of providing no real emissions reductions, reflects deep challenges in baseline and additionality methodology that cannot be overcome by better measurement of current conditions alone.

Progress requires treating data quality and attribution uncertainty as complementary dimensions of a unified verification challenge rather than as separate technical problems. High-quality measurement data is necessary but insufficient for credible climate verification; simultaneously, rigorous causal attribution is impossible without understanding and properly characterizing measurement uncertainties. The institutional infrastructure for harmonizing remote sensing approaches across the carbon verification community remains underdeveloped, creating opportunities for both inadvertent inconsistency and deliberate manipulation through methodological choices that favor inflated emissions reductions estimates.

The pathway forward involves coordinated action across multiple dimensions: establishing formal standardization governance for forest carbon MMRV, investing in SI-traceable benchmark satellites to improve multi-mission consistency, democratizing access to high-quality remote sensing data through open-source products and regional capacity development, explicitly linking uncertainty quantification to credit issuance decisions, and advancing causal inference methodologies for rigorous baseline and additionality determination. These advances would transform climate agreement verification from a system where measurement and attribution uncertainties are often hidden or inadequately addressed into one where uncertainties are transparent, rigorously quantified, and actively incorporated into decision-making. Only through such comprehensive approaches can remote sensing realize its genuine potential to provide credible, equitable verification of climate action under the Paris Agreement.
