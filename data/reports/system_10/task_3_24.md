## Theoretical Foundations of Digital Interaction Hypergraphs and Trust Networks

Digital interaction hypergraphs represent a fundamental departure from traditional pairwise network models, allowing simultaneous interactions among arbitrary numbers of participants rather than confining relationships to dyadic exchanges[1][4][27]. In these structures, nodes represent agents or entities participating in digital systems, while hyperedges represent collective interactions involving more than two nodes concurrently. The emergence of higher-order interactions in digital systems reflects realistic complexity in contemporary information ecosystems, where decisions, communications, and collaborative processes frequently involve group-level dynamics that cannot be adequately captured by simple graphs[1][9][12]. Trust, in the context of digital interaction hypergraphs, represents the probabilistic or deterministic assessment by one agent of the reliability, competence, and benevolence of other agents or groups with which they interact[2][25].

The representation of trust relationships through matrices has emerged as a standard mathematical approach in network analysis. Trust matrices T encode the degree to which agent i trusts agent j, typically as real-valued entries \(T_{ij}\) representing the strength of trust relationships[2][28]. In the context of hypergraphs, these matrices extend to higher-order structures through tensor representations, where interactions among multiple agents are encoded in multi-dimensional arrays[1][6][14]. The fundamental mathematical object for analyzing hypergraph structure is the adjacency tensor, which generalizes the adjacency matrix from graphs to hypergraphs. For a k-uniform hypergraph where all hyperedges contain exactly k vertices, the adjacency tensor \(\mathbf{A}\) has entries that indicate which k-tuples of vertices form hyperedges[1][6][14][24].

Trust matrices in digital systems frequently exhibit specific structural properties that distinguish them from arbitrary networks. Structural balance theory provides a foundational framework for understanding how trust and distrust relationships interact within triadic configurations, suggesting that triangles of relationships tend toward balanced states where the product of signs along any cycle is positive[2]. This theory predicts that within triads, if agent A trusts B and B trusts C, then A is more likely to trust C, reflecting transitivity of trust relationships. Extended structural balance theory has been applied to large-scale social networks with heterogeneous trust strengths, accounting for situations where relationships vary from strongly positive through neutral to strongly negative[2]. Such extensions acknowledge that real trust networks exhibit continuous rather than binary trust values, with significant implications for stability analysis.

The stability of interaction forms in digital hypergraphs fundamentally depends on how trust relationships constrain or enable group behaviors. When agents must coordinate through higher-order interactions, the collective stability of such coordination depends both on individual agents' trust in group decisions and on the global structure of trust relationships throughout the system[9][23][27]. In biological systems, social networks, and engineered multi-agent systems, these stability properties determine whether synchronized behaviors, consensus formation, or cooperative equilibria are achievable and robust against perturbations[9][20][23][27].

## Spectral Radius: Mathematical Properties and Physical Significance

The spectral radius constitutes the largest absolute value among all eigenvalues of a matrix, serving as a critical quantity in analyzing matrix behavior and network stability[7][10][13][19][22]. For a trust matrix T representing relationships in a network, the spectral radius \(\rho(T)\) corresponds to the magnitude of the dominant eigenvalue and fundamentally constrains the growth or decay of matrix powers \(T^n\) as n increases[7][10]. The Perron-Frobenius theorem establishes foundational results showing that for non-negative matrices (which naturally describe trust strengths), the spectral radius equals a real, non-negative eigenvalue, and moreover, there exists a non-negative eigenvector associated with this eigenvalue[38][41]. This mathematical structure has profound implications for network dynamics because it implies that the long-term behavior of diffusive processes on networks, such as information propagation or belief diffusion, is ultimately dominated by the spectral radius and its associated eigenvector[28].

In the context of hypergraphs, the spectral radius takes on enhanced importance because the spectral theory of higher-order structures is substantially richer than for ordinary graphs[1][4][9][12][24]. For m-uniform hypergraphs, where each hyperedge contains exactly m vertices, the adjacency tensor \(\mathbf{A}\) defines a natural spectrum through tensor eigenvalue problems. The H-eigenvector centrality and Z-eigenvector centrality represent two distinct generalizations of matrix eigenvector centrality to tensors, each capturing different aspects of node importance in hypergraph structure[1][14]. The Z-eigenvector formulation defines eigenvalues \(\lambda\) and eigenvectors x through the equation \(\mathbf{A}x^{m-1} = \lambda x\), where the exponentiation indicates repeated tensor-vector multiplication[1][14][39]. For strongly connected hypergraphs, the Perron-Frobenius theorem generalizes to tensors, guaranteeing the existence of a unique positive Z-eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue, which equals the Z-spectral radius[1][14][39].

The spectral radius of trust matrices encodes information about the maximal rate at which trust-based signals can propagate through the network[28]. When agents update their trust beliefs based on weighted averages of their neighbors' assessments, as occurs in many models of social learning and consensus formation, the convergence to consensus or equilibrium beliefs depends critically on whether the spectral radius of the averaging matrix remains less than unity[28]. For strongly connected stochastic matrices representing belief diffusion, aperiodicity and convergence to consensus require that the spectral radius equals exactly one, with all other eigenvalues having absolute value strictly less than one[28]. This condition ensures that repeated averaging operations drive the system toward a fixed point rather than creating oscillations or divergence[28].

In dynamical systems on networks, the spectral radius determines stability thresholds through the master stability function (MSF) framework, a powerful tool for analyzing synchronization and collective behaviors on complex networks[9][20][23][27][40]. The MSF approach decouples the dynamics of individual oscillators or agents from the network topology by analyzing stability in terms of a single parameter that depends on eigenvalues of the network Laplacian or adjacency matrix[9][20][23][27][40]. Stability regions in the MSF analysis correspond to parameter ranges where the Lyapunov exponent remains negative, indicating that perturbations from the synchronized manifold decay rather than grow[9][20][23][27][40]. The boundaries of these stability regions are precisely determined by spectral properties of the coupling matrix, with transitions occurring when eigenvalues cross critical thresholds[9][20][23][27][40].

## Trust Matrices as Representations of Network Topology

Trust relationships in digital systems embody asymmetries, heterogeneity, and complex structural patterns that substantially influence network behavior. Traditional approaches to trust representation treat trust as a scalar quantity on each directed edge, with values typically ranging from negative (indicating distrust) through zero (neutral) to positive (indicating trust)[2][25]. However, empirical studies of real trust networks reveal that trust relationships vary significantly in strength across the network, with some relationships demonstrating robust stability while others oscillate or exhibit sensitivity to perturbations[2][25]. The distribution of trust strengths fundamentally affects how information propagates, how consensus emerges, and how robustly collective coordination can be maintained against adversarial actors or noise[2][25].

In the context of hypergraphs, trust matrices must be extended to higher-order structures where the relationships are no longer simple pairs but rather groups of agents. One approach uses tensor representations where a higher-order trust relationship involves specifying trust assessments regarding the reliability of groups or collective decisions[1][14]. Another approach treats hyperedges as interactions where some nodes serve as "information sources" and others as "information recipients," creating an asymmetric structure that differs from the undirected hyperedges typically studied in spectral hypergraph theory[1]. This heterogeneous hyperedge structure complicates but also enriches the spectral analysis because it creates opportunities for spectral properties to encode the flow of trust and information through complex pathways[1].

Extended structural balance theory applied to networks with heterogeneous trust strengths reveals that the spectrum of the trust matrix encodes information about how balanced or unbalanced the network is in various structural contexts[2]. Specifically, the second-largest eigenvalue (in absolute value) of a symmetrized trust matrix often indicates the presence of community structure or clustering, with smaller eigenvalue gaps indicating stronger community boundaries[2]. The framework for analyzing trust and distrust in networks using metric distance representations maps trust relationships to distances in continuous spaces, where the eigenvalue spectrum of the associated distance matrices constrains which network configurations are achievable and stable[2].

Trust matrices in realistic digital systems frequently exhibit power-law degree distributions, where a small number of highly-trusted agents receive disproportionate attention, while most agents have sparse trust relationships[7][10][25][29][31]. This degree heterogeneity fundamentally alters the spectral properties compared to homogeneous or random networks. The spectral radius scales with the maximum degree in heterogeneous networks, meaning that the presence of highly-connected nodes increases spectral radius substantially[7][10][13]. Moreover, the relationship between spectral radius and network heterogeneity is nonlinear, with heterogeneous networks often exhibiting larger spectral radii than corresponding random networks with identical average degree[7][10]. This observation has profound implications for stability analysis because it suggests that heterogeneous trust networks may be closer to stability boundaries than homogeneous networks with similar average connectivity[7][10][29][31].

## Spectral Stability Analysis in Hypergraph Dynamics

The master stability function provides a rigorous framework for connecting spectral properties of network coupling matrices to stability conditions for dynamical systems on hypergraphs[9][20][23][27][40]. In this framework, coupled oscillators or agents on a network are analyzed by considering perturbations from a synchronized state, then deriving conditions on the network Laplacian eigenvalues under which such perturbations decay[9][20][23][27][40]. For systems with higher-order interactions, the Laplacian tensor replaces the ordinary Laplacian matrix, and the spectral theory becomes correspondingly richer[9][12][24][27].

When generalizing master stability analysis to hypergraphs, researchers study Laplace-type interaction structures in detail, investigating how different definitions of hypergraph Laplacians (normalized, unnormalized, random walk-based) produce different dynamical phenomena[9][12][24][27][40]. The spectrum of the hypergraph Laplacian determines which synchronized or patterned states are stable, with the smallest non-zero Laplacian eigenvalue indicating the maximum coupling strength compatible with stability[9][12][27][40]. Importantly, the spectral gaps—differences between consecutive eigenvalues—influence not only stability but also synchronization time scales and the robustness of synchronized states to perturbations[9][12][27][40].

Recent theoretical advances have extended master stability analysis to incorporate both pairwise and higher-order interactions simultaneously[20][23][34][43]. In such mixed-interaction systems, the interplay between pairwise and triadic (or higher) couplings creates complex phase diagrams with multiple stability regions and transitions[20][23][34]. The spectral properties of coupling matrices associated with each interaction type combine to determine overall stability, with spectral radius playing a central role in determining the maximum coupling strength compatible with stability[20][23][34]. Notably, higher-order interactions can either enhance or degrade stability depending on their relationship to the underlying network structure and the specific dynamical processes unfolding on the hypergraph[34][59].

Evidence from numerical simulations demonstrates that the spectral radius of coupling matrices fundamentally constrains the coupling strengths at which stable collective dynamics emerge[9][20][23][27][40]. When studying coupled Rössler systems on complex networks, researchers found that the critical coupling strength \(\sigma_c\) at which stable synchronization first emerges is inversely related to the second-largest Laplacian eigenvalue, which is complementary to the spectral radius consideration[23]. For networks with multiple stability regions separated by parameter ranges where synchronization is lost, the spectral gap structure determines the widths of these stability windows[23]. Such observations provide empirical support for the hypothesis that spectral properties, including the spectral radius, delimit the achievable stability regimes[23][40][43].

However, the relationship between spectral radius and stability boundaries is not monotonic. In systems with time-varying or adaptive coupling, where trust relationships themselves evolve based on agent interactions, the spectral radius itself becomes time-dependent[20][23]. In such scenarios, the stability boundary depends not only on the instantaneous spectral radius but also on how rapidly it changes and whether it crosses critical thresholds[20][23]. Moreover, in nonlinear systems far from synchronized states, perturbation analysis relying on spectral properties may fail to capture the full basin of attraction, leading to situations where linear stability analysis predicts instability but the system nonetheless converges to stable patterns through nonlinear effects[34][59].

## Stability Boundaries and Spectral Radius Thresholds

The concept of a stability boundary delineates parameter regimes where qualitatively different long-term behaviors occur in dynamical systems. In the context of trust-mediated interactions on hypergraphs, stability boundaries separate regions where agents achieve consensus or maintain stable coordinated states from regions where coordination breaks down and the system exhibits oscillations, divergence, or fragmentation[9][23][27][29][31][34][43][50][51]. The spectral radius of the relevant coupling or trust matrix emerges as a fundamental quantity determining these boundaries[7][10][13][22][29][31][51].

For linear systems of belief dynamics, where agents update beliefs as weighted averages of neighbors' beliefs with weights given by a stochastic matrix T, convergence to consensus requires that the matrix T be convergent, meaning that \(\lim_{n \to \infty} T^n p\) exists for all initial belief vectors p[28]. The necessary and sufficient condition for convergence in strongly connected networks is aperiodicity, which ensures that the spectral radius equals exactly one while all other eigenvalues have absolute value strictly less than one[28]. In such systems, the boundary between convergent and non-convergent behavior is crossed when the spectral radius reaches unity; below this threshold, convergence to a fixed point occurs, while at or above this threshold, convergent behavior may fail[28]. This represents a precise delimitation of stability by spectral radius in the linear regime[28].

In reservoir computing systems, particularly Echo State Networks (ESNs), the spectral radius of the reservoir connectivity matrix determines whether the recurrent dynamics exhibit stable, dissipative behavior or chaotic trajectories[13]. Stability requires that the pseudo-Lyapunov exponent remain negative, which typically occurs when the spectral radius is less than unity for zero-input networks[13]. However, hierarchical clustering within the reservoir can extend the range of spectral radius values compatible with stability substantially, with some systems remaining stable for spectral radii exceeding 6[13]. This observation indicates that the spectral radius alone does not fully determine stability boundaries; network structure and clustering patterns also matter significantly[13].

In control-theoretic approaches to network stabilization, where external control signals are applied to pinned nodes to drive the entire network toward desired states, stability boundaries are characterized by critical coupling strengths and feedback gains[51]. The relationship between these parameters follows a hyperbolic form, with the shape and position of the hyperbola determined by network structure through eigenvalues of the controlled network's Laplacian[51]. The vertical shift of this hyperbola depends on \(1/\mu_1(G)\), where \(\mu_1(G)\) is the smallest eigenvalue of the control matrix G[51]. This demonstrates that spectral properties directly delimit the achievable stability regions by determining the critical control parameters[51].

In systems with higher-order interactions, the situation becomes more complex because multiple spectral parameters must be simultaneously satisfied for stability[20][23][34][59]. Consider a system with both pairwise coupling (strength \(\sigma\)) and triadic coupling (strength \(\sigma_\Delta\)). The stability region in the two-parameter space (\(\sigma\), \(\sigma_\Delta\)) forms a domain whose boundary depends on eigenvalues of multiple coupling matrices simultaneously[34][59]. Recent research reveals that higher-order interactions can have counterintuitive effects: they can simultaneously increase linear stability (making attractors harder to destabilize) while decreasing basin stability (making attractors harder to find from random initial conditions)[34][59]. This decoupling between linear and global stability means that the spectral-based stability boundaries determined by master stability functions do not fully capture the global picture of stability in systems with higher-order interactions[34][59].

## The Role of Network Heterogeneity and Structural Complexity

Network heterogeneity—the nonuniform distribution of connection strengths and node degrees—fundamentally influences the relationship between spectral properties and stability boundaries. In heterogeneous networks, the spectral radius tends to be larger than in comparable homogeneous networks, implying that heterogeneous trust networks are pushed closer to stability boundaries[7][10][29][31]. Research on eigenvalue spectra of weighted and directed networks reveals that degree heterogeneity creates corrections to classical random matrix theory results (Wigner semicircle law, Girko circle law, elliptic law), with these corrections scaling systematically with the heterogeneity measures[7][10].

The interaction between network heterogeneity and stability has been studied in the context of complex ecological and social systems. Theory predicts that heterogeneous networks are generally less stable than homogeneous ones—a principle known as the diversity-stability debate[29][31]. However, recent research on networked systems with higher-order interactions demonstrates that this principle can be reversed under certain conditions[29][31]. Specifically, when higher-order interactions are embedded in sufficiently structured networks with appropriate clustering patterns, stability can actually improve with increasing heterogeneity, provided the expected number of common higher-order interaction sets for any pair of nodes is less than one[29][31]. This finding suggests that spectral radius alone cannot predict stability; the global structure of hyperedges must also be considered[29][31].

Trust matrices in realistic digital systems exhibit hierarchical structure, where trust relationships cluster into communities or groups with strong internal trust but weaker cross-community trust[2][25]. The spectral properties of such hierarchical trust structures reflect this organization, with the eigenvector associated with intermediate eigenvalues often correlating with community membership[2][25]. For purposes of analyzing stability, this hierarchical structure means that the effective spectral radius for local interaction dynamics within communities may differ substantially from the global spectral radius[2][25]. Understanding stability boundaries therefore requires analyzing spectral properties at multiple scales corresponding to different levels of the organizational hierarchy[2][25].

Directed trust networks, which often arise in systems where agents have asymmetric information or asymmetric relationships, exhibit spectral properties fundamentally different from undirected networks[2][7][10]. The Girko circle law describes the distribution of eigenvalues for random directed networks, showing that eigenvalues fill an approximately circular region in the complex plane rather than being confined to the real axis[7][10]. For directed networks with heterogeneous degree distributions, the maximum modulus of eigenvalues (spectral radius) is larger than for corresponding undirected networks, and the corrections due to heterogeneity are more pronounced[7][10]. This implies that directed trust networks may be inherently less stable than undirected networks, requiring stronger stabilizing mechanisms to maintain coordinated interaction forms[7][10].

## Trust Boundaries as Distinct from Spectral Stability Boundaries

A crucial distinction exists between trust boundaries—conceptual and practical demarcations between entities or regions with different trust levels—and spectral stability boundaries determined by eigenvalue analysis[5][52]. Trust boundaries are often defined based on data sensitivity, system type, or organizational structure rather than spectral properties[5][52]. The relationship between cybersecurity trust boundaries and spectral stability in networks represents an understudied area. The spectral properties of the full trust matrix, including these cross-boundary trust edges, determine whether coordinated dynamics across the boundary are stable[5][52]. If cross-boundary trust is weak or absent, the spectral structure exhibits a gap corresponding to quasi-disconnected components, leading to multiple spectral radii (or a complex spectrum with multiple dominant eigenvalue magnitudes)[5][52].

This observation suggests that trust boundaries and spectral stability boundaries are interrelated but distinct. Spectral analysis captures how strongly connected a trust network is and whether information or influence can flow smoothly across all parts of the network. Trust boundaries, by contrast, represent policy or design choices about which connections should or should not be trusted, independent of spectral properties. A well-designed trust boundary might intentionally disconnect components with different trust levels, deliberately creating spectral gaps that prevent synchronized dynamics across the boundary—a desirable feature from a security perspective but potentially detrimental to overall system coordination[5][52].

## Conclusion: Integration of Spectral and Dynamical Perspectives

The spectral radius of trust matrices does indeed provide meaningful constraints on stability boundaries of interaction forms in digital interaction hypergraphs, but not as an absolute determinant in all circumstances. Rather, the spectral radius serves as a fundamental quantity that encodes essential information about network capacity for supporting stable coordinated interactions. When spectral conditions are violated (spectral radius exceeds critical thresholds), stability cannot be achieved regardless of other system properties. However, spectral conditions being satisfied constitutes a necessary rather than sufficient condition for stability in most practical scenarios[9][20][23][27][34][40][59].

The relationship between spectral properties and stability boundaries operates at multiple levels simultaneously[1][6][9][12][14][24]. At the local level, the second-largest eigenvalue magnitude determines convergence rates and robustness to perturbations[23][40]. At the intermediate level, eigenvalue multiplicity and spectral gaps reveal clustering and hierarchical organization in trust networks, which profoundly influences achievable stable patterns[2][4][25][35]. At the global level, the spectrum in its entirety through the master stability function framework determines which synchronized or patterwise coordinated states are stable[9][20][23][27][40].

Heterogeneity, hierarchy, and directed trust relationships complicate the relationship between spectral radius and stability boundaries, often pushing systems closer to critical thresholds[2][7][10][29][31]. Higher-order interactions introduce additional layers of complexity, capable of both enhancing and degrading stability depending on the relationship between pairwise and higher-order coupling strengths[20][23][34][59]. The decoupling between linear stability (spectral) and global stability (basin-based) in systems with higher-order interactions indicates that comprehensive stability analysis must integrate both spectral and nonlinear perspectives[34][59].

Future research should develop methods for efficiently computing spectral properties of large-scale trust matrices and hypergraphs, enabling real-time stability monitoring in digital systems[1][6][24]. Integration of spectral analysis with machine learning and control-theoretic methods could optimize network design to maintain stability while preserving efficiency and resilience[15][33][43]. Extension of detectability theory to hypergraph community detection would characterize fundamental information-theoretic limits on stability inference from spectral properties[35]. Most importantly, comprehensive frameworks bridging spectral theory, trust dynamics, and higher-order interactions will provide the mathematical foundation for designing next-generation digital systems that maintain stable, trustworthy interaction forms at unprecedented scales[1][9][27][40].

The answer to whether spectral radius delimits stability boundaries is fundamentally affirmative yet nuanced: the spectral radius provides irreplaceable constraints on achievable stability, yet must be understood within the broader context of network structure, heterogeneity patterns, trust hierarchy, interaction dimensionality, and the specific dynamics governing agent behavior. Neither spectral properties alone nor dynamical analysis in isolation suffices; rather, their integration provides the most complete understanding of stability boundaries in digital interaction hypergraphs.
