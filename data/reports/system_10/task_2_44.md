# Balancing Execution Speed and Price Discovery in Matching Engines: The Latency Arbitrage Dilemma in Modern Markets

The tension between enabling rapid order execution and maintaining fair price discovery represents one of the most significant challenges in contemporary financial market design. This comprehensive analysis examines whether trading platform matching engines can successfully balance these competing objectives, or whether high-frequency trading's reliance on latency advantages has fundamentally compromised market integrity. The evidence suggests that while matching engines themselves are not inherently flawed, the fragmented, speed-dependent market structure that has evolved enables sophisticated exploitation strategies that blur the line between legitimate arbitrage and market manipulation, creating regulatory challenges that existing legal frameworks struggle to address effectively.

## Understanding Matching Engine Architecture and Core Functions

Modern matching engines serve as the central nervous system of electronic trading venues, automatically pairing buy and sell orders according to predefined algorithms that determine execution sequence, price, and fill sizes[1][4]. The matching engine continuously scans incoming orders and identifies compatible counterparties based on price convergence, executing trades when buyers are willing to accept the prices at which sellers are offering liquidity[1]. This automation enables execution speeds measured in microseconds or even nanoseconds, creating a market environment fundamentally different from manual floor trading where human intermediaries required seconds or minutes to complete transactions[10][4].

The most widely adopted matching algorithm is price-time priority, also known as FIFO (first in, first out), which ranks orders first by price and then by submission time[1][7][56]. Under this rule, a buyer offering the highest price receives priority over other buyers, and among buyers offering identical prices, the trader who placed their order first gets filled first[1][56][59]. This approach is considered fair and intuitive because it rewards traders who either improve the market price or provide liquidity early, aligning incentives with efficient price discovery[1][7]. The alternative pro-rata model, used in some derivatives markets, allocates fill volume proportionally based on order sizes at the best price, encouraging larger orders but creating different fairness characteristics[1][56].

Matching engine performance is determined by three critical metrics: latency, throughput, and determinism[1][4][10]. Latency refers to the speed at which orders are processed and matched, measured in milliseconds for retail-focused venues and microseconds or nanoseconds for high-frequency trading infrastructure[4][10]. Low latency reduces the likelihood of trades occurring on stale prices and prevents fast strategies from being disadvantaged by processing delays[1]. Throughput measures how many orders per second the engine can process, essential during volatile periods when order flow explodes[1][4]. Determinism ensures that the matching algorithm follows its stated rules consistently even under stress, preventing unpredictable queue jumps or fairness violations that erode trader confidence[1][56][59]. When deterministic behavior breaks down due to system overload, traders experience unexplained execution outcomes that undermine the fairness guarantees the matching algorithm was designed to provide[1][56].

The infrastructure supporting high-speed matching engines involves substantial technological investment. Bare metal systems co-located in exchange data centers achieve sub-100-microsecond latency, while cloud-based deployments struggle to achieve latency below 100 milliseconds[4][10]. The difference of a single cable's length or the choice between fiber optics and microwave transmission for long-distance data transmission can provide competitive advantages worth millions of dollars annually[4][10][11]. This technological arms race has created a market where infrastructure investment becomes a primary competitive factor, separate from trading skill or information advantage[4][10][11].

## The Latency Arbitrage Phenomenon and Its Market Implications

Latency arbitrage exploits the inevitable delays in information transmission and order processing across fragmented markets[2][5]. When the same security trades on multiple venues, market fragmentation creates temporary price discrepancies as information about trades on one exchange propagates with delay to other venues[2][5]. A trader with superior infrastructure can observe a trade occurring on Exchange A and execute against it on Exchange B before slower traders even know a price change has occurred[2][5]. This strategy operates at microsecond or even nanosecond timescales, exploiting time delays that are measured in millionths or billionths of a second[2][5].

The mechanics of latency arbitrage involve several key elements. First, the arbitrageur must have faster access to market data feeds than competitors, achieved through co-location facilities, dedicated network connections, or custom hardware that reduces processing time[2][5]. Second, when markets fragment across multiple venues, the same bid-ask spread information is reported with delay through the consolidated tape that broadcasts the NBBO (National Best Bid and Offer)[5]. An infinitely fast arbitrageur observing actual prices on each market before the consolidated tape updates can detect arbitrage situations that slower traders cannot see[5]. Third, the arbitrageur must execute trades faster than the information spreads, which requires sophisticated algorithms and minimal network latency[2][5].

Latency arbitrage is mathematically certain to generate profits when executed faster than other market participants can react, creating what researchers describe as "mechanical arbitrage rents" built into the market structure[18][31]. These profits do not depend on superior fundamental information but merely on being first to react to publicly observable information[18][31]. Research using agent-based simulations demonstrates that latency arbitrage reduces total economic surplus and negatively impacts liquidity[5]. By repeatedly executing trades at favorable prices before information fully disseminates, latency arbitrageurs extract value that would otherwise accrue to other market participants, functioning as a hidden tax on trading[2][5].

The impact of latency arbitrage on market microstructure is significant. Market makers facing potential latency arbitrage losses must widen spreads to compensate for the risk of being "picked off" by faster traders who detect when quotes become stale[2][5][11]. This adverse selection cost increases the transaction costs borne by all traders, reducing overall market efficiency[2][11]. Furthermore, when latency arbitrage is widespread, market makers have reduced incentives to provide liquidity because their quoted prices can be exploited by faster competitors[2][5][11]. The result is a vicious cycle where wider spreads attract latency arbitrage strategies, which in turn increases adverse selection risk and further widens spreads[2][11].

## Price Discovery Mechanisms and the Speed-Efficiency Trade-off

Price discovery is the process by which market prices adjust to reflect new information about asset values[1][4][56][59]. In a well-functioning market, prices should converge toward fundamental values as information disseminates and traders incorporate new knowledge[1][4][56]. The matching engine plays a crucial role in this process by facilitating the interaction of informed and uninformed traders whose order flow collectively determines prices[1][56][59]. When price-time priority rules operate perfectly, the fairest traders are those who respond to information most quickly while still providing honest prices, and informed traders willing to trade at small discounts generate revenue that attracts liquidity provision[1][4].

The relationship between execution speed and price discovery is not monotonic and involves complex trade-offs[11][18][31][34][42]. Faster execution enables prices to adjust more rapidly to new information, which appears beneficial for price discovery[11][18]. However, the opportunity for latency arbitrage to extract profits from speed alone—independent of information insight—creates what researchers identify as an inefficient form of arbitrage[11][31]. According to research on asymmetric speed bumps, there are two distinct forms of high-frequency trading: passive-side order anticipation, where HFT firms observe order cancellations and quickly cancel their own quotes before being picked off, and aggressive-side order anticipation, where HFT firms execute trades against stale quotes[11]. The latter form is economically inefficient because it extracts value purely from speed advantages rather than from superior information processing[11].

The efficiency loss from speed-dependent arbitrage manifests in reduced information production. When faster traders can profit from symmetrically observed public information merely by reacting first, the economic incentive for traders to conduct research and develop genuine information advantages diminishes[11][18][31]. Fundamental researchers receive lower compensation because their information advantage can be partially exploited by speed arbitrageurs before the information's full impact is reflected in prices[11][18][31]. This dynamic reduces the quantity of information-driven research in markets, making prices less informationally efficient even as they adjust more rapidly to public signals[11][18][31][42].

Price discovery also depends on market depth—the availability of liquidity at multiple price levels—which is negatively affected by latency arbitrage[1][11][17][32]. When market makers must widen spreads to protect against picking off risk, they also typically reduce the depth of liquidity they provide, meaning fewer shares are available at each price level[1][11][17]. This reduced depth makes large orders more likely to move prices significantly, reducing market resilience and increasing execution costs for institutional investors[1][11][17]. The result is a counterintuitive outcome where faster markets may produce less efficient prices due to reduced liquidity depth, creating costs that outweigh the benefits of rapid adjustment to public signals[11][34][49].

## Market Manipulation Through Latency Exploitation and Sophisticated Strategies

The distinction between legitimate arbitrage and market manipulation has become increasingly blurred in an environment where speed advantages are exploited at nanosecond scales[3][13][16][26][38][48][51][54][57]. Market manipulation is typically defined as conduct designed to deceive investors by creating false or misleading impressions about supply and demand[57][60]. Traditional manipulation involves explicit deception through spoofing (placing fake orders intended to be cancelled before execution), layering (multiple non-bona fide orders designed to move prices), or quote stuffing (rapidly placing and canceling large volumes of orders to create confusion)[13][16][26][38][48].

However, sophisticated manipulation strategies have evolved that exploit matching engine design and latency properties in ways that are difficult to characterize as fraudulent. One emerging strategy involves precisely timing order placement and cancellation to influence short-term price movements in directions favorable to positions held elsewhere[3][13][16][38][48]. Another involves executing large volumes of trades at specific times designed to influence the settlement price calculations that determine option expiry values[3]. These strategies may not technically involve false information but instead manipulate prices through the timing and sequencing of real trades executed within the rules of the matching engine[3][16].

The Jane Street case, brought by India's Securities and Exchange Board (SEBI) in July 2025, exemplifies how sophisticated trading strategies operating within technical compliance can still be characterized as manipulative[3]. SEBI alleged that the U.S.-based proprietary trading firm manipulated Indian derivatives markets through coordinated trading in cash and futures markets designed to influence settlement prices[3]. The investigation identified that Jane Street executed large purchases of Bank Nifty constituent stocks and futures in the morning, accounting for 15-25 percent of total market volume in select stocks, allegedly raising the index price by 1-1.3 percent artificially[3]. Simultaneously, the firm maintained large short positions in Bank Nifty options, meaning it profited when the index price moved in the direction its cash market trading was pushing it[3].

The critical question in the Jane Street case involves distinguishing between legitimate index arbitrage—where a trader hedges a position in index futures by trading the underlying constituent stocks—and coordinated market manipulation[3][13][38]. Index arbitrage is essential for market efficiency, keeping futures and spot prices aligned[1][3][13]. However, the evidence SEBI presented suggested that Jane Street's trading was not delta-neutral hedging but rather deliberately unbalanced positioning designed to create market impact[3]. The firm earned net losses in cash markets while simultaneously earning outsized profits in options markets, a pattern suggesting the cash market trading was not economically justified on its own but instead served to move prices benefiting options positions[3].

SEBI's investigation also identified a pattern of "extended marking the close" where Jane Street allegedly executed trades in the final minutes of trading designed to influence the settlement price calculation used for option expiry[3]. Options settlement relies on official closing prices, meaning traders who can move closing prices can influence which options expire in or out of the money[3]. By executing large trades at the market close when liquidity is lowest and prices are most sensitive to marginal volumes, traders can move official settlement prices with fewer shares traded than would be required at other times of day[3][13][16][38].

The sophistication of these strategies lies in their exploitation of predictable matching engine behavior combined with market microstructure features. When a matching engine uses price-time priority and guarantees immediate execution, a trader placing a large market order knows that the order will be executed against the existing order book, moving prices to the point where the large order becomes entirely filled[1][4][13]. By timing such orders to coincide with low liquidity periods when even modest order sizes can move prices significantly, sophisticated traders can create temporary price movements that reverse after the targeted strategy achieves its goal[13][16][26][38].

Detecting such manipulation is extraordinarily difficult because the trades themselves are real and executed at prices within the normal bid-ask spread[3][13][57]. The proof of manipulation requires demonstrating that the trader intended to move prices for strategic benefit rather than responding to their own legitimate information advantage or hedging needs[3][13][57]. This distinction between intent and outcome is precisely where existing regulatory frameworks struggle, particularly when dealing with algorithms that lack clear evidence of human intent[57].

## Regulatory Responses: Speed Bumps, Batch Auctions, and Structural Reforms

Recognizing the challenges posed by latency-dependent arbitrage, regulators and exchanges have implemented various mechanisms designed to reduce the profitability of pure speed advantages. Speed bumps, which impose deliberate delays on order processing, represent the most direct approach[8][11]. The rationale is straightforward: if all orders experience an identical delay before execution, faster traders lose their speed advantage, and competition must then occur on price rather than speed[8][11]. Canadian exchanges implemented speed bumps adding a few milliseconds of random delay to non-cancellation orders, ostensibly to slow high-frequency traders[8][11].

However, research on speed bump effectiveness reveals a counterintuitive backfire effect[8][11]. In theoretical models with strategic traders who internalize how their speed decisions affect market spreads, speed bumps can actually increase equilibrium speeds and worsen adverse selection costs[8][11]. The mechanism works through strategic complementarity: when a speed bump increases the delay all traders experience, market makers become less concerned about being picked off by extremely fast traders since all traders face the same delay[8][11]. Consequently, market makers become less responsive to marginal increases in speed, and the endogenous cost of being faster decreases[8][11]. This reduced cost incentivizes individual traders to invest more in speed to maintain their relative advantage, defeating the purpose of the speed bump[8][11].

A more sophisticated approach is the asymmetric speed bump, which applies delays selectively to certain order types while exempting others[11]. Specifically, if cancellations are exempt from delays while new orders experience delays, then liquidity providers can quickly cancel their stale quotes before snipers can trade against them[11]. This design eliminates aggressive-side order anticipation by ensuring that any mispriced quotes are cancelled before they can be exploited[11]. Research supporting asymmetric speed bumps demonstrates that this surgical approach can reduce inefficient arbitrage while preserving the benefits of passive-side order anticipation, which involves market makers responding more quickly to information and tightening spreads[11].

Frequent batch auctions represent a more fundamental redesign of market structure, addressing latency arbitrage by moving from continuous-time to discrete-time trading[18][31][34][49][52]. In a frequent batch auction, all orders received during a fixed time interval (such as one-tenth of a second) are aggregated and executed simultaneously at a single clearing price, with participants unable to gain advantage through marginal speed improvements[18][31][34][49][52]. The theoretical benefits are compelling: by eliminating the value of small speed differences, batch auctions reduce the technological arms race incentive and force competition onto price dimensions rather than speed[18][31][34][49][52]. Batch auctions can match the same orders and prices as continuous trading when tested on historical data, but with dramatically reduced opportunities for latency arbitrage[31][34][49].

However, empirical analysis of market transitions reveals that batch auctions involve significant trade-offs. Research examining the Taiwan Stock Exchange's transition to continuous trading found that switching from discrete auctions to continuous trading dramatically improved liquidity, with order book depth more than doubling and spreads declining by amounts worth hundreds of millions to over a billion TWD in cost savings[34]. This suggests that while batch auctions eliminate latency arbitrage, they may reduce the total liquidity available to traders and increase trading costs in normal market conditions[34][49]. The fundamental tension is that continuous markets attract HFT liquidity provision even though some of that liquidity provision is predatory, whereas batch auctions deter all speed competition including the beneficial forms[34][49].

An alternative approach involves tightening market quality standards through reduced tick sizes and access fee caps. The SEC recently implemented rules reducing minimum tick sizes for approximately 74 percent of U.S. stocks to half-penny increments for stocks with spreads below 1.5 cents[27][30]. Smaller tick sizes allow more granular price competition, theoretically reducing the profitability of latency arbitrage by making it easier for multiple traders to compete at nearly identical prices[27][30]. However, the effectiveness of this approach depends on whether reduced tick sizes actually increase competition or merely reduce spreads while widening them relative to transaction costs.

Regulatory oversight of specific manipulative strategies has intensified across jurisdictions. The SEC, CFTC, FCA (UK), and ESMA (EU) have brought enforcement actions against firms engaged in spoofing, layering, and quote stuffing[6][13][26][38][48][57]. Notably, regulators have begun to bring broader market manipulation charges related to coordinated trading strategies that may not involve explicit false information but instead rely on timing and sequencing advantages[3][13][57]. The challenge remains that proving intent in algorithmic cases is extraordinarily difficult, and the existing legal frameworks emphasizing scienter (intentional or reckless conduct) may be mismatched to algorithmic trading where intent can be genuinely ambiguous[57].

## The Feasibility of Balancing Speed and Price Discovery: Structural Constraints and Contradictions

The fundamental challenge in balancing execution speed and price discovery derives from the fact that these objectives sometimes conflict rather than complement each other. Markets can be fast or efficient at price discovery, but achieving both simultaneously is technically constrained and may be economically impossible[11][18][31][34][49]. The source of this tension is that speed-dependent arbitrage—while economically wasteful—is extremely difficult to prevent without sacrificing legitimate speed that enables rapid price adjustment.

Consider the mechanics of genuine information-driven trading. When a significant news announcement occurs, informed traders want to execute trades before other market participants react and move prices against them[11][18][31]. The ability to trade quickly after observing information is essential for markets to function efficiently[11][18]. However, this same speed advantage also enables uninformed latency arbitrage, where traders without genuine information still profit from being marginally faster than other traders[11][18][31]. Any regulatory intervention that slows down all traders reduces latency arbitrage profitability but also reduces the speed at which informed traders can execute, delaying price discovery[11][18].

This creates what researchers term the "price-efficiency trade-off"[11][18][31][34]. In theoretical models, moderately faster speeds improve price efficiency up to a point, but beyond that point, additional speed improvements become socially wasteful because they merely transfer wealth from slower to faster traders without improving fundamental information content of prices[11][18][31][34]. The socially optimal amount of speed is less than the equilibrium amount that traders invest in, because each individual trader benefits from being faster than competitors even if total welfare would be higher with slightly less speed[11][18][31].

Matching engine design can reduce but not eliminate these tensions. By using price-time priority rather than pro-rata allocation, exchanges ensure that at any given price level, the earliest traders get filled first, reducing the pressure for microsecond-level speed improvements[1][4][56][59]. However, matching engines cannot eliminate the value of being fast when prices are changing, because in a continuous market, the best prices are constantly being updated as new information arrives[1][4][56].

Market fragmentation exacerbates these structural tensions. When the same security trades on multiple venues with different latencies to consolidated data feeds, latency arbitrage becomes both more profitable and more damaging to overall market efficiency[5][15][50][53]. An arbitrageur observing prices on multiple venues before the consolidated tape updates can guarantee profits, but this same activity degrades the quality of prices on the consolidated tape because trades execute with de facto stale prices[5][15][50][53]. Consolidating trading onto a single venue eliminates cross-venue latency arbitrage but creates concentration risks and may reduce liquidity by eliminating competition between venues[5][15][50][53].

The evidence from empirical research suggests that latency arbitrage does create room for manipulative activities, but distinguishing legitimate speed-based trading from manipulation requires nuanced analysis[3][13][26][38][48][51][57]. Not all latency-based profits represent market manipulation; some reflect genuine competitive advantages in information processing that improve price discovery[1][4][13]. However, strategies that are deliberately designed to exploit matching engine rules to move prices in specific directions without economic justification beyond those specific positions clearly cross into manipulative territory[3][13][16][38][48].

## Practical Feasibility Assessment: Trade-offs and Realistic Constraints

The practical feasibility of balancing execution speed and price discovery depends on accepting fundamental trade-offs rather than achieving perfect optimization. A realistic assessment reveals that matching engines can be designed to reduce but not eliminate opportunities for latency-based exploitation, and that regulatory frameworks must target the most egregiously manipulative applications while accepting some degree of speed-based arbitrage as unavoidable[8][11][27][34][49].

First, matching engines can be designed to minimize unnecessary speed sensitivity through several features. Using price-time priority instead of other matching algorithms reduces the value of microsecond-level speed improvements[1][4][56][59]. Ensuring that matching engines operate deterministically and that their rules are transparent prevents hidden sources of advantage[1][56]. Implementing order types specifically designed to provide protection against latency arbitrage, such as iceberg orders that gradually release volume or post-only orders that refuse to take existing liquidity, gives traders tools to manage speed risks[1][4].

Second, market structure design can reduce latency arbitrage opportunities through consolidation and synchronization. When markets are fragmented across multiple venues with different latencies, arbitrage opportunities proliferate[5][15][50]. Consolidating trading onto unified venues where all participants receive information simultaneously would eliminate cross-venue latency arbitrage[5][15][50]. However, this consolidation creates monopoly risks and may reduce beneficial competition between venues that currently drives innovation in order types and execution algorithms[5][15][50].

Third, technological accessibility can be equalized to reduce latency as a competitive advantage source. Currently, major financial firms spend hundreds of millions of dollars annually on infrastructure investments to achieve microsecond-level latency improvements[2][5][10]. If all market participants had equivalent access to co-location facilities, market data feeds, and computing power through standardized exchange services, speed advantages would diminish[2][5][10]. However, equalizing access would require regulatory mandates that exchanges provide infrastructure at regulated rates, potentially stifling innovation and increasing costs for all participants[2][5][10].

Fourth, regulatory frameworks can target specific manipulative strategies while preserving beneficial speed-based trading through harm-based liability standards. Rather than trying to prove scienter (intent) in complex algorithmic cases, regulators could hold traders liable for demonstrable harm to market quality regardless of intent[57]. Under a harm-based regime, strategies that consistently generate profits from moving prices in ways that increase volatility or reduce liquidity without proportional information gains could be restricted[57]. This approach would be more administratively feasible than proving intent but would require careful calibration to avoid catching beneficial arbitrage activity[57].

Fifth, monitoring and surveillance systems can be enhanced to detect manipulative patterns even at high-frequency timescales. Modern surveillance systems can now capture data at microsecond granularity and identify trading patterns indicative of coordination or price manipulation[26][51][54]. By analyzing order placement, execution, and cancellation patterns across multiple venues and time horizons, regulators can identify strategies that consistently exploit matching engine properties to move prices in predictable directions[26][51][54]. Machine learning models trained on known manipulative behaviors can flag suspicious activity for human review[26][51][54].

## Contemporary Market Evolution and Emerging Challenges

The market landscape has evolved significantly since the regulatory focus on high-frequency trading intensified following the 2010 Flash Crash. Payment for order flow (PFOF), where retail order flow is sold to wholesalers who internalize the orders off-exchange, has created new forms of potential exploitation alongside speed-based strategies[33][50][53]. Wholesalers pay more for retail order flow precisely because retail traders are less likely to be information-driven, meaning internalization profits come from spreads with uninformed traders rather than information advantages[33][50][53]. This segmentation of order flow by information content reduces the liquidity available to informed traders on exchanges, potentially degrading price discovery on lit markets[33][50][53].

Dark pools and other non-transparent trading venues have grown to account for approximately 40 percent of U.S. equity trading volume, fragmenting markets in ways that enable new forms of latency arbitrage[37][40][50][53]. Traders observing executions on dark pools before they are reported to consolidated tapes can front-run the price movements likely to result from these trades[37][40][50][53]. Regulatory responses have included increased transparency requirements for dark pools, but enforcement challenges remain given the volume of trading and complexity of detecting manipulation[37][40][50][53].

Artificial intelligence and machine learning have introduced new dimensions to the latency arbitrage debate[51][54]. Unlike preset algorithms with fixed decision rules, AI systems using reinforcement learning can adaptively optimize their trading strategies in ways that may inadvertently learn to manipulate markets[51][54]. A reinforcement learning algorithm incentivized to maximize trading profits could theoretically learn that moving prices in specific directions increases future profits, essentially discovering manipulative strategies through machine optimization[51][54]. Regulators have begun to warn about concentration risks from multiple firms using similar AI models trained on the same data, creating "monoculture" risks where many firms execute correlated strategies during stress periods[51][54].

## Conclusion: Synthesis of Evidence and Forward-Looking Recommendations

The evidence from matching engine design, empirical market studies, regulatory enforcement actions, and academic research supports a nuanced conclusion: matching engines can be designed to balance speed and efficiency, but the balance is inherently imperfect and trading-off-dependent rather than optimizable. The Jane Street case and other recent enforcement actions demonstrate that latency arbitrage and sophisticated exploitation of matching engine properties do create room for market manipulation, particularly when traders coordinate strategies across multiple venues to move prices in directions favoring their derivatives positions.

However, the feasibility of preventing latency-based exploitation without sacrificing beneficial speed-dependent trading is limited by structural constraints. In continuous markets, speed necessarily confers advantages in responding to information, making it impossible to eliminate speed-based profits without eliminating information-responsive trading entirely. Batch auctions can theoretically eliminate latency arbitrage but do so at the cost of reduced liquidity in normal market conditions. Speed bumps can reduce but not eliminate speed advantages and may backfire through strategic complementarities that increase equilibrium speed investment.

The most realistic and practically feasible approach involves targeted interventions addressing the most egregious manipulative strategies while accepting some degree of speed-based arbitrage as inevitable. This includes implementing asymmetric speed bumps that prevent aggressive-side order anticipation while preserving beneficial passive-side market-making speed competition, consolidating fragmented markets to reduce cross-venue arbitrage opportunities, and establishing harm-based liability standards that hold traders accountable for demonstrable market quality degradation. Additionally, regulatory frameworks must adapt more quickly to emerging technologies including AI and machine learning, establishing requirements for algorithmic governance, stress testing, and transparency that prevent unintended learning of manipulative strategies.

For market participants, the most prudent approach involves designing strategies that are robust to latency variations and that do not depend on maintaining speed advantages relative to competitors for profitability. For regulators, the priority must shift from attempting to eliminate latency advantages entirely toward ensuring that matching engines operate transparently and fairly according to well-defined rules that are consistently applied, and that strategies exploiting matching engine properties to move prices without economic justification are reliably detected and punished[3][6][26][51][54][57]. By accepting that perfect balance between speed and efficiency is unattainable while implementing targeted measures addressing the most damaging exploitation strategies, markets can achieve acceptable levels of both execution efficiency and price discovery integrity.
