## Network Flow Assignment Fundamentals and Problem Formulation

Network flow assignment represents a foundational problem in transportation modeling and network analysis, where the primary objective is to distribute travel demand across available routes in a network while accounting for congestion effects[1][31]. The classic traffic assignment problem assumes that travelers select routes to minimize their individual travel costs, creating what is known as user equilibrium[1]. At this equilibrium state, no traveler can unilaterally improve their travel time by switching to an alternative route, establishing a stable configuration of flows across the network[27]. The traditional formulation constrains origin-destination demands to fixed, exogenously determined values, meaning the total number of trips between any pair of locations is predetermined and independent of actual travel costs or network conditions[31].

The mathematical framework for network flow assignment relies on several key components that structure both the problem representation and solution methodology. Link travel costs, which form the basis for route selection decisions, depend upon the volume of traffic using each link through functional relationships called volume-delay functions[31]. These functions typically assume that as more vehicles use a link, travel time increases, creating congestion effects that discourage demand from concentrating on limited links. Path costs are calculated as the sum of link costs for all links comprising a particular route, reflecting the additive property of travel times across sequential network links[8]. The equilibrium conditions require that all utilized paths between an origin-destination pair must have equal travel cost, while any unused paths must have equal or greater cost, preventing travelers from improving their situations through route switching[27][31].

The fundamental objective of traffic assignment algorithms is to forecast link volumes accurately, recognizing that the primary purpose of such models is to determine how traffic flows through networks rather than to find theoretical optima[1]. Early approaches to traffic assignment relied on iterative capacity restraint methods, which applied all-or-nothing assignments repeatedly while updating travel times, establishing a conceptual foundation upon which modern algorithms build[1]. These iterative approaches consist of a series of all-or-nothing assignments where demand is loaded onto shortest paths, followed by travel time recalculation and subsequent iterations that progressively refine the solution[1]. The all-or-nothing assignment procedure, despite its simplicity, remains central to virtually all contemporary traffic assignment algorithms, serving as the core operation within more sophisticated iterative frameworks[31].

## Elastic Demand Formulation and Its Theoretical Foundation

Elastic demand represents a fundamental departure from the classical fixed-demand assumption by allowing origin-destination travel demands to vary endogenously based on travel costs and network conditions[19]. In elastic demand models, both the demand quantities and network flows become decision variables simultaneously, creating a coupled system where demand quantities adjust to achieve consistency with realized link travel costs[2][3]. This formulation recognizes that high travel costs discourage some trips from occurring entirely, while lower costs can induce additional demand, reflecting how real transportation systems respond to network changes[15][19]. The inverse demand functions specify the maximum acceptable travel cost (disutility) for different demand levels between origin-destination pairs, establishing the trade-off between accessibility and demand generation[19].

The theoretical formulation of elastic demand equilibrium requires both demand and flow consistency conditions to be satisfied simultaneously[27][39]. Demand consistency conditions ensure that the calculated demand between an origin-destination pair at current travel costs matches the demand that would actually materialize given those costs. Flow consistency conditions require that link flows properly distribute demand across paths and that path flows sum to total demand[27][39]. This dual consistency requirement distinguishes elastic demand problems from fixed-demand variants and introduces nonconvex optimization characteristics that complicate solution procedures[2][23]. The feasible set in elastic demand problems becomes significantly more complex, as it must accommodate both endogenous demand adjustments and corresponding flow changes across the entire network[25].

The mathematical formulation of elastic demand assignment typically employs an optimization framework where the objective function combines congestion costs and disutility functions[2][23]. The congestion function represents the total travel time cost incurred by all network users, calculated through integration of link costs over link flows[19]. The disutility function captures the cost of not traveling or choosing not to make a trip, quantifying the loss in consumer surplus when demand is suppressed below potential levels due to high travel costs[19]. By minimizing the sum of these two functions subject to network flow constraints, the optimization approach simultaneously determines equilibrium flows and demands that satisfy both consistency conditions[2][19][23]. This formulation elegantly transforms the elastic demand equilibrium problem into a convex optimization problem when demand and cost functions satisfy appropriate monotonicity conditions[27][39].

An alternative and computationally useful approach for solving elastic demand problems involves network transformation, particularly the Gartner transformation[19][60]. This method expands the network by adding artificial links that directly connect each origin to each destination, with cost functions derived from the inverse demand functions[19][60]. The artificial links carry "unmet" demand—the portion of potential demand that does not travel through the real network due to high costs[19][60]. When a fixed-demand assignment is executed on this transformed network with total demand set to the sum of actual and unmet demand, the solution to the expanded network problem corresponds exactly to the elastic demand equilibrium on the original network[19][60]. This transformation elegantly reduces elastic demand problems to fixed-demand equivalents, allowing practitioners to apply established fixed-demand algorithms without requiring new computational procedures[19][38].

## Convergence Properties of Fixed-Demand Assignment Algorithms

The Frank-Wolfe algorithm, developed from general optimization methods adapted for traffic assignment, represents one of the most important and influential approaches for solving fixed-demand traffic assignment problems[1][31]. The algorithm operates through an iterative procedure where each iteration consists of three steps: first, calculate current link travel costs using the method of successive averages to blend new and previous costs; second, execute an all-or-nothing assignment using these costs to determine auxiliary flows on shortest paths; and third, update current link flows by moving a fraction toward the auxiliary flows[17]. The convergence rate of Frank-Wolfe for fixed-demand problems is known to be \(O(1/k)\) where \(k\) is the iteration number, meaning that achieving acceptable convergence typically requires multiple dozens of iterations[17][31]. This relatively slow convergence rate reflects the fundamental trade-off in conditional gradient methods between simplicity and speed[17].

The method of successive averages (MSA) emerged as a popular alternative to Frank-Wolfe decomposition, particularly because it can handle more complex relationships between speed and volume and can accommodate combined distribution-assignment problems[31]. MSA operates similarly to Frank-Wolfe but uses a simple averaging scheme where flows are updated as the arithmetic mean of all flows encountered across iterations, with the step size diminishing according to \(1/k\)[31]. While MSA exhibits slower convergence than Frank-Wolfe on elementary traffic assignments, it provides greater flexibility for handling more complex problem formulations and demand relationships[31]. The computational advantage of MSA lies in its ability to avoid explicit optimization of a master problem, instead relying on simple averaging that remains computationally efficient even on very large networks[31].

Evans' algorithm represents a theoretically rigorous approach to elastic-demand equilibrium assignment that correctly solves the mathematical formulation of the elastic demand problem[1][14][33]. The algorithm combines a variable-demand trip table recalculation step with an all-or-nothing assignment and an averaging step that uses a specialized objective function[1]. However, the computational burden of Evans' algorithm significantly exceeds that of simpler approaches because the objective function of its averaging step requires substantially more computation and memory than regular equilibrium assignment, particularly on large multipurpose networks[1]. Despite these computational demands, Evans' algorithm demonstrates superior convergence robustness compared to Frank-Wolfe methods, as its convergence does not depend critically on artificial tuning parameters[14][33][36]. Comparative studies show that Evans' algorithm converges consistently faster than Frank-Wolfe approaches for elastic assignment problems, with convergence rates that are less sensitive to the specific parameters and network structure[14][33][36].

Incremental assignment methods provide an important computational alternative, particularly for practical applications where computational resources are limited[1][31]. These methods divide total demand into incremental portions, typically using fractions like fifty percent, then twenty-five percent, then ten percent, and assign each portion sequentially while recalculating travel times between assignments[31]. The major benefit of incremental assignment lies in model runtime, as flows typically converge to acceptable accuracy levels within five to ten iterations, far fewer than the dozens required by equilibrium methods[31]. However, incremental assignment shows limitations in heavily congested networks where even the initial fifty percent of demand can generate significant congestion, causing the algorithm to miss subsequent demand patterns that would occur given true congestion levels[31]. Research comparing incremental methods to equilibrium methods found that elastic-demand incremental assignment produces root-mean-square differences in link volumes of approximately 0.8 percent compared to equilibrium solutions, demonstrating reasonable accuracy despite its computational simplicity[1].

The introduction of elastic demand fundamentally alters the convergence characteristics of traffic assignment algorithms, creating new challenges that differ qualitatively from fixed-demand problems[14][33][36]. The Frank-Wolfe algorithm demonstrates notable instability when applied to elastic demand problems, with convergence rates becoming highly sensitive to values selected for upper bounds on trips, which are artificial tuning parameters with no direct physical interpretation[14][33][36]. The theoretical convergence properties of Frank-Wolfe, which appear robust in fixed-demand applications, become unreliable and parameter-dependent when handling elastic demand[14][33][36].

Evans' algorithm demonstrates substantially more robust convergence behavior under elastic demand conditions, achieving consistent convergence across different network configurations and parameter specifications[14][33][36]. The convergence robustness of Evans' algorithm reflects its theoretical grounding in the mathematical formulation of elastic demand equilibrium, which specifies both demand and flow consistency conditions simultaneously[14][33][36]. Rather than relying on artificial parameters, Evans' algorithm directly enforces consistency between travel costs and demand quantities, creating a solution procedure that responds appropriately to the problem's intrinsic structure[1][14]. Computational results comparing these approaches show that Evans' algorithm converges with greater consistency than Frank-Wolfe for both elastic demand assignment and mode split-assignment problems[14][33][36]. The computational cost of this improved convergence robustness lies in the larger computational and memory requirements for each iteration, creating a trade-off between convergence reliability and per-iteration computational expense[1][14].

Elastic-demand incremental assignment, contrary to expectations, demonstrates convergence characteristics comparable to or superior to equilibrium methods on many network configurations[1]. This unexpected result emerged from empirical testing showing that incremental assignment performs well even on difficult networks where equilibrium methods converge slowly[1]. The elastic-demand incremental approach, which recalculates the trip table and loads a portion of it at each iteration, exhibits both faster runtime per iteration and comparable overall convergence when measuring number of iterations to reach specified accuracy thresholds[1]. On the UTOWN network, elastic-demand incremental assignment achieved RMS differences in link volumes of only 0.8 percent compared to equilibrium solutions, while requiring considerably less computational time per iteration than Evans' algorithm[1]. The convergence behavior of elastic demand algorithms shows strong dependence on network structure and topology[1]. ## Optimization Formulation Approaches and Solution Methods

Variational inequality formulations provide a powerful mathematical framework for expressing traffic assignment equilibrium conditions for both fixed and elastic demand problems[27][39]. The fixed-demand user equilibrium problem specifies that link cost multiplied by the difference between flows and current flows must be non-negative, expressed as the variational inequality \(c(f^*) \cdot (f - f^*) \geq 0\)[27]. This formulation elegantly captures the equilibrium condition that all utilized paths have equal minimum cost while all non-utilized paths have equal or greater cost[27]. For elastic demand problems, variational inequality formulation extends to include both flow variables and demand variables, with the comprehensive inequality specifying that both cost and demand conditions must be simultaneously satisfied[27][39]. This enlarged formulation captures the coupled consistency conditions required for elastic demand equilibrium[27][39].

The convex optimization approach formulates traffic assignment as the minimization of an objective function combining link cost integrals and demand disutility integrals subject to flow conservation constraints[2][19][23]. The objective function measures total travel time cost plus the total disutility of demand suppression, with the optimization solution yielding flows and demands that simultaneously minimize this combined cost while satisfying network constraints[2][19][23]. The convexity of this optimization problem, when demand and cost functions satisfy appropriate monotonicity conditions, ensures uniqueness of the solution and enables the application of gradient-based optimization methods[2][19][23]. Computing the objective function's gradient provides descent direction information that optimization algorithms use to iteratively approach the optimum[2][19][23].

The cutting plane method, specifically the analytic center cutting plane method (ACCPM), provides an alternative optimization approach particularly suited to elastic demand problems with different congestion and demand function specifications[2][20][23]. ACCPM generates cutting planes that progressively refine an outer approximation of the feasible region, while the analytic center within the current feasible region serves as the next iterate[2][20][23]. Numerical experiments testing ACCPM on elastic demand problems with Bureau of Public Roads congestion functions and both constant elasticity and linear demand functions demonstrated the method's ability to solve large instances with high accuracy[2][20][23]. The computational efficiency of ACCPM for elastic demand problems suggests that specialized cutting plane approaches may provide advantages over general-purpose methods for problems with specific structure[2][20][23].

Generalized Benders decomposition combines a fixed-demand assignment method with decomposition strategies to solve elastic demand problems[3][22]. This approach decomposes the elastic demand problem into a master problem that determines demand quantities and subproblems that solve fixed-demand assignments for given demands[3][22]. Iterative coordination between master and subproblems progressively refines both demand quantities and flow patterns until convergence[3][22]. The advantage of decomposition approaches lies in their ability to leverage existing fixed-demand algorithms, avoiding the need to develop entirely new solution procedures[3][22]. However, decomposition can introduce additional overhead from the need to solve multiple subproblems and maintain convergence of the coordinating master problem[3][22].

Multimodal network equilibrium extensions address problems where multiple transportation modes share network infrastructure and compete for demand[6][21][32]. These extensions allow link costs to depend on flows of all modes, not merely total flow, and permit demand between origin-destination pairs to vary by mode and to depend on costs of competing modes[6][21][32]. These expanded problem formulations will create even greater computational complexity, motivating continued algorithmic research and development[8][25].

The fundamental insight emerging from this analysis is that network flow assignment convergence properties under elastic demand cannot be understood through simplistic metrics focusing solely on iteration counts or per-iteration computation. Rather, comprehensive complexity assessment must consider multiple facets including convergence rate, memory requirements, robustness to data variations, sensitivity to algorithmic parameters, solution quality, and practical implementability[1][8][14][31]. The superior balance achieved by elastic-demand incremental assignment across these dimensions explains its emergence as a preferred practical approach, even as other algorithms provide superior performance on specific dimensions[1]. This multidimensional trade-off characterization provides essential guidance for algorithm selection in real-world applications where no single algorithm dominates all criteria[1][8][14].
