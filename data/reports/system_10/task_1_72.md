# The Critical Link Between Scenario Design and Capital Adequacy Calibration in Environmental Risk Stress Testing

Environmental risk stress testing has emerged as a fundamental tool for assessing the resilience of financial institutions to climate-related shocks, yet the design of scenarios used in these exercises profoundly influences the capital requirements that institutions must maintain. The relationship between scenario construction and capital adequacy calibration represents one of the most consequential technical challenges in modern prudential supervision, as the assumptions, pathways, and parameters embedded in environmental scenarios directly determine the projected losses that drive regulatory capital buffers. This report examines how specific design choices in scenario development—including emissions trajectories, time horizons, physical risk modeling, transition risk assumptions, and transmission channel specification—cascade through stress testing methodologies to affect the calibration of capital adequacy frameworks, with implications for both microprudential bank soundness and macroprudential financial stability.

## The Foundational Framework: Scenario Design and Its Regulatory Mandate

Scenario design in environmental risk stress testing serves as the critical bridge between climate science and financial risk quantification, establishing the plausible futures against which institutions test their resilience. The European Banking Authority's Guidelines on Environmental Scenario Analysis explicitly mandate that institutions must define baseline and adverse scenarios that incorporate environmental risk drivers, particularly climate-related factors, and identify the transmission channels through which these drivers could impact financial metrics and indicators[1]. This requirement fundamentally shapes how capital adequacy must be assessed, because the scenarios themselves determine which risks are tested and therefore which risks institutions must hold capital against. Unlike traditional stress testing, which typically relies on historical precedents and statistical relationships, environmental scenario analysis must grapple with the unprecedented nature of global warming and the structural shifts in economic activity that transition pathways will produce[17].

The regulatory mandate for scenario design reflects a recognition that historical data provides limited predictive capacity for the future losses that may arise from climate-related risk drivers, as climate risks are characterized by long time horizons, high uncertainty, and non-linear dynamics[17]. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision has established principles requiring that scenario assumptions be clearly grounded in relevant scientific, technological, and economic literature, with particular emphasis on ensuring internal consistency within scenarios across assumptions about technological change, demographics, climate impacts, and macroeconomic factors[17]. This coherence requirement means that scenario design cannot be conducted in isolation; rather, it must reflect a comprehensive narrative about how the economy, technology, policy, and climate systems will interact over specified periods. The tightness of this coherence directly influences capital adequacy calibration, because inconsistencies or implausibilities in scenarios can either overstate or understate the true magnitude of potential losses.

The European Central Bank has operationalized this principle through its guidance on climate-related and environmental risks, establishing supervisory expectations that institutions with material climate exposures incorporate climate risks into their capital planning processes, including their Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP)[19]. This integration requirement means that scenario design must be sufficiently robust to support not only regulatory stress tests but also institutions' own forward-looking assessments of capital needs, with the results informing binding capital requirements through the Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)[25][28]. The stakes of scenario design are therefore extraordinarily high; poor scenario design can lead to either inadequate capital buffers that leave institutions vulnerable to climate-related losses, or excessive capital requirements that constrain lending and economic activity without corresponding risk reduction.

## Scenario Construction: The Architecture of Environmental Risk Drivers and Transmission Channels

The design of scenarios for environmental risk stress testing requires institutions and supervisors to make a series of foundational choices about which climate risk drivers to model, how to represent the evolution of these drivers over time, and which transmission channels to include in the analysis. The NGFS (Network for Greening the Financial System) scenarios, which have become the most widely adopted reference framework globally, distinguish between transition risks and physical risks, with transition risk pathways based on different levels of policy ambition and physical risk pathways derived from climate science models[14][37]. The Network for Greening the Financial System has developed scenarios including Net Zero 2050, Delayed Transition, Disorderly Transition, Divergent Net Zero, and Current Policies pathways, each reflecting different assumptions about the speed and nature of the transition to a low-carbon economy[19].

The choice between these scenarios has direct implications for capital adequacy calibration. A Net Zero 2050 scenario, which assumes stringent climate policies and rapid innovation enabling smooth transition, produces relatively subdued transition risks in the short term but potentially significant physical risks if policies fail; by contrast, a Delayed Transition scenario assumes that meaningful policy action is postponed, resulting in lower near-term transition costs but much higher long-term physical and transition risks when action eventually becomes unavoidable[19]. When institutions run stress tests under these different scenarios, they project divergent paths for probability of default (PD), loss given default (LGD), and exposure at default (EAD) for their credit portfolios. These projections then become inputs to capital adequacy frameworks; institutions must hold capital sufficient to absorb projected losses across the scenarios tested, and supervisors use the results to calibrate Pillar 2 requirements that supplement the standardized Pillar 1 framework[28].

The EBA Guidelines on Environmental Scenario Analysis emphasize that institutions should define both baseline scenarios (representing the most likely outcome according to the institution's view) and adverse scenarios (representing severe but plausible alternatives)[1][9]. This dual-scenario approach creates a fundamental tension in scenario design, because the baseline scenario must incorporate environmental factors given that climate change is already occurring; in consequence, the baseline itself embeds climate stress, and the adverse scenario must represent even more severe conditions[17]. This structural feature of climate scenario design differs markedly from traditional stress testing, where the baseline typically represents a benign environment and adverse scenarios introduce stress. The implications for capital adequacy are significant: if the baseline scenario already incorporates substantial climate impacts, the distance between baseline and adverse scenarios becomes compressed, potentially understating tail risks if extreme but plausible scenarios are not adequately captured.

Transmission channels represent the causal pathways through which climate risk drivers materialize as financial risks to banks[8][32]. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision has identified microeconomic and macroeconomic transmission channels, where microeconomic channels affect individual counterparties and their financial assets (through credit risk, market risk, liquidity risk, and operational risk effects), while macroeconomic channels affect the broader economy and financial system through effects on productivity, growth, employment, and fiscal sustainability[32]. The completeness with which transmission channels are specified in scenario design directly determines whether stress test results capture the true magnitude of climate-related losses. If a scenario includes rising carbon prices (a transition risk driver) but fails to model the effects of higher energy costs on supply chains, firm profitability, and ultimately household consumption and employment, the projected losses from credit risk deterioration will be understated. Institutions must therefore carefully consider which transmission channels are material to their specific portfolios and business models, and supervisors must ensure that the scenarios provided to institutions include sufficient granularity to support credible impact assessment.

The EBA Guidelines specify that institutions should identify and model the most relevant transmission channels as a foundation for conducting scenario analysis, noting that as institutions develop greater sophistication, they may initially rely heavily on qualitative approaches and expert judgment before progressing to quantitative modeling[1][9]. This proportionality approach recognizes that data limitations and methodological immaturity constrain the ability of institutions to precisely quantify all transmission channels, yet it creates a potential risk: institutions that rely on qualitative assessment may underestimate climate impacts, and supervisors must have sufficient expertise to identify when qualitative scenarios inadequately capture material risks. The calibration of capital requirements based on scenarios where key transmission channels are only qualitatively assessed introduces measurement uncertainty that itself represents a form of capital adequacy risk.

## The Mechanics of Impact: How Scenario Design Determines Credit Risk Parameters

The scenario design choices made in environmental risk stress testing ultimately affect capital adequacy through their influence on the traditional credit risk parameters—probability of default (PD), loss given default (LGD), and exposure at default (EAD)—that form the foundation of credit risk capital requirements under both standardized and internal ratings-based approaches. These parameters determine expected credit loss (ECL) according to the formula ECL = PD × LGD × EAD, and under the Basel Framework, capital requirements are calibrated based on projected losses under stress scenarios[20][46]. The scenario design therefore functions as the initial condition that determines how these parameters will evolve under different environmental futures.

Probability of default is affected by transition risks through multiple pathways. Rising carbon prices assumed in transition scenarios increase operating costs for carbon-intensive firms, reducing profitability and cash flow available for debt service, thereby increasing the likelihood of default[20]. Policy scenarios that mandate rapid decarbonization can force stranded asset recognition, where assets become uneconomical and lose value, effectively wiping out equity buffers that protect creditors. The scenario design choice about the timing and stringency of carbon price trajectories therefore directly affects projected PD values. If a scenario assumes carbon prices rise gradually to €100 per ton by 2035, firms will have time to adjust production and invest in low-carbon alternatives, resulting in lower PD increases than a scenario assuming sudden €200 per ton carbon prices in 2025[12]. When stress tests project PD under these different scenarios, they produce different estimates of credit losses, which then inform how much capital institutions must hold.

Physical risks affect PD through damage to productive assets and business disruption. A scenario incorporating acute physical risks must specify not only which regions face increased flood, wildfire, or extreme temperature risks, but also the timing, severity, and cascading consequences of these events. The challenge of scenario design here is substantial, because physical risks are highly localized, and damage from individual events depends on factors beyond climate parameters—including adaptation measures already in place, property insurance coverage, and business continuity capabilities[55]. The EBA Guidelines note that while methodologies to identify and assess environmental risks are not yet sufficiently developed to serve as a basis for robust scenario analyses, institutions must begin resilience testing with climate-related factors[1][9]. This gap between desired analytical rigor and current capacity has direct implications for capital adequacy calibration; supervisors must decide whether to defer capital requirements for physical risks until methodologies mature, or to impose precautionary capital requirements based on qualitative assessment of potential vulnerabilities.

Loss given default is affected by scenario design choices about collateral values and recovery prospects. Physical risks can reduce collateral values; a scenario assuming significant flooding in coastal regions implies that real estate collateral in those regions will be less valuable, increasing LGD for mortgages and commercial real estate loans[21][43]. Transition risks can similarly affect collateral recovery, as the assets securing loans may become economically obsolete. For example, a scenario assuming rapid phase-out of internal combustion engine vehicles would reduce the recovery value of collateral consisting of auto dealer inventory or fleet leasing businesses. The scenario must specify not only the climate outcome but also how asset values will be impacted, and institutions must project LGD based on these assumptions. The design choice about whether to assume static portfolio positions or allow dynamic adjustment of portfolios over the scenario horizon significantly affects LGD projections; if institutions are assumed to manage exposures over time, recovery prospects improve, but supervisors may be skeptical of this assumption on the grounds that all institutions managing exposures simultaneously could create fire-sale dynamics that depress asset prices further.

Exposure at default reflects the amount of credit outstanding when default occurs. Under traditional stress testing with short time horizons, EAD is typically assumed to be fixed at the current level, on the assumption that there is no time for the borrower's behavior to change substantially[43]. However, environmental scenarios with long time horizons (typically 10-30 years) create a fundamental challenge: will institutions continue to extend credit to carbon-intensive firms throughout the scenario period, or will they reduce exposures as climate risks materialize? The scenario design choice about whether to assume a static balance sheet (where current exposures remain unchanged through the entire scenario) or a dynamic balance sheet (where institutions adjust exposures based on economic conditions and risk management) profoundly affects projected losses[19][44]. A static balance sheet approach may overstate losses because it assumes institutions hold exposures that they would actually divest, but a fully dynamic approach may understate losses because it assumes institutions can exit risky exposures before losses materialize[43].

The Finance Watch organization has advocated for a static balance sheet approach as mandatory for climate stress testing, arguing that dynamic balance sheet assumptions obscure the true fragility of institutions by assuming they can manage exposures proactively[12][44]. By contrast, some financial institutions and supervisors view a hybrid approach as more realistic, allowing dynamic adjustment over longer time horizons while maintaining static balance sheet assumptions for near-term horizons[51]. This scenario design choice has direct capital adequacy implications; a static balance sheet produces higher projected losses and therefore higher capital requirements, while a dynamic balance sheet produces lower projected losses and lower capital requirements. The Federal Reserve has noted that the design choices underpinning models and scenarios ultimately drive the cost of loans and financing in the economy, making it essential that scenario design choices be transparent and well-justified[5].

## Time Horizons and the Temporal Dimension of Capital Adequacy Calibration

The choice of time horizons in environmental scenario design fundamentally shapes capital adequacy calibration by determining when climate-related losses are expected to materialize and therefore whether current capital adequacy frameworks, which typically focus on one-year loss horizons, are appropriate for capturing climate risks. The European Banking Authority Guidelines distinguish between short-term stress testing (less than five years) to assess financial resilience to environmental shocks, and longer-term climate resilience analysis (10+ years) to challenge the business model[1][9]. This temporal bifurcation creates complications for capital adequacy calibration, because it suggests that different time horizons may be appropriate for different dimensions of risk, but the regulatory capital frameworks are typically calibrated based on losses over a single horizon.

The Federal Reserve conducts annual stress tests under the Dodd-Frank Act using a nine-quarter projection horizon, substantially longer than the one-year horizon that characterizes much of the Basel capital framework[2]. This extended horizon allows capture of macroeconomic feedback effects and dynamic portfolio adjustments over a medium-term timeframe. However, climate risks with their characteristic long time horizons present a conceptual challenge: should capital requirements be calibrated based on potential losses over nine quarters (approximately two years), or should they incorporate anticipated losses over 30 or 50 years? If supervisors calibrate capital based only on near-term losses, they may fail to capture the cumulative effects of climate risks that compound over decades. Conversely, if capital requirements are calibrated based on extremely long-term scenarios, institutions may be forced to hold excessive capital relative to risks that may never materialize due to technological or policy changes.

The Bank for International Settlements has recommended that scenario assumptions be clearly grounded in relevant literature and that modelling assumptions consider how historical relationships may plausibly shift to reflect structural shifts in economic and climate conditions[17]. This principle has direct implications for time horizon selection, as the relevant time horizon depends on the question being asked. For assessing whether institutions can absorb near-term losses from extreme weather events or sudden policy shocks, shorter horizons (3-5 years) are appropriate. For assessing the sustainability of business models in a transition to net-zero emissions, longer horizons (20-30 years) are necessary. The International Monetary Fund's recent analysis of macroprudential capital buffers notes that scenario design must address when and to what levels buffers should be built, when they should be released, and how releases should be communicated[18]. This framework suggests that capital adequacy calibration may need to incorporate multiple time horizons, with short-term buffers addressing near-term risks and longer-term buffers addressing structural business model risks.

The NGFS scenario framework specifies climate outcomes at multiple time points (2030, 2040, 2050), enabling analysis of how risks evolve over time[37][58]. Institutions can therefore run scenario analysis that projects how PD, LGD, and other risk parameters will evolve over decades, generating loss distributions at multiple time horizons. Supervisors can then calibrate capital requirements at the time horizon most relevant to their mandate; European authorities focused on medium-term financial stability might calibrate based on losses through 2035, while central banks concerned with long-term systemic resilience might calibrate based on 2050 outcomes. However, this flexibility in time horizon creates potential for inconsistency; if different supervisors calibrate capital requirements based on different time horizons using the same scenarios, banks operating in multiple jurisdictions will face divergent capital requirements that may not be coherent with underlying climate risks.

The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) in Canada has conducted scenario analysis examining exposures over multiple time horizons simultaneously, recognizing that climate risks manifest differently in the short, medium, and long term[55]. Their findings highlight a structural challenge: standardized scenarios, while useful for comparability and transparency, can obscure portfolio-specific vulnerabilities that may be more acute in near-term or medium-term horizons. This observation suggests that capital adequacy calibration based solely on standardized scenarios may either overstate or understate risks for individual institutions, depending on their specific exposure profiles and time horizons most material to their business models.

## Baseline and Adverse Scenario Design: Implications for Capital Buffer Calibration

The design of baseline and adverse scenarios directly determines the magnitude of capital buffers that supervisors require institutions to maintain, as Pillar 2 guidance and Pillar 2 requirements are typically set based on how institution capital ratios decline under adverse stress scenarios relative to the baseline. The European Central Bank's approach to setting Pillar 2 guidance involves placing banks into buckets based on how much their capital ratios decline in stress tests, with larger declines corresponding to higher Pillar 2 guidance levels[25]. If environmental scenario design leads to larger assumed declines in capital ratios under adverse scenarios, institutions will be placed into higher-depletion buckets and assigned higher Pillar 2 guidance requirements.

The conceptual challenge in baseline scenario design for climate risks is that a truly realistic baseline that incorporates current policy trajectories will itself embed substantial climate impacts. Scientists expect approximately 3.2°C of warming by 2100 under current policy pathways, meaning that the baseline scenario, if accurate, must already incorporate significant physical risks from extreme weather, sea level rise, and ecosystem disruption[12]. This structural feature—that the baseline is not benign but already stressed—fundamentally changes the interpretation of stress test results. Traditional stress tests compare severely adverse scenarios to a benign baseline, creating large gaps in projected losses that justify substantial capital buffers. Climate stress tests, by contrast, compare scenarios ranging from orderly transitions to disorderly transitions, with the baseline already incorporating substantial climate impacts. The gaps between baseline and adverse scenarios may be smaller than in traditional stress testing, potentially suggesting that climate-related capital buffers should be smaller than those for other risks. However, the absolute level of losses in the baseline itself may be higher than under traditional stress testing scenarios that exclude climate impacts, suggesting that total capital requirements may need to increase to account for baseline climate impacts.

The European Central Bank's 2022 climate risk stress test divided scenarios into short-term (three-year) and long-term (through 2080) horizons, with the long-term scenarios including Net Zero 2050, Current Policies, and Disorderly Transition pathways[19]. In the Net Zero 2050 scenario, both physical and transition risks are relatively subdued due to smooth, gradual transition. In the Current Policies scenario, transition risks are negligible (because no major transition occurs), but physical risks escalate substantially due to unmitigated climate change. In the Disorderly Transition scenario, both physical and transition risks are severe. The choice of which scenario to treat as the baseline and which as adverse is not obvious; the ECB effectively used Current Policies as the baseline and Disorderly Transition as the adverse scenario, implying that institutions must hold capital to absorb losses in the Disorderly Transition scenario relative to unmitigated climate change in the baseline. This design choice means that capital buffers are calibrated to protect against disorderly transition risk above and beyond the physical risks that would occur if no decarbonization happened. An alternative scenario design might treat Net Zero 2050 as the adverse scenario most material to financial stability, on the grounds that failure to achieve net zero commitments represents the greatest tail risk. Such a design choice would lead to different capital adequacy requirements.

The calibration of Pillar 2 requirements based on stress test results uses a bucketing methodology where banks are placed into buckets based on their capital depletion in the stress test, and each bucket has a corresponding Pillar 2 requirement range[25][41]. The EBA has indicated that it will develop guidelines requiring institutions to conduct stress testing every two years as a minimum standard, with banks expected to build upon these standards according to their own specifications and needs[4]. This approach means that scenario design decisions made at the supervisory level will create a floor below which institutions' own scenario analysis cannot go. If the EBA's scenarios are designed with conservative assumptions about transition speed or physical risk magnitude, all institutions will face capital requirements calibrated to these conservative assumptions. If the scenarios are designed with more optimistic assumptions, capital requirements will be correspondingly lower. The implications are substantial: a scenario design choice that reduces assumed carbon prices by 50% across a transition scenario would directly reduce projected corporate credit losses and potentially lower Pillar 2 requirements for banks with significant exposures to carbon-intensive sectors.

## The Role of Risk Transmission Channels in Capital Parameter Specification

The completeness and accuracy with which scenario design specifies transmission channels between climate drivers and financial outcomes determines whether projected credit losses truly reflect the magnitude of climate-related risks facing institutions. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision has identified credit risk, market risk, liquidity risk, and operational risk as channels through which climate risks materialize, with each channel requiring different modeling approaches and data inputs[32]. Scenario design must translate climate drivers (such as carbon prices, temperature increases, or policy changes) into paths for variables affecting each risk type; for credit risk, this might involve projecting how higher energy costs affect firm profitability, how physical damage affects collateral values, or how regulatory compliance costs affect industry viability.

The specification of transmission channels in scenario design directly affects which risk parameters need to be stressed and to what magnitude. For example, if a transition risk scenario assumes gradual carbon price increases, the transmission channel through operating cost increases to firm profitability is relatively straightforward; institutions can use historical energy cost elasticity estimates to project how firm revenues and profitability will decline. However, if the scenario assumes policy shocks (such as sudden bans on fossil fuel production), the transmission channels become more complex, as firms may lose access to key inputs, face stranded asset write-downs, or experience demand destruction for their products. The more complex the transmission channels, the more difficult it is to model impacts quantitatively, and institutions may need to resort to qualitative assessment or range estimates rather than point estimates. This uncertainty in transmission channel specification creates corresponding uncertainty in capital adequacy requirements; supervisors must decide whether to add conservative adjustments to account for transmission channel uncertainty, or whether to rely on institutions' best estimates of impacts.

The EBA Guidelines emphasize that institutions should identify data, methodology, and assumption limitations when specifying transmission channels and that supervisors should require banks to make qualitative adjustments to compensate for scenario limitations[12][44]. Finance Watch has recommended that institutions incorporate a compensatory stress percentage into their economic stress factors to improve adverse scenarios when quantitative modeling is unable to capture all relevant transmission channels[12]. This recommendation essentially suggests that capital adequacy calibration should include an explicit precautionary markup on top of projected losses from quantitatively modeled transmission channels, to account for transmission channels that are known to exist but are difficult to model precisely. The magnitude of this precautionary markup is itself a critical design choice; if it is too small, it fails to capture material risks; if it is too large, it imposes unnecessary capital costs that constrain credit supply without corresponding risk reduction.

Physical risk transmission channels present particular challenges for scenario design and capital calibration. The direct effect of flooding or wildfire damage on collateral values is relatively straightforward to model if geospatial risk data is available, but the indirect macroeconomic effects are complex and context-dependent. Flooding in a major agricultural region could disrupt supply chains, reduce crop yields, affect food prices and employment across the economy, and ultimately reduce the ability of households and firms throughout the country to service debts. However, the magnitude of these second-round effects depends on adaptation capacity, insurance availability, supply chain diversification, and numerous other factors that vary geographically and temporally. The OSFI's standardized climate scenario exercise found that institutions have made limited progress in geolocating exposures and assessing property-level physical risk, constraining their ability to integrate location-specific climate impacts into credit risk assessment[55]. This capability gap means that scenario design for physical risk must often rely on simplified geographic aggregation rather than granular property-level analysis, potentially understating concentration risk if physical hazards affect multiple exposures in the same location simultaneously.

## Capital Adequacy Frameworks: Pillar 1, Pillar 2, and Scenario-Based Calibration

The Basel Framework distinguishes between Pillar 1 minimum capital requirements (which apply standardized risk weights to asset classes) and Pillar 2 requirements (which address risks not fully captured by Pillar 1, including those identified through stress testing). The integration of environmental scenario analysis into capital adequacy frameworks raises the question of whether climate risks should be addressed through adjustments to Pillar 1 parameters (such as risk weights for climate-sensitive assets) or through Pillar 2 requirements (through supervisory judgement based on stress test results and institution-specific factors). This choice has significant implications for capital calibration consistency and transparency.

The ECB has indicated that it expects banks to incorporate climate-related and environmental risks into their internal capital adequacy assessment process (ICAAP), with the results informing supervisory assessments under the Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)[7]. The ECB's approach has been to use climate stress test results informally in SREP assessments, setting higher Pillar 2 requirements for banks that demonstrate inadequate climate risk management or high exposures to climate-sensitive sectors, rather than formally adjusting Pillar 1 risk weights to reflect climate risks. This approach provides flexibility but creates potential for inconsistency; banks with similar climate risk exposures may receive different Pillar 2 requirements depending on supervisory judgment, time period of assessment, and the specific scenarios used in stress testing.

An alternative approach involves climate-adjusted capital requirements (CACRs), which would explicitly adjust Pillar 1 risk weights for assets subject to climate risks. The proposal would require banks to fund loans to carbon-intensive firms with higher percentages of shareholder capital, making such lending more expensive and creating incentives for banks to reduce exposure to climate-risky activities[3][13][16]. The implementation of CACRs requires supervisors to define which assets are subject to climate risks and to calibrate appropriate risk weight adjustments. Climate risks are not uniformly distributed across asset classes; coal mining, petroleum refining, and fossil fuel power generation face substantial transition risks, while renewable energy and energy efficiency retrofitting face lower transition risks[11]. However, the calibration of risk weight adjustments requires supervisors to make assumptions about future carbon prices, technology costs, policy pathways, and firm-level adaptation capacity. If these assumptions are embedded in scenario design, then the scenario design directly determines the risk weight adjustments and therefore the Pillar 1 capital requirements.

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision has recommended that banks take a conservative approach to assigning risk weights when they lack sufficient information to fully know borrowers' climate risks[3][16]. This recommendation, while prudent, essentially delegates scenario design to individual banks; each bank decides what scenario assumptions to use in assessing climate risks, and adjusts risk weights accordingly. The result is heterogeneous risk weighting across banks for similar assets, potentially undermining the goal of a level playing field in capital requirements. The European Banking Authority has explored whether Pillar 1 should be adjusted to reflect climate risks, noting that meaningful adjustments would require substantial retooling of the risk weight framework, and that the approach could be justified only if evidence demonstrates that climate risks cause banks significant losses[4].

The design of scenarios affects Pillar 2 calibration more immediately than Pillar 1, because Pillar 2 requirements are based on supervisory assessments of risks not fully captured by Pillar 1, including climate risks. The ECB has adopted a bucketing methodology where banks' Pillar 2 requirements are set based on how their capital ratios decline in stress tests, combined with supervisory judgment about individual circumstances[25][41]. This approach means that changes to scenario design directly change the basis for Pillar 2 calibration; more severe scenarios produce larger projected capital declines, moving banks into higher buckets and resulting in higher Pillar 2 requirements. The Federal Reserve has similarly used stress test results to inform capital requirements, though with less explicit bucketing methodology. The Federal Reserve's recent proposal to bring stress testing scenario design into the Administrative Procedure Act's notice-and-comment framework represents an effort to increase transparency and consistency in scenario design choices that ultimately determine capital requirements[5].

## Measurement Challenges and Methodological Gaps in Scenario Design

The calibration of capital adequacy based on environmental scenario design confronts substantial measurement challenges and methodological gaps that introduce uncertainty into the entire process. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision has identified three types of uncertainty in climate risk measurement: intrinsic future uncertainty inherent to projections of physical and transition risk drivers, measurement uncertainty related to data gaps that limit the suitability of backtesting, and model-based uncertainty reflecting the limited ability of current models to quantitatively assess identified climate risk drivers[50]. Each of these uncertainty types affects scenario design and capital adequacy calibration differently.

Intrinsic future uncertainty arises because climate risk drivers are not predetermined but depend on future policy choices, technological breakthroughs, and behavioral responses. The NGFS scenarios represent alternative possible futures reflecting different policy ambitions and technological trajectories, but no scenario is more likely than any other, as future policy choices are inherently uncertain[14][37]. Scenario design must therefore reflect a range of plausible futures rather than attempting to predict the most likely future. However, capital adequacy frameworks typically require point estimates of required capital rather than ranges; supervisors must ultimately decide how much capital institutions should hold, and range estimates create ambiguity. This has led some supervisors to calibrate capital based on the most severe adverse scenario (perhaps the Disorderly Transition scenario from the NGFS), while others use more middle-ground scenarios. The choice of which scenario to treat as the binding scenario for capital calibration directly determines capital requirements; using a Disorderly Transition scenario implies higher capital requirements than using a Delayed Transition scenario.

Measurement uncertainty arises from data limitations that constrain institutions' ability to precisely quantify climate-related risks. Many institutions lack comprehensive data on their borrowers' greenhouse gas emissions, physical asset locations relative to climate hazards, energy consumption, and other variables needed to model climate risk exposure[55]. In the absence of complete data, institutions must use proxies, estimates, or industry-level data, introducing measurement error. The OSFI's standardized climate scenario exercise found that only 14% of financial institutions' borrowers reported progress against climate targets, and Scope 3 emissions (value-chain emissions) are disclosed by only 27% of companies in major indices[51]. The scenario design must therefore be based partly on estimated or proxy data rather than direct observations, reducing the reliability of projected losses. When capital adequacy is calibrated based on stress test results derived from proxy data, the resulting capital requirements incorporate this measurement uncertainty. Supervisors may address this by adding a buffer to account for data limitations, or by revising capital requirements downward as data improves over time.

Model-based uncertainty reflects limitations in current approaches to translating climate scenarios into financial risk parameters. The transmission channels through which climate risks affect credit, market, liquidity, and operational risks are complex and non-linear, with interactions and feedback effects that existing models struggle to capture. The Bank for International Settlements has noted that while conventional risk management tools may serve as a springboard for climate-related financial risk measurement, the unique features of climate risks could challenge incorporation into existing processes[50]. In particular, historical relationships between economic variables and risk parameters (such as the relationship between GDP growth and probability of default) may not hold in a transition to a low-carbon economy with rapid technological change and structural economic shifts. Models calibrated on historical data may systematically underestimate or overestimate climate-related losses.

The Federal Reserve and other supervisors conducting stress tests have adopted what might be termed "informed conservatism" in scenario design, making design choices that bias scenarios toward severity when underlying data or models are uncertain[49]. For example, the Federal Reserve has noted that some macroeconomic variables in its proposed 2026 stress test scenarios are calibrated near the upper one-third of their historical ranges of severity without fully explaining how this calibration level was chosen[5]. This approach effectively creates additional conservatism in scenario design that translates to higher capital requirements than would result from scenarios at the median of historical ranges. The tradeoff is that institutions must hold more capital than would be required under less conservative scenarios, but supervisors have greater confidence that capital buffers are adequate to protect against adverse outcomes.

## Global Regulatory Divergence in Scenario Design and Capital Calibration

The lack of standardization in how different supervisory authorities design scenarios for environmental risk stress testing creates potential for inconsistent capital adequacy requirements across jurisdictions, undermining the level playing field that Basel regulatory harmonization was intended to establish. The MSCI analysis of climate stress tests conducted by different authorities found substantial divergences in time horizons (with some supervisors using 5-year horizons and others using 30-year horizons), in balance sheet assumptions (static versus dynamic), in risk types covered (transition only versus both transition and physical), and in scenario assumptions[51]. These divergences mean that a bank operating in multiple jurisdictions faces different scenario-based capital requirements despite facing similar underlying climate risks, complicating capital planning and creating potential for regulatory arbitrage.

The European Central Bank's climate risk stress test has used scenarios calibrated for European conditions, with European carbon price assumptions and physical risk geography reflecting European hazards[19][22]. The Bank of England has similarly developed climate stress test scenarios specific to the United Kingdom's economic structure and risk profile[10][35]. These jurisdiction-specific adaptations improve local relevance, as climate risks vary geographically and economically by region. However, they also create divergence; a bank with substantial retail mortgage lending in both the UK and the EU must comply with different scenario assumptions in the two jurisdictions, even though the underlying climate risks are determined by global climate science and policy, not national regulations. The Financial Stability Board has recommended that supervisors develop system-wide approaches to climate-related risks to promote consistency, yet the practical difficulty of achieving global coordination on scenario design has meant that such consistency remains aspirational rather than realized[54].

The European Central Bank has published supervisory expectations that banks meeting all remaining climate and environmental risk expectations by the end of 2024 should incorporate climate risks into their internal capital adequacy assessments[3]. This timeline created urgency for banks and supervisors to operationalize environmental scenario analysis, but the rapid implementation has come at the cost of methodological consistency. Different banks in the same jurisdiction have adopted different scenario frameworks and modeling approaches, leading to heterogeneity in how climate-related capital needs are assessed. The EBA has subsequently indicated that it will develop more detailed guidelines on environmental scenario analysis to provide common standards, though the guidelines themselves represent a choice about which scenarios to use and how to design them[1][9].

The Bank Policy Institute and other banking industry associations have argued that the Federal Reserve's stress testing framework, which until recently lacked transparency in how scenarios were designed, granted excessive discretion to the Federal Reserve in making design choices that directly drive capital requirements[5]. The industry associations have called for the Federal Reserve to publish its scenario design methodology and to subject it to notice-and-comment rulemaking similar to traditional administrative procedure, arguing that the design choices about calibration levels and scenario severity should be subject to public scrutiny and comment. This demand reflects the reality that scenario design is not a purely technical exercise but involves policy choices about acceptable risk levels and tradeoffs between bank capital, credit availability, and financial stability.

## Climate Risk as an Overarching Framework Affecting Multiple Risk Categories

The integration of environmental scenario analysis into capital adequacy frameworks requires supervisors and institutions to recognize that climate risks operate across all major risk categories—credit risk, market risk, liquidity risk, operational risk, and insurance risk—rather than being confined to any single category. Scenario design must therefore be comprehensive enough to address how climate drivers affect each risk type, and capital adequacy frameworks must ensure that requirements across all risk types adequately account for climate impacts. The EBA Guidelines on Environmental Scenario Analysis explicitly require that institutions consider the impact of environmental factors on multiple risk types, not just credit risk[1][9].

Credit risk, as discussed earlier, is affected through impacts on borrower ability to repay (from physical damage, stranded assets, or operating cost increases), the likelihood of default, and the recovery value of collateral. Market risk is affected through changes in asset prices as investors reassess the climate risks embodied in securities; a scenario assuming accelerated fossil fuel divestment would reduce demand for energy sector equities and bonds, depressing prices for these assets[35]. Liquidity risk is affected through potential changes in funding costs and availability if investors or depositors become concerned about a bank's exposure to climate-sensitive assets. Operational risk is affected through physical disruptions from extreme weather events, supply chain disruptions, cybersecurity risks related to climate adaptation, and human capital risks from climate migration[6].

The calibration of capital adequacy across these multiple risk types creates a coordination problem in scenario design. If a transition scenario assumes rapid coal phase-out, the credit risk impact is relatively straightforward (coal mining firms face lower demand and higher stranded asset risk), but the market risk impact depends on how asset prices adjust to this assumption. If the scenario is designed with the assumption that coal phase-out happens gradually (allowing time for market repricing), then market risk impacts may be muted; if the scenario assumes sudden policy shocks, market risk impacts could be severe. The scenario design choice about speed of transition therefore affects not only credit risk capital requirements but also market risk capital requirements. Supervisors must ensure that scenario design is coherent across risk types, avoiding the situation where scenario assumptions create implausible divergence in how different risk categories are affected.

The Bank of England has noted that market risk capital requirements may underestimate risks from climate factors, as traditional value-at-risk models rely on historical volatility and correlations that may not persist under climate-related dislocations[38]. Similarly, operational risk models may underestimate risks from extreme weather or climate-induced supply chain disruptions, as such events are rare in the historical data that calibrates operational risk models. The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions found that many institutions have expanded their climate scenario analysis beyond credit risk to encompass market risk, operational risk, and liquidity risk, but methodologies for integrating climate risks across risk types remain underdeveloped[4]. This suggests that scenario design for capital adequacy calibration will need to evolve toward more integrated frameworks that address how scenarios affect institutions across all risk types simultaneously, rather than treating climate risk as primarily a credit risk phenomenon.

## Forward-Looking Indicators and Dynamic Scenario Updating

The calibration of capital adequacy based on environmental scenarios creates a temporal challenge: institutions and supervisors must decide how frequently to update scenarios, what forward-looking indicators to monitor to trigger scenario revisions, and how to manage the potential volatility in capital requirements that could result from frequent scenario changes. Unlike traditional stress tests that typically remain constant across multiple years, environmental scenarios are expected to evolve as climate science advances, policy developments occur, and technology costs change. The scenario design choice about how to balance scenario stability (to allow institutions and supervisors to plan forward with stable capital requirements) against scenario accuracy (to ensure that capital requirements reflect current best estimates of risks) has significant implications for the effectiveness of capital buffers.

The International Monetary Fund has noted in its analysis of macroprudential capital buffers that supervisors must consider when, and to what levels, buffers should be built, when they should be released, and how releases should be communicated[18]. This framework suggests that capital adequacy requirements based on environmental scenarios may need to be dynamic, increasing as climate risks materialize more clearly and decreasing as successful mitigation efforts reduce risks. However, dynamic capital requirements create uncertainty for institutions about future capital levels, potentially affecting their lending and investment decisions. If banks anticipate that capital requirements will increase in coming years due to advancing understanding of climate risks, they may reduce lending today to build capital buffers, potentially constraining credit supply and economic growth.

The design of forward-looking indicators to trigger scenario updates remains largely underdeveloped in current regulatory frameworks. Some proposals suggest that supervisors should trigger scenario updates when key assumptions in existing scenarios diverge significantly from observed developments—for example, if actual carbon prices diverge substantially from scenario assumptions, or if observed physical damage from extreme weather events exceeds scenario projections. However, determining what magnitude of divergence justifies scenario revision requires judgment, and institutions may dispute supervisory decisions about when updates are warranted. The scenario design choice about how frequently scenarios should be updated and what indicators trigger updates is therefore a critical aspect of the capital adequacy framework that remains underspecified in most regulatory regimes.

## Stress Testing Versus Business Model Resilience: The Two-Pillar Scenario Approach

The EBA Guidelines on Environmental Scenario Analysis distinguish between Climate Stress Testing (CST) designed to assess short-term financial resilience to environmental shocks and Climate Resilience Analysis (CRA) designed to challenge medium-term to long-term business model sustainability[1][9]. This bifurcation creates a challenge for scenario design and capital adequacy calibration, as short-term stress tests and long-term resilience analysis may point to divergent conclusions about how much capital institutions should hold. A scenario could project that institutions can absorb short-term climate shocks with adequate capital buffers, but that their business models are unsustainable in the longer term if transition to net zero requires fundamental restructuring of activities.

The finance watch organization has recommended that the EBA maintain consistency between CST and CRA by requiring that both exercises use the same baseline and adverse scenarios, applied over different time horizons[12]. This approach would mean that institutions would project financial impacts under the same scenario assumptions over short-term horizons (for stress testing) and long-term horizons (for business model analysis), enabling supervisors to see how risks evolve over time and calibrate capital requirements that address both near-term shocks and long-term sustainability. However, implementing this recommendation requires scenario design that is internally consistent across time horizons, ensuring that short-term dynamics are compatible with long-term trajectories, which is technically complex.

The alternative approach, which some supervisors have adopted, is to design scenarios separately for CST and CRA purposes. The ECB's 2022 climate risk stress test used three-year horizons for short-term assessment and multi-decade horizons for long-term assessment, allowing tailoring of scenarios to the specific purpose[19]. This approach provides flexibility but creates potential for inconsistency; if short-term scenarios assume rapid near-term policy changes while long-term scenarios assume gradual transitions, the two scenario sets may paint divergent pictures of climate risks. Supervisors must then decide how to weight these divergent assessments in calibrating capital requirements. Should capital be calibrated based on the severe near-term shocks captured in CST, or on the cumulative long-term impacts captured in CRA? The scenario design choice about whether to integrate CST and CRA or keep them separate therefore affects how supervisors translate scenario results into capital requirements.

## The Application of Scenario Results to Capital Adequacy Assessment

The final step in the relationship between scenario design and capital adequacy calibration is the translation of scenario results (distributions of projected losses under different scenarios) into binding capital requirements. Supervisors and institutions must apply judgment in deciding how much capital is required to absorb the losses projected in scenarios, accounting for the probability and severity of different scenarios, the confidence in projections, and policy objectives around financial stability and credit supply.

The ECB's bucketing methodology for Pillar 2 guidance translates capital ratio depletion in stress tests into Pillar 2 guidance ranges[25]. Banks whose capital ratios decline by 8 percentage points in stress tests are placed in a specific bucket with a corresponding Pillar 2 guidance range. This approach creates a direct linkage between scenario design (which determines how much capital ratios decline) and capital requirements (which are set based on the bucket assignment). The Federal Reserve has used a similar approach, calculating stress losses and adjusting capital requirements to ensure that banks maintain capital above regulatory minimums even after stress test losses. However, the Federal Reserve has indicated that it retains discretion in how to set capital requirements based on stress test results, which some have criticized as lacking transparency[5].

The design choice about what confidence level or quantile to use in translating loss distributions to capital requirements affects the resulting capital amounts substantially. If supervisors use the 95th percentile of the loss distribution (meaning there is a 5% chance of losses exceeding the capital requirement), capital requirements will be lower than if they use the 99th percentile (1% exceedance probability). For environmental scenarios, where tail risks are substantial and poorly understood, the confidence level chosen effectively determines how much protection against extreme outcomes is deemed adequate. Supervisors have generally not been explicit about what confidence levels they implicitly use in setting climate-related capital requirements, creating ambiguity about the relationship between scenario design and capital adequacy.

The Finance Watch organization has recommended that supervisors establish clear governance for scenario analysis exercises, with assumptions and limitations reviewed and understood by senior management, and with supervisory review and validation of scenario analysis results before they are used to calibrate capital requirements[44]. This recommendation reflects recognition that scenario design involves judgment calls that have significant implications for capital adequacy, and that these judgments should be transparent and subject to supervisory oversight rather than delegated entirely to institutions. The implementation of this recommendation would require supervisors to develop greater expertise in climate science, macroeconomic modeling, and financial transmission channels, enabling them to critically assess whether institutions' scenarios are adequate and whether projected impacts are credible.

## Conclusion: Toward More Transparent and Coherent Scenario Design Frameworks

The examination of how scenario design in environmental risk stress testing affects capital adequacy calibration reveals a complex system where technical choices about climate pathways, transmission channels, time horizons, and modeling assumptions cascade through to determine the capital requirements that banks must maintain. The scenario design choices are not purely technical; they embody policy judgments about acceptable levels of financial stability risk, the appropriate balance between prudential conservatism and credit supply, and the proper role of supervisors in directing banks toward net-zero transitions. The diversity of approaches taken by different supervisors reflects legitimate differences in priorities and constraints, yet it also creates potential for regulatory inconsistency that undermines the goal of a level playing field in global finance.

The path forward requires supervisors to improve the transparency and coherence of scenario design frameworks. The Federal Reserve's recent proposal to subject its stress testing scenarios to notice-and-comment rulemaking represents a step in this direction, bringing scenario design choices into the public domain where they can be debated and refined. The European Banking Authority's development of detailed guidelines on environmental scenario analysis will establish minimum standards that should improve consistency across European banks, though challenges remain in aligning these with supervisory practices in other jurisdictions. The NGFS scenario framework provides a foundation for global consistency, yet its implementation varies significantly across supervisors depending on how they adapt scenarios to local conditions and integrate them into their specific capital adequacy frameworks.

Institutions must also develop greater capabilities in translating climate scenarios into financial projections. Many banks currently lack the data infrastructure, modeling expertise, and governance frameworks to conduct robust climate scenario analysis. The OSFI's finding that institutions are making limited progress in geolocating physical risk exposures and integrating climate risks into credit risk models suggests that many financial institutions have substantial capability gaps that constrain the quality of their internal scenario analysis and capital adequacy assessments. Addressing these gaps requires sustained investment in data infrastructure, model development, and training of personnel with expertise in climate science, environmental economics, and financial risk modeling.

The calibration of capital adequacy based on environmental scenarios will necessarily evolve as methodologies improve, data becomes more available, and supervisory experience accumulates. In the near term, supervisors will likely continue to rely on combinations of quantitative scenario analysis and qualitative expert judgment to ensure that capital requirements are set at levels that protect financial stability without excessively constraining lending. Over the medium term, as institutions develop greater analytical capabilities and supervisors refine their understanding of climate-related financial risks, scenario-based capital requirements should become more precise and consistent across jurisdictions. This evolution must be guided by a commitment to transparency about the judgment calls embedded in scenario design, to ensure that the public and market participants understand the basis for capital adequacy requirements and can assess whether they are proportionate and credible.
