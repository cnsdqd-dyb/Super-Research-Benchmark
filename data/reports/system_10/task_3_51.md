# Error Propagation in Cloud Microphysics Parameterization and Its Impact on Regional Climate Prediction Resolution

Cloud microphysics parameterization represents one of the most significant sources of uncertainty in climate and weather prediction models, with error propagation through these schemes fundamentally constraining the fidelity of regional climate predictions at all resolutions. This comprehensive analysis demonstrates that while error propagation in cloud microphysics does indeed limit regional climate prediction resolution, the relationship is complex and nonlinear, varying with both model configuration and the specific atmospheric regimes being simulated. The primary challenge stems from the inability to resolve the intricate microphysical processes occurring at scales far smaller than model grid cells, forcing modelers to employ simplified bulk parameterizations that embed both parametric uncertainties—uncertainties in the values of physical parameters—and structural uncertainties arising from approximations and oversimplifications in the fundamental equations governing these processes. As regional models attempt to operate at increasingly fine resolutions to capture local and extreme weather phenomena, they enter the "grey zone" where both resolved and subgrid-scale contributions to cloud processes remain significant, creating a paradoxical situation where increasing resolution does not necessarily improve predictions and may actually degrade them if parameterizations are not appropriately adjusted. Recent advances combining process-based parameterization development, machine learning techniques, and multi-moment microphysics schemes offer promising pathways forward, yet fundamental limitations rooted in computational feasibility and scientific understanding suggest that error propagation in cloud microphysics will continue to impose practical constraints on regional climate prediction resolution for the foreseeable future.

## Fundamentals of Cloud Microphysics Parameterization and Uncertainty Sources

Cloud microphysics remains a fundamental challenge in atmospheric modeling because the processes governing the formation, growth, and transformation of cloud and precipitation particles occur at scales far too small to be explicitly resolved in even the finest-resolution climate models.[1][9] The microphysical processes include droplet nucleation, collision-coalescence, riming, deposition, evaporation, and the interactions between aerosols and hydrometeors, all of which operate at scales of micrometers to millimeters and milliseconds to seconds.[15][30] Global climate models typically operate with grid spacing on the order of one hundred kilometers or more, while even cutting-edge regional convection-permitting models working at horizontal resolutions of one to four kilometers still cannot resolve individual cloud particles or the turbulent eddies that govern their behavior.[11][32] This fundamental scale mismatch necessitates the use of parameterization schemes that aggregate the effects of all microphysical processes occurring within a model grid cell into simplified mathematical relationships.[15][30]

The uncertainty in cloud microphysics parameterization arises from two fundamentally different sources that are often confused in the literature but require distinct treatment.[3][24][34][37] Parametric uncertainty refers to uncertainty in the values of parameters that appear in the governing equations of a parameterization scheme, such as the intercept parameter of a particle size distribution, the coefficients governing particle fall speed, or the activation threshold for cloud condensation nuclei.[3][4][7][37] These parameters often come from laboratory experiments, field observations, or high-resolution modeling studies that may not represent the full range of atmospheric conditions encountered in climate simulations, or may themselves contain substantial measurement uncertainty.[19][21][39] Structural uncertainty, by contrast, refers to uncertainty inherent in the mathematical form and fundamental assumptions embedded in a parameterization scheme itself.[3][24][34][37] For example, virtually all bulk microphysics schemes assume that the particle size distribution within a grid cell can be adequately represented by a single analytical function, such as a Marshall-Palmer exponential distribution.[39] This assumption, while computationally tractable, dramatically oversimplifies the actual complexity of particle populations in real clouds, which often exhibit multimodal size distributions, rapid temporal variations, and strong spatial heterogeneity.[39][57]

The most commonly employed microphysics parameterization frameworks are bulk schemes, which predict one or more moments of an assumed particle size distribution rather than tracking individual particles.[1][9][31][39][57] Single-moment bulk schemes, the simplest and most computationally efficient approach, predict only the mass mixing ratio of each hydrometeor category (cloud water, rain, snow, graupel, ice), and must assume both the shape of the size distribution and the number concentration of particles.[39][57] Two-moment schemes predict both mass and number concentration, allowing the size distribution to evolve somewhat more freely, but at substantially greater computational cost and with additional uncertain parameters related to the particle number evolution.[1][9][24][31][39][57] Three-moment schemes, which additionally predict reflectivity to account for size distribution shape evolution, represent a further increase in complexity and computational burden.[1] Alternative approaches include bin schemes, which track particle size distributions in discrete size classes but are computationally prohibitive for large-scale climate applications, and spectral microphysics approaches that attempt to evolve the full particle size distribution explicitly.[1][39][57]

Recent research has revealed that structural differences between schemes can be as important as parametric differences in determining microphysical simulation outcomes.[57] Examining bulk and bin schemes with identical physical parameterizations, researchers found that differences could be small for many quantities but could exceed fifty percent for precipitation rate, with the largest differences arising specifically from how collisional processes are treated in the different structural frameworks.[57] This finding has profound implications for understanding error propagation, suggesting that developing more sophisticated parameterizations alone will not eliminate uncertainty unless the fundamental structural approaches are also critically evaluated and potentially redesigned.[1][24][34][57]

## Sources and Mechanisms of Error Propagation Through Climate Models

Error propagation in cloud microphysics parameterization operates through multiple interconnected mechanisms that cascade through climate models, amplifying uncertainties at larger scales through nonlinear feedbacks and interactions with other physical processes.[3][13][26][37] Understanding these mechanisms requires distinguishing between different types of propagation pathways and recognizing that errors in microphysics do not simply accumulate linearly but interact with the chaotic dynamics of the atmosphere itself.[13][26][37]

The most direct pathway for error propagation involves the impact of microphysical parameterizations on the production and loss rates of cloud water and precipitation.[9][14][25][31][55] Numerous sensitivity studies have demonstrated that uncertainty in critical parameters governing particle fall speeds, size distributions, and conversion thresholds can substantially alter simulated precipitation amounts and spatial distributions.[9][14][21][25][31] For instance, research examining sensitivity of tropical condensate distribution to microphysics parameters found that modifications to a single property—the fall speed of particles—largely determines changes in condensate amount across different hydrometeor categories, with the effect being remarkably consistent across different types of microphysics schemes.[9][31] When particles fall faster, less cloud water accumulates in the atmosphere, which has cascading consequences for cloud radiative properties, surface precipitation, and atmospheric stability.[9][31] This fundamental mechanism reveals how a subtle parameterization detail propagates into large-scale model behavior.

Cloud radiative forcing represents a second critical pathway through which microphysics errors propagate to affect climate predictions.[6][13][43] Cloud microphysical properties directly determine cloud optical depth, which controls how much solar radiation is reflected back to space and how much longwave radiation is trapped in the atmosphere.[6][13][43] The systematic errors in cloud fraction and cloud properties produced by microphysics parameterizations translate directly into errors in Earth's radiative energy balance.[13][39] A comprehensive analysis of global climate model error in CMIP5 models revealed that systematic biases in simulated cloud fraction produce an annual average long-wave cloud forcing error of approximately plus or minus four watts per square meter—a substantial uncertainty that, when propagated through climate simulations, yields plus or minus fifteen degrees Celsius uncertainty in projected global temperature change over a century.[13] This striking finding demonstrates that parameterization errors at the cloud microphysics level ultimately limit the reliability of climate projections at the global scale.[13][43]

Latent heating represents a third propagation mechanism of particular importance for convective systems and regional precipitation.[17][25][26][55] The growth and transformation of cloud particles releases or absorbs latent heat, which directly affects atmospheric stability and convective intensity.[25][55] Parameterization errors that misrepresent the rates at which cloud water converts to rain or snow, or the efficiency of the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen process in mixed-phase clouds, alter the timing, intensity, and spatial organization of convection.[17][25][26][55] These altered convective properties then feed back into the model dynamics through altered wind patterns, vertical motion, and moisture convergence, creating complex nonlinear interactions that are difficult to predict a priori.[17][26][55] Research examining aerosol effects on deep convection using different microphysics parameterizations found that subtle differences in how the schemes handle condensation and evaporation could produce dramatically different convective responses to the same aerosol perturbation, demonstrating the sensitivity of convective dynamics to microphysical details.[55]

The time-stepping and numerical integration of parameterizations introduces another important error propagation mechanism that is often overlooked in discussions of parameterization uncertainty.[14][26] The time step chosen for model integration directly affects how microphysical processes are computed and accumulated.[14] Research examining the sensitivity of convection-permitting simulations to model time step revealed strong sensitivities in cloud microphysics, with different time steps producing substantial differences in predicted hydrometeor distributions and precipitation patterns even though the underlying physics parameterization remained identical.[14] This finding indicates that error propagation is not solely a consequence of parameterization inaccuracy but also depends critically on numerical implementation details, complicating efforts to isolate and quantify the true sources of uncertainty.[14]

Interactions and feedbacks between microphysics parameterizations and other physical processes amplify error propagation in ways that are difficult to predict without explicit modeling.[3][8][15][26][37] Convection parameterizations interact strongly with microphysics parameterizations—the rate at which convection parameterizations remove atmospheric moisture depends on how precipitation is produced by microphysics parameterizations, and conversely, the vertical motion field produced by convection schemes affects microphysical process rates.[8][11][26][35] Radiation parameterizations depend critically on cloud properties determined by microphysics, and surface processes respond to precipitation patterns determined by microphysics parameterizations.[8][15][26] This web of interactions means that errors in microphysics propagate through the entire model system, and conversely, can be partially compensated by errors in other schemes—a phenomenon known as error cancellation or tuning that can mask fundamental parameterization weaknesses.[3][54] The practice of model tuning, wherein parameters are adjusted to match observations even when this violates physical constraints, exemplifies how error propagation can become hidden through compensating errors.[54]

## Resolution Dependence of Parameterization Schemes and the Grey Zone Challenge

One of the most profound implications of cloud microphysics parameterization uncertainty for regional climate prediction is the resolution-dependent behavior of parameterization schemes and the emergence of the "grey zone" problem at intermediate resolutions.[11][38][50][53] As models increase in resolution from traditional coarse-resolution global models (100-200 km) toward convection-permitting resolutions (1-4 km), the behavior of parameterization schemes changes in ways that are not always intuitive and often degrade model performance.[11][18][32][38][50]

At coarse resolutions characteristic of traditional global climate models (100+ km), parameterization schemes were designed with the assumption that an ensemble of many clouds and turbulent eddies exists within each grid cell, with properties that can be described by deterministic statistical relationships.[38][50] Under this assumption, parameterizations generally function as intended, transforming grid-cell-averaged environmental conditions into average effects of unresolved processes.[38][50] However, at finer resolutions, this assumption progressively breaks down. The grid zone, defined as the range of resolutions between roughly 200 meters and 10 kilometers where both resolved and subgrid-scale contributions to cloud and turbulent processes provide non-negligible contributions, creates fundamental challenges for parameterization development.[38][50] Within this grey zone, some aspect of cloud structure and convection become partially resolved by the model dynamics, while other aspects remain subgrid.[38][50] This creates a paradoxical situation: removing the convection parameterization entirely at these resolutions—a common practice in convection-permitting models—often degradation rather than improvement because the partially resolved convection is less accurate than the parameterized version, since convective instability accumulates at the coarse subgrid scale and then releases catastrophically at the resolved scale, producing unrealistic convective mass fluxes and precipitation.[11][38][50]

The dependence of parameterization behavior on model resolution requires the development of "scale-aware" parameterization schemes that explicitly account for how much of a process is resolved versus subgrid at a given model resolution.[38][50][53] In principle, as resolution increases and more of a process becomes explicitly resolved, the parameterization should transition smoothly from predicting the full effect of that process (at coarse resolutions) to predicting only the effect of the unresolved portion (at fine resolutions).[38][50][53] However, implementing this scale awareness is extremely challenging both conceptually and practically.[38][50][53] Research examining boundary layer turbulence parameterizations in the grey zone found that the best agreement between simulations at different resolutions occurs when parameterization intensity is at full strength rather than reduced according to intuitive scale-aware logic, suggesting that current scale-aware approaches may be fundamentally misguided or that resolution-independent parameterization behavior is not achievable.[38]

This resolution-dependent parameterization behavior has profound implications for regional climate prediction error propagation because it means that errors introduced by parameterizations change systematically as model resolution changes.[18][25][32][33][50][53] A regional model at 10 km resolution may produce acceptable precipitation patterns because the convection parameterization compensates for underresolved updrafts, while the same model at 4 km resolution without convection parameterization might produce excessive convection due to explosive release of partially-resolved convective instability.[11][38][50] The error propagation pathways change with resolution, making it difficult to develop parameterizations that perform well across the range of resolutions used in operational weather prediction and climate modeling.[11][18][25][32][38][50][53] Critically, kilometer-scale models have required extensive retuning of microphysics and other parameterizations compared to coarser-resolution models, indicating that the error propagation mechanisms themselves differ substantially between resolutions.[18][32][51]

Recent research comparing cloud microphysics schemes across different spatial configurations has revealed additional complexity in how parameterization errors depend on model domain setup.[25][33] Examining the same microphysics schemes in regional versus global model configurations, researchers found that domain configuration amplifies sensitivities to microphysics parameterizations, with global simulations exhibiting substantially different fog and cloud ice amounts compared to regional simulations using identical microphysics schemes.[25][33] These differences arise because global models simulate large-scale circulation patterns differently than regional models constrained by specified boundary conditions, and these circulation differences interact with microphysics parameterizations in ways that produce substantially different condensate distributions.[25][33] This finding indicates that error propagation in microphysics parameterizations is not purely a resolution issue but depends on the broader modeling framework and how it constrains the large-scale flow.

## Quantification of Parameterization Error and Its Climate Impact

Recent advances in quantifying parameterization uncertainty have provided unprecedented insight into the magnitude of error propagation and its climate impacts, revealing both the seriousness of the challenge and potential pathways for improvement.[3][4][7][21][24][37] Traditional approaches to parameterization development focused primarily on parametric uncertainty—the uncertainty in parameter values—while treating structural uncertainty as a secondary concern to be addressed through empirical tuning.[3][37] However, emerging research demonstrates that structural uncertainty may actually dominate parametric uncertainty in many cases, and that the two sources of uncertainty interact in complex ways that simple linearized error propagation cannot capture.[3][24][34][37]

Methods for quantifying parametric uncertainty have advanced considerably through ensemble approaches and Bayesian inference techniques.[3][4][7][21][37] Perturbed parameter ensembles (PPEs), wherein a climate model is run many times with different parameter values sampled from plausible ranges, allow researchers to explore the sensitivity of model outputs to individual parameters and their interactions.[3][21][37] This approach has revealed several important findings about microphysics parameterization error: first, that sensitivities to microphysics parameters are often highly nonlinear and can be substantially different in different atmospheric regimes; second, that multiple parameter combinations can produce acceptable matches to observations, indicating that parameterization error is underdetermined by current observational constraints; and third, that the parameter sensitivities often interact with other aspects of the model system in unexpected ways.[3][4][7][21][37] Research examining winter precipitation using perturbed parameter ensembles found that parameter optimization using Bayesian approaches could reduce precipitation simulation errors by up to thirty percent, but that the optimal parameter values differed between different precipitation cases, indicating that no single parameter set is optimal for all conditions.[21]

Bayesian approaches to parameterization development have emerged as a powerful framework for simultaneously addressing both parametric and structural uncertainty while propagating uncertainty through predictions to produce probabilistic climate projections.[3][7][27][37] Rather than attempting to identify a single best-fit parameter value, these approaches characterize uncertainty as a distribution over parameter space, with the distribution constrained by available data and refined through iterative calibration.[3][27][37] The distribution can then be propagated through predictions to produce a corresponding distribution of possible model outputs, yielding quantitative probabilistic information about where predictions are particularly uncertain.[3][27][37] Research applying ensemble Kalman inversion to parameterization learning demonstrated that this approach can learn parameter distributions from noisy, chaotic climate model trajectories while automatically accounting for observational error and model structural inadequacies.[27] Importantly, the correlation structure within the parameter distribution revealed by Bayesian learning can guide further improvements in parameterization structure—parameters that are highly correlated may be redundant and could potentially be replaced by functions of other parameters.[27]

Machine learning approaches to parameterization learning and uncertainty quantification have emerged as complementary techniques, though they introduce their own challenges related to interpretability and generalization.[3][15][18][27][29] Neural networks trained on high-resolution simulations or observations can learn to predict cloud microphysical process rates given resolved-scale variables, potentially providing more accurate and computationally efficient parameterizations than traditional physics-based schemes.[29] However, the "black box" nature of neural network parameterizations raises legitimate concerns about their physical interpretability, whether they will generalize to climate states outside the training data distribution, and whether they appropriately conserve energy and mass.[15][27][29] A balanced approach combining process-based parameterizations that encode physical laws with machine learning techniques that optimize closure functions has emerged as a promising direction, allowing leverage of the strengths of both approaches while mitigating their individual weaknesses.[18]

Sensitivity analyses have quantified which aspects of microphysics parameterizations have the largest impact on model predictions, helping prioritize parameterization development efforts and identify key uncertainties.[4][19][21][25][31] A comprehensive examination of how cloud microphysical parameter perturbations affect cloud phase in mixed-phase clouds found that a single parameter—the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen time scale—dominates the variance in cloud phase partitioning, accounting for the vast majority of uncertainty in supercooled cloud fraction.[19] This finding is particularly important because cloud phase in mixed-phase clouds strongly affects cloud optical properties and radiative effects, which in turn significantly influence climate sensitivity to CO₂ increases.[19] Another sensitivity analysis of tropical cloud microphysics found that fall speed modifications alone could explain most changes in cloud condensate amount across different parameter perturbations and different microphysics schemes, suggesting that relatively simple properties of microphysics parameterizations may dominate their climate impact.[9][31] These findings suggest that focused efforts to constrain key parameters—particularly those governing fall speeds and cloud phase partitioning—may yield substantial improvements in parameterization accuracy despite the large number of total uncertain parameters.

## Impact on Regional Climate Prediction Across Model Resolutions

The practical impact of cloud microphysics parameterization error on regional climate prediction depends critically on model resolution, application, and the specific atmospheric phenomena of interest. Regional climate models, which nest high-resolution limited domains within coarser-resolution global models, are increasingly important tools for developing local climate projections needed for adaptation planning.[2][5][11][17][32] However, regional models inherit biases from their parent global models and introduce their own biases through the choice of parameterizations and other model configurations, with microphysics parameterization uncertainty contributing substantially to overall model error.[8][11][17][32]

For precipitation prediction, which is a primary application of regional climate models, microphysics parameterization uncertainty represents a major source of error across all resolutions.[11][17][25][33] Traditional global climate models with convection parameterizations produce systematic biases in precipitation, including underestimation of extreme precipitation intensities and poor representation of the diurnal cycle of precipitation, with microphysics parameterizations contributing to these biases through their control on precipitation formation efficiency and particle fall speeds.[11][15][35] High-resolution convection-permitting models without convection parameterizations were initially expected to eliminate many of these biases, but instead introduced new challenges: while these models can capture observed heavy precipitation events with reasonable accuracy, they sometimes exhibit systematic biases that equal or exceed those of coarser-resolution models, suggesting that finer resolution alone does not solve parameterization-related problems and may actually introduce new ones if microphysics parameterizations are not appropriately adjusted for the new resolution.[11][17][32][38][50]

Convection-permitting models at 1-4 km resolution operate in the grey zone where parameterization uncertainty is particularly acute. These models typically eliminate the deep convection parameterization on the grounds that they explicitly resolve convection, but microphysics parameterization becomes increasingly important at these resolutions because it controls cloud droplet activation, warm rain formation, and ice particle properties—processes that even the highest-resolution models cannot fully resolve.[11][18][25][32][33][50] Research comparing different microphysics schemes at convection-permitting resolution found that scheme choice substantially affects simulated precipitation characteristics and cloud properties, with single-moment and double-moment schemes producing dramatically different amounts of graupel (up to five times differences), rainfall (100 percent differences), and fog (up to five times differences).[25][33] Perhaps more importantly, these large differences in hydrometeor characteristics occurred despite relatively similar predictions of total precipitation and outgoing radiation at larger scales, suggesting that microphysics scheme differences are dampened at the scale of integrated quantities but remain substantial for cloud structure—a pattern consistent with large-scale constraints on precipitation mentioned earlier in this analysis.[25][33]

For precipitation extremes, a key application of regional climate models, microphysics parameterization choices have been shown to substantially affect simulated extreme precipitation amounts.[17][40][44] Convection-permitting models can capture heavy precipitation events more accurately than coarser models, and when appropriate microphysics parameterizations are employed, the model output can be used in probabilistic flood risk assessments.[17] However, biases remain, and in some cases systematic underestimation or overestimation of extreme precipitation occurs depending on parameterization choices.[17] A critical finding from recent research is that the uncertainty from microphysics parameterization effects on extreme precipitation (along with other model physics choices like planetary boundary layer schemes) can be quantified through ensemble approaches, allowing for quantification of prediction uncertainty stemming specifically from parameterization choices.[17]

For cloud feedbacks and climate sensitivity, which determine the magnitude of climate change for a given greenhouse gas forcing increase, microphysics parameterization uncertainty represents one of the largest sources of model uncertainty.[6][43] Cloud feedbacks—the way that clouds change in response to warming and how those changes affect the radiation balance—vary dramatically among climate models, with much of this variation attributable to differences in how cloud microphysics is parameterized.[6][43] The uncertainty in cloud microphysics feedbacks is particularly large for mixed-phase clouds at temperatures near the freezing point, where the balance between liquid and ice phase strongly affects cloud optical properties.[6][19][43] Research examining cloud phase in mixed-phase clouds found that parameterization uncertainties in the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen process could shift projections of cloud phase and thus cloud radiative effects, with implications for regional and global temperature projections.[19] This research explicitly connects local-scale microphysical uncertainties to large-scale climate sensitivity, demonstrating that error propagation from microphysics parameterizations scales from the micrometer level to the global scale.

## Contemporary Approaches to Mitigating Parameterization Error Propagation

Recognizing the severity of cloud microphysics parameterization error, the scientific community has developed and continues to develop multiple complementary approaches to reduce uncertainty and improve prediction fidelity.[3][7][15][16][18][27][37][51] These approaches operate at different levels, from fundamental physics improvements to calibration techniques to emerging machine learning methods.

Process-based parameterization development aims to ground parameterizations more firmly in fundamental physics by deriving them through systematic coarse-graining of the detailed equations governing small-scale processes.[3][18][37] Rather than developing parameterizations through purely empirical fitting to observations or high-resolution simulations, process-based approaches systematically derive closure equations for parameterizations by averaging or integrating the governing equations over subgrid scales.[3][18][37] For instance, conditional averaging can be used to derive distinct equation sets for coherent structures like convective plumes and their surrounding turbulent environment, then combining these through well-defined coupling terms.[16][18] This approach has led to the development of unified parameterizations capable of representing the full spectrum of atmospheric turbulence and convection from boundary layer turbulence through deep cumulonimbus convection, achieving improved simulation of the diurnal cycle of convection compared to traditional parameterizations.[16][18]

Model calibration and tuning using advanced optimization techniques represents a second major approach to reducing parameterization error propagation.[3][51][54] Rather than manually tuning parameters based on expert judgment, automated machine learning-based tuning employs sophisticated optimization algorithms to systematically search the parameter space and identify parameter combinations that minimize model biases in key observational metrics.[51][54] Machine learning-based tuning of the ICON climate model identified critical parameters controlling cloud radiative effects and precipitation patterns, achieving substantial improvements in model performance.[51] Importantly, this approach can also identify which parameters have the most influence on which aspects of the climate system, providing scientific insight beyond the simple goal of improving model skill.[51] Parameter tuning has been shown effective at reducing model biases even when using a relatively coarse model resolution, suggesting that better tuning alone, without other parameterization improvements, can yield substantial improvements in regional climate predictions.[51]

Uncertainty quantification frameworks provide another crucial approach by making previously hidden parameterization uncertainties explicit and quantifiable.[3][7][21][27][37] By characterizing parameterization uncertainty as distributions over parameter space and propagating these distributions through climate predictions, researchers can produce probabilistic predictions that inform decision-making about climate impacts and enable evaluation of which aspects of the climate system are predicted with high versus low confidence.[3][7][27][37] This probabilistic approach fundamentally changes how climate predictions should be interpreted and used, from a deterministic view of a single best-fit model to a probabilistic view recognizing inherent limitations in current knowledge and modeling capability.[3][27][37] The development of the Calibrate, Emulate, Sample framework demonstrates that this uncertainty quantification can be computationally feasible even for expensive climate models through the use of surrogate models and targeted data assimilation.[3][37]

Multi-physics ensemble approaches, wherein climate models are run with different parameterization schemes or parameter values and the ensemble spread quantified, provide practical methods for accounting for parameterization uncertainty in operational applications.[17][25][26][33] By comparing predictions across multiple realizations with different parameterization choices, forecasters and climate scientists can assess robustness of findings to parameterization uncertainty, identify where predictions are particularly sensitive to parameterization choices, and potentially improve predictions through ensemble-based combination methods.[17][26][33] However, ensemble methods alone do not solve the underlying parameterization problem—if all ensemble members use flawed parameterizations, the ensemble may simply be confidently wrong in a consistent manner.[26][54]

Resolution optimization, wherein model resolution is selected based on specific application goals rather than simply maximizing resolution as computational resources allow, represents an underutilized approach to managing parameterization error propagation.[18][32][50][51] Not all applications benefit from kilometer-scale resolution, and in some cases coarser resolution models with better-tuned parameterizations may outperform fine-resolution models with undertested parameterizations.[18][51] The practice of extensive model tuning at coarser resolutions before attempting to develop kilometer-scale models, and the recognition that parameterizations must be substantially redesigned for kilometer-scale applications, suggests that intermediate resolutions (10-20 km) may represent an optimal balance between capturing important phenomena explicitly and avoiding the parameterization challenges of the grey zone.[18][32][38][50]

## Future Directions and Emerging Research Frontiers

The field of cloud microphysics parameterization is actively evolving, with several emerging research directions offering promise for substantially reducing error propagation in regional climate predictions. These developments operate at multiple scales and employ diverse approaches, from improved observational constraints to fundamental theoretical advances to revolutionary computational approaches.

High-resolution observations of cloud microphysics, obtained through sophisticated remote sensing and in situ measurement campaigns, provide increasingly valuable constraints on parameterization development.[59][60] Two-dimensional video disdrometer measurements, cloud radar observations, and satellite-based cloud property retrievals supply information about particle size distributions, cloud phase, and cloud properties that can be compared directly to parameterization predictions.[21][59][60] By collecting extensive observational datasets specifically designed to test parameterizations, researchers can constrain the most important sources of parameterization uncertainty and reduce the underdetermination inherent in current approaches.[21][59] The International Collaborative Experiments for the PyeongChang 2018 Olympic and Paralympic winter games field campaign exemplifies this approach, providing constraints on winter precipitation parameterizations that enabled identification of systematic deviations between model assumptions and observations and quantification of parameter optimization improvements.[21]

Coupled multi-scale modeling approaches, which link high-resolution large-eddy simulations that explicitly resolve clouds with coarser-resolution climate models, offer a pathway to systematically develop and test parameterizations against reality.[3][7][12][18] Large-eddy simulations can simulate cloud microphysical processes at resolutions approaching the size of individual cloud droplets, providing information about microphysical process rates under specific environmental conditions that can inform parameterization development and testing.[7][12][18] By establishing a hierarchy of models from detailed large-eddy simulations through convection-resolving models to global climate models, researchers can trace how uncertainty propagates through different levels of model complexity and develop parameterizations grounded in detailed process understanding.[7][18] This hierarchical approach, exemplified in the development of the BOSS (Bayesian Observationally-constrained Statistical-physical Scheme) parameterization, provides a principled framework for bottom-up parameterization development that is then constrained using top-down global observational constraints.[7]

Kilometer-scale global modeling represents an emerging frontier that will eventually allow explicit simulation of most cloud processes, though substantial parameterization challenges remain even at this resolution.[18][32][50][51] Current decade-long simulations at 2-5 km global resolution demonstrate the feasibility of explicitly simulating deep convection and many cloud processes without convection parameterizations, but these simulations require extensive parameterization tuning and still employ parameterizations for microphysics and subgrid turbulence.[18][32][50][51] As these simulations become routine through continued computational advances, they will provide unprecedented opportunities to test and refine parameterizations against observations while simultaneously serving as high-fidelity benchmarks for lower-resolution models.[18][32] However, computational feasibility remains a limiting factor, with kilometer-scale simulations currently prohibitively expensive for routine climate projections.[18][32]

Fundamental theoretical advances in understanding small-scale atmospheric processes offer additional promise for improving parameterizations.[16][18][37] Recent theoretical developments in turbulence and convection parameterization have led to improved understanding of entrainment and detrainment processes, momentum exchange between plumes and their environment, and the representation of convective plumes in bulk parameterization frameworks.[16][18] By grounding parameterizations in improved theoretical understanding rather than purely empirical relationships, researchers hope to develop schemes that will perform better across different climate states and resolutions than empirically-tuned parameterizations.[16][18][37] The unified eddy-diffusivity mass-flux framework developed at CliMA exemplifies this approach, providing a theoretically sound parameterization that captures the behavior of atmospheric turbulence and convection from boundary layers through deep convection within a single consistent framework.[16]

## Conclusion: Synthesis and Implications for Regional Climate Prediction

Error propagation in cloud microphysics parameterization demonstrably limits regional climate prediction resolution, but the relationship between parameterization error and prediction resolution is complex, nonlinear, and multidimensional rather than simple or monotonic. The fundamental physical basis of this limitation lies in the enormous disparity between the scales at which microphysical processes occur (micrometers to millimeters) and the scales at which climate models operate (kilometers to global), forcing the use of simplified parameterization schemes that embed both parametric uncertainties arising from insufficient knowledge of parameter values and structural uncertainties arising from necessary approximations in the fundamental equations governing these processes. Error propagates from microphysics parameterizations through multiple interconnected pathways—affecting precipitation formation, cloud radiative properties, latent heating, and ultimately the dynamics of the resolved-scale flow—making it impossible to fully isolate microphysics errors from the broader model system.

The resolution-dependent behavior of parameterization schemes, exemplified by the grey zone problem at intermediate resolutions where neither fully parameterized nor fully resolved representations of clouds and convection prove adequate, imposes a particularly severe constraint on regional climate prediction resolution. At coarse resolutions (100+ km), parameterizations were designed to function and generally perform adequately. At very fine resolutions (1-4 km), researchers can explicitly simulate deep convection and many cloud processes while microphysics parameterization remains important but more tractable. However, at intermediate resolutions in the grey zone (5-30 km), the situation deteriorates, with parameterization uncertainties amplified and model performance sometimes degrading with increasing resolution unless parameterizations are substantially modified.[11][38][50] This suggests that the traditional approach of pursuing ever-finer resolution may not be optimal for regional climate prediction, and that intermediate-resolution models with carefully tuned parameterizations may provide better predictions than kilometer-scale models with undertested parameterizations.

Yet despite these fundamental limitations, substantial progress is possible and is actively occurring. Emerging approaches combining process-based parameterization development, machine learning optimization, Bayesian uncertainty quantification, and multi-physics ensembles offer pathways to substantially reduce (though never eliminate) parameterization error propagation and improve regional climate predictions.[3][7][15][18][27][37][51] The key insight from recent research is that parameterization development is not simply a matter of collecting more observational data or developing more sophisticated mathematical forms, but rather requires a shift in how parameterizations are developed, tested, and applied—from traditional single-parameter-set approaches to probabilistic frameworks that explicitly quantify and propagate uncertainty through predictions.[3][27][37] This paradigm shift, combined with continued advances in computational capacity enabling finer-resolution models and more ensemble members, offers genuine hope for substantially improved regional climate predictions within the next decade.

For practitioners developing regional climate models and generating climate projections for adaptation planning, the implications are clear: (1) microphysics parameterization uncertainty should be quantified and propagated through predictions to generate probabilistic projections rather than deterministic point estimates; (2) model resolution should be selected based on specific application goals rather than simply maximizing resolution, with recognition that intermediate resolutions may be optimal for some applications; (3) multiple parameterization schemes should be employed through multi-physics ensembles to characterize uncertainty related to parameterization choices; (4) extensive model tuning and calibration using advanced optimization methods should be prioritized as fundamental to achieving good model performance; and (5) interdisciplinary collaboration between cloud physicists, mathematicians, machine learning specialists, and traditional atmospheric modelers is essential for continued progress. The field has recognized that error propagation in cloud microphysics represents a fundamental challenge requiring fundamental advances in how parameterizations are developed and employed, and the research directions being pursued offer genuine promise that regional climate predictions will become increasingly reliable in the coming decades, even if parameterization uncertainty will necessarily remain a significant source of uncertainty in climate projections.
