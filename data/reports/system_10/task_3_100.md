# Rebuilding Cross-Species Justice Foundations in Genome Editing Ethics: Moving Beyond Anthropocentrism

The emergence of genome editing technologies, particularly CRISPR-Cas9, has catalyzed unprecedented ethical debates about humanity's relationship with nature and non-human life. However, existing ethical frameworks for genome editing remain fundamentally constrained by anthropocentric assumptions that privilege human interests, human autonomy, and human definitions of harm and benefit. This report argues that developing truly just frameworks for genome editing requires a paradigmatic shift toward multispecies justice—an approach that recognizes the intrinsic value of non-human animals, plants, and ecosystems while acknowledging the complexity of interspecies relationships and the historical exclusion of Indigenous perspectives from scientific and ethical decision-making. By examining how genome editing technologies intersect with the interests of non-human species, examining the limitations of conventional bioethical principles, and exploring emerging frameworks that center relational justice and ecological stewardship, this report demonstrates that cross-species justice in genome editing ethics demands both conceptual innovation and institutional transformation. The rebuilding of justice foundations must incorporate Indigenous knowledge systems, recognize vulnerability as a universal condition affecting all living beings, operationalize ecological stewardship as a binding principle, and establish governance mechanisms that decenter human exceptionalism while attending to the specific historical and political contexts in which genome editing technologies are deployed and might be implemented.

## The Anthropocentric Foundations of Contemporary Genome Editing Ethics

Contemporary bioethical frameworks for genome editing remain deeply rooted in anthropocentric premises that assume human beings occupy a privileged moral position in the natural world.[1][4][30] The fundamental ethical principles that guide discussions of genome editing—autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice—are framed almost exclusively around human interests, human preferences, and human decision-making.[1] When ethical discussions of genome editing do address non-human animals, they typically do so instrumentally, asking whether using animals for research or creating genetically modified animals advances human interests or causes unacceptable suffering to individual animals, rather than questioning the underlying assumption that non-human life exists primarily to serve human purposes.[2][34] This anthropocentric orientation reflects deeper structural features of Western scientific and legal institutions that have historically treated nature as a resource to be exploited, rather than as a collection of beings with their own forms of agency, value, and moral standing.[3][19]

The problem of anthropocentrism in genome editing ethics becomes particularly acute when examining applications intended to modify non-human animals or manipulate wild ecosystems through technologies such as gene drives.[2][9][12] Even when ethicists express concern about animal welfare or ecological consequences, the analysis typically remains bounded within human-centered frameworks of harm and benefit.[3][4] Questions about whether editing an animal's genome violates its intrinsic value, affects its capacity to flourish according to its own nature (its *telos*), or impacts its relationships with other species and ecosystem processes are often treated as secondary to questions about whether the modification serves human medical or agricultural goals.[5][7][13] Similarly, discussions of gene drive technologies designed to control disease vectors or invasive species focus predominantly on how these interventions affect human public health or economic interests, with less sustained attention to how they might impact the agency and flourishing of non-human organisms or the ecological communities in which these organisms participate.[9][12][32]

The anthropocentrism embedded in current genome editing ethics also manifests in how decisions are made about which research is ethically permissible and whose perspectives count in deliberative processes.[12][27][51] Academic ethicists, scientists, and policy makers—predominantly from wealthy, industrialized nations and educated in Western scientific traditions—have exercised disproportionate influence in shaping global discussions about genome editing ethics, while Indigenous peoples, who have stewarded specific territories and their non-human inhabitants for thousands of years and possess sophisticated knowledge systems about ecological relationships, have been systematically excluded from these conversations.[20][23][45][51] This epistemic injustice—the exclusion of marginalized knowledge systems and the perspectives of those most directly affected by biotechnological innovations—perpetuates colonial patterns in which Western expertise is privileged and other ways of knowing are rendered invisible or subordinated.[54] The result is a body of ethical guidance that reflects the values and concerns of a narrow segment of the global population and fails to account for the multiplicity of relationships between humans and non-human worlds that characterize human societies globally.

The anthropocentric framing of genome editing ethics also obscures how power operates in decisions about which organisms get genetically modified, for what purposes, and who benefits from such modifications.[8][11][44] By focusing ethical analysis narrowly on questions of individual autonomy and welfare, anthropocentric frameworks fail to address the ways that genome editing might intensify existing inequalities within human societies or between humans and other species.[8][11][43] For instance, germline editing technologies that prevent genetic disease could, if available only to wealthy populations, create genetic stratification within human societies, intensifying class-based inequalities at the genetic level.[43] Similarly, genome editing in non-human animals directed toward increasing agricultural productivity might enable greater intensification of industrial farming, potentially increasing suffering for vast numbers of animals while benefiting economically powerful agribusiness corporations.[5][31][46] Anthropocentric ethics, by leaving these structural and political dimensions unexamined, risks legitimizing uses of genome editing that reinforce existing patterns of domination and exploitation.

## Multispecies Justice as an Alternative Framework

Emerging from interdisciplinary scholarship in environmental ethics, animal studies, Indigenous studies, and political ecology, **multispecies justice** offers a fundamentally different approach to understanding humanity's ethical obligations across and beyond species boundaries.[15][19][22] Rather than assuming that justice applies uniquely or primarily to human beings, multispecies justice recognizes that questions of fairness, recognition, and proper distribution of burdens and benefits necessarily involve non-human animals, plants, ecosystems, and the complex webs of relationship that connect all living beings.[15][19] This framework rejects the premise that human exceptionalism is a justified foundation for ethics, instead arguing that the particularity and specificity of each being—what makes it the kind of entity it is—generates morally relevant claims that deserve recognition and consideration in decisions that affect it.[15][19][22]

Multispecies justice explicitly challenges what scholars have called "the fiction of humans as individual, isolated, unattached and unencumbered" and the correlative assumption that non-human nature is merely passive background to human activity.[15] Instead, this framework emphasizes that humans are constitutively entangled with other species and ecosystems—that human flourishing depends upon relationships with and within more-than-human worlds, and that those relationships are reciprocal rather than unidirectional.[15][19][49] This understanding has profound implications for how we conceptualize justice. If justice is about ensuring that all beings who are affected by decisions can have their interests recognized and their agency respected, then justice in the context of genome editing must extend beyond asking what humans want or how human autonomy is affected, to asking what impacts genome editing technologies have on the capacities, relationships, and futures of non-human beings.[15][19]

The philosophical foundations of multispecies justice draw on several distinct but complementary traditions.[15][19][22] Some versions ground multispecies justice in biocentric approaches that recognize all living organisms as having intrinsic value—value that inheres in the organism itself rather than being conferred by its utility to humans.[14][58] From this perspective, any living organism has moral standing in its own right; what matters ethically is not only whether a being can experience suffering, but whether it has its own good—its own way of flourishing or failing to flourish—that is independent of human purposes.[14][58] Other versions of multispecies justice emphasize relationality and interdependence: justice from this perspective involves recognizing and honoring the complex networks of relationship within which all beings exist, and attending to how decisions about one being affect others within those networks.[15][19][49] This relational approach suggests that justice requires not merely restraining humans from harming other species, but actively working to cultivate relationships of reciprocity, care, and mutual flourishing across species boundaries.

Still other formulations of multispecies justice ground themselves in the concept of vulnerability—the recognition that all living beings, including humans, depend upon others for survival, well-being, and flourishing, and are thus vulnerable to harm and loss.[56][59] From this perspective, the task of justice is to attend to the specific forms of vulnerability that characterize different beings and communities, and to create social and institutional arrangements that protect and scaffold the capacity for flourishing of those whose vulnerability puts them at risk.[56] This vulnerability-centered approach to justice offers important insights for genome editing ethics, because it highlights that all beings affected by genome editing technologies—including future generations, non-human organisms whose genomes might be edited, and ecosystems into which gene drive organisms might be released—face heightened vulnerability to unintended consequences of technological interventions they did not choose and for which they cannot provide informed consent.

A crucial aspect of multispecies justice frameworks is their emphasis on epistemic justice—the recognition that different knowledge systems, ways of knowing, and interpretations of what matters ethically deserve equal consideration in deliberative processes, particularly when decisions affect those who hold different epistemologies.[15][19][54] This dimension of multispecies justice is essential for addressing genome editing ethics because it challenges the dominance of Western scientific frameworks in shaping ethical guidance and insists that Indigenous knowledge systems, local ecological knowledge, feminist and disability-centered perspectives, and other ways of understanding relationships between humans and the more-than-human world must be genuinely incorporated into discussions about which genome editing applications are ethically permissible.[20][23][51][54]

## Genome Editing and Non-Human Animal Ethics: Beyond Instrumentalism

The application of genome editing technologies to non-human animals raises particularly acute tensions with anthropocentric ethical frameworks, because it forces explicit confrontation with questions about what we owe to other species and whether their interests merit protection independent of human benefit.[2][5][7][13][31][38][46] Current animal welfare regulations, even in relatively progressive jurisdictions, treat animals primarily as subjects whose capacity to experience pain and distress warrants some protection, but who remain fundamentally subject to human purposes and control.[2][13] From this instrumental perspective, an animal's primary moral significance derives from its ability to suffer; the ethical question becomes whether genome editing causes or alleviates suffering, rather than whether such editing respects the animal's own nature, autonomy, or intrinsic value.

However, the creation of genetically altered non-human primates for biomedical research exemplifies the inadequacy of instrumentalist frameworks and the urgency of developing multispecies justice approaches.[2][7] Researchers in the United States, China, and Japan are using genome editing technologies to create non-human primates carrying genetic modifications that model human neurological diseases.[2][7][38] While the stated goal is advancing human understanding of diseases and developing therapeutic interventions, the creation of these animals raises profound ethical questions that extend far beyond whether the animals experience pain during procedures or during their captive lives in laboratory settings. These questions include whether it is ethically defensible to deliberately create animals with genetic conditions that will cause them suffering during their entire lives, simply to benefit human research; whether modifying the genome of a non-human primate violates something fundamental about the animal's dignity or its intrinsic nature; and whether scientists have the right to make decisions on behalf of future generations of primates who will inherit these genetic modifications without their consent.[2][7][13][31]

The concept of animal *telos*—understood as the essence, nature, or proper mode of functioning characteristic of a particular species—has emerged in some ethical discussions as a way to capture what is lost or violated when animals are genetically modified in ways that prevent them from expressing their species-typical behaviors and capacities.[5][13][26][31][38] From this perspective, to edit an animal's genome in ways that fundamentally alter what it means to be that kind of animal—what behaviors, capacities, and relational possibilities characterize members of that species—is to do a wrong that goes beyond merely increasing or decreasing suffering.[5][13][31] When animals are edited to be less sentient, less capable of socializing with their own kind, or unable to migrate or engage in other behaviors central to their species identity, the argument goes, something essential to their dignity and worth is violated, even if individual animals do not experience greater pain as a result.

At the same time, scholars working within multispecies justice frameworks have noted important limitations to *telos*-based arguments, and have suggested alternative approaches to understanding what genome editing of animals might violate.[5][13][26][31] Some have argued that the concept of species essence or telos is itself contested and unstable—that species characteristics are not fixed in nature but emerge through evolutionary and ecological relationships, and that the line between unacceptable modification and acceptable change is difficult to draw with certainty.[5][26][31] Others have suggested that the key ethical issue is not whether an animal's telos is modified, but whether modifying an animal's genetic makeup is consistent with treating it as a being whose own interests and flourishing matter, rather than merely as a tool to serve human purposes.[2][7][13][34] This latter approach shifts the focus from questions about species essence to questions about power and respect: when humans deliberately create animals with genetic modifications that serve human interests, regardless of what those modifications mean for the animal's own flourishing, we are treating the animal instrumentally rather than respecting it as a subject with its own ends and purposes.

The question of whether genome editing in animals might sometimes be justified to reduce animal suffering further complicates ethical analysis and highlights the need for justice frameworks that move beyond simple instrumentalism.[5][31][46] Some scholars have proposed using genome editing to create animals that are less capable of experiencing pain, thereby reducing suffering on industrial farms.[5][31][46] From an instrumentalist perspective focused narrowly on minimizing suffering, creating animals incapable of experiencing pain might seem to reduce animal welfare problems. But from a multispecies justice perspective, this proposal becomes deeply troubling, because it suggests responding to the systematic suffering caused by industrial animal agriculture—which itself violates animals' interests and dignity—by modifying animals themselves rather than transforming the systems that cause suffering in the first place.[5][31][46] Rather than recognizing that industrial farming practices are unjust to animals and working to change those practices, this approach asks us to accept the injustice and modify animals to be less capable of protesting through suffering. Such an approach fails to respect animals' intrinsic value and instead suggests that it is ethically permissible to use genetic technology to adapt animals to unjust conditions rather than changing those conditions.

## Gene Drives, Ecosystems, and Environmental Justice

Gene drive technologies—genetic systems engineered to spread rapidly through wild populations through super-Mendelian inheritance patterns—represent an even more expansive application of genome editing that extends from individual animals to entire ecosystems, and thereby raises particularly complex questions about environmental justice and multispecies ethics.[9][12][32] Gene drives are being developed to control insect vectors of diseases like malaria, eliminate invasive species, or restore endangered populations.[9][12][32] Unlike genome editing of individual animals kept in controlled laboratory or agricultural settings, gene drive applications involve deliberately releasing genetically modified organisms into the environment with the intention that they will spread their genetic modifications throughout wild populations, potentially affecting entire species and ecosystems.[9][12][32]

The ethical issues raised by gene drives extend far beyond whether individual organisms suffer harm, and instead encompass questions about ecological integrity, environmental justice, and the legitimacy of deliberately intervening in ecosystem processes at a global scale.[9][12][32][33] A fundamental problem is that the potential consequences of releasing gene drive organisms into ecosystems remain radically uncertain and potentially irreversible.[9][12][32] Ecosystems are characterized by complexity, emergence, and non-linear relationships; an intervention intended to control a single species might have cascading consequences across multiple trophic levels and across different ecosystems as organisms move and populations migrate.[9][12][32] While gene drive developers discuss theoretical containment strategies, there is presently no reliable way to prevent the geographic spread of organisms carrying active gene drives once they are released into the environment, and there is limited ability to predict or reverse ecological consequences once they manifest.[9][12]

The issue of who gets to decide whether to release gene drive organisms into ecosystems, and whose interests are considered in those decisions, is fundamentally a question of environmental justice.[12][27][51][52] Proposals to use gene drives to control disease vectors like malaria-carrying mosquitoes are often presented as humanitarian interventions that will save lives in the Global South where malaria is endemic.[12][32] However, decisions about releasing gene drive organisms have typically been made by scientists and policy makers in wealthy, technologically advanced nations, with limited meaningful participation from communities in areas where organisms would be released or ecosystems that would be affected.[12][27][51][52] This pattern replicates colonial relationships in which decisions about land, resources, and ecological futures are made by external actors without genuine consent or meaningful participation from those most directly affected.[12][27][51][52]

Moreover, gene drive applications raise profound environmental justice issues around who bears risks and who receives benefits.[12][27][51][52] If a gene drive organism has unintended ecological consequences, it is often those living closest to affected ecosystems—frequently indigenous peoples, poor communities in the Global South, and other historically marginalized populations—who will experience the negative impacts.[12][27][51][52] Meanwhile, the benefits of disease control or invasive species management accrue to distant populations, to the scientific institutions and corporations that develop the technology, and to wealthy nations with interests in global health or conservation.[12][27][51][52] This pattern of risks and benefits distributions reflects and reinforces existing global inequalities and patterns of environmental colonialism.

From a multispecies justice perspective, gene drives raise additional concerns about the moral status and interests of the organisms that would be targeted for elimination or radical alteration.[32][33] Approaches to gene drive ethics that focus solely on human interests—asking whether malaria control justifies releasing modified mosquitoes, or whether eliminating invasive species serves environmental restoration—fail to grapple with the ethical status of the organisms whose populations would be devastated or eliminated.[32][33] While some might argue that the interests of disease vectors or invasive species do not merit significant moral consideration, a genuinely multispecies approach to justice would at minimum require acknowledging that such organisms have their own modes of being and relating to ecosystems, and that deliberately engineering their elimination or radical genetic alteration involves choices with profound significance for what the living world will be like in the future.[32][33]

## Ecological Stewardship and Reciprocity as Justice Principles

Emerging frameworks for genome editing ethics, particularly those developed in response to gene drive research, have begun identifying principles specifically aimed at attending to non-human interests and ecological systems.[9][12][32][33] The principle of **ecological stewardship** recognizes that all humans share responsibility for the long-term health of planetary ecosystems and that interventions into nature should be guided by commitment to minimizing harm to other species and ecological systems and working to restore damage already caused.[9][12][32][33] Unlike anthropocentric frameworks that ask what humans can do to nature while minimizing human suffering or maximizing human benefit, ecological stewardship asks what interventions are consistent with respecting the flourishing of non-human beings and ecosystems as having value in their own right.

The implementation of ecological stewardship as a binding principle for genome editing requires several key commitments.[9][12][32][33] First, it requires rigorous assessment of potential risks to non-target species and ecosystem processes before any genome editing technology is applied or released into the environment, and honest accounting for uncertainty—recognition that many potential consequences cannot be predicted in advance.[9][12][32] Second, it requires that developers of genome editing technologies work proactively to identify, minimize, and justify any adverse effects their work might have on animal and plant life and the natural environment.[9][12][32][33] Third, it requires that ecological impact assessments be informed not only by Western scientific knowledge but by local ecological knowledge held by Indigenous peoples and communities who have long lived in and stewarded the areas where genome editing applications might be deployed.[9][12][32][33][51]

Complementary to ecological stewardship is the principle of **reciprocity**, which offers a distinct approach to reconceptualizing humanity's relationship with non-human nature and the other species with which we share the world.[49][52] Reciprocity, as understood in Indigenous and other non-Western philosophical and spiritual traditions, suggests that the world is characterized by relationships of mutual obligation and interdependence; humans take from nature and other species, and therefore have reciprocal obligations to give back, to care for, and to support the flourishing of other beings and ecosystems.[49][52] This principle stands in stark contrast to frameworks that treat nature as a resource to be exploited, or even to frameworks that merely ask what harm we should avoid causing; instead, reciprocity suggests that justice requires active care and restoration, not merely restraint.

The principle of reciprocity has important implications for how we think about genome editing ethics.[49][52] Rather than asking whether a particular genome editing application is "acceptable" because it meets some threshold for harm or benefit to humans, a reciprocity-based approach asks whether the application is consistent with recognizing our fundamental interdependence with other species and expressing gratitude and responsibility toward them.[49][52] This might mean, for example, that even if gene drive technology could theoretically control malaria-carrying mosquitoes without ecological catastrophe, the application would not be ethically justified if it represented a unidirectional imposition of human will on other beings and ecosystems without genuine consideration of how other beings might flourish, or without efforts to reciprocally support the flourishing of affected ecosystems and communities.[49][52] Alternatively, reciprocity-based approaches might support applications of genome editing directed toward ecological restoration or helping endangered species adapt to climate change, if those applications are carried out in genuine partnership with communities and ecosystems affected, with transparency about risks and limitations, and with commitment to reciprocally supporting the flourishing of the species and ecosystems affected.

## Indigenous Knowledge Systems and Epistemic Justice in Genome Editing Governance

The systematic exclusion of Indigenous peoples and Indigenous knowledge systems from decisions about genome editing ethics and governance represents one of the most significant failures of current frameworks to achieve justice beyond anthropocentrism.[20][23][45][51][54] Indigenous peoples have maintained sophisticated, place-based knowledge systems about relationships between humans, other species, and ecosystems for thousands of years, knowledge that has enabled sustainable management of lands and resources across incredibly diverse biophysical and cultural contexts.[20][23][45][48][49][51][54] Yet when genome editing applications are proposed that would directly affect Indigenous territories, species sacred to Indigenous cultures, or ecosystems in which Indigenous communities hold rights and responsibilities, Indigenous peoples have typically been excluded from meaningful participation in deliberations about whether such applications should proceed.

This exclusion constitutes an epistemic injustice—a failure to recognize Indigenous ways of knowing as legitimate sources of knowledge about nature, ethics, and appropriate relationships between humans and the more-than-human world.[23][45][54] It also represents a broader pattern of colonial appropriation in which Western scientific institutions claim authority over knowledge, resources, and decision-making power on Indigenous lands and regarding Indigenous species, reproducing patterns of domination that have characterized European colonialism for centuries.[23][45][51][54] Indigenous scholars and activists have documented how these patterns extend to genomic research and conservation efforts: Indigenous peoples' biological samples and genetic material are collected by researchers from wealthy institutions without meaningful consent or benefit-sharing; data derived from Indigenous peoples and species found on Indigenous lands is claimed as scientific property and used without acknowledging Indigenous contributions; and decisions about what knowledge matters and how it should be used are made by non-Indigenous experts without consulting Indigenous communities whose territories, species, and futures are directly affected.[23][45][51]

Addressing these epistemic injustices requires fundamental changes to how genome editing governance is structured and conducted.[20][23][45][51][54] First, it requires recognizing that Indigenous peoples should be acknowledged as key stakeholders and decision-makers regarding genome editing applications that affect their territories, species, or cultural practices, not merely as communities to be consulted after decisions have been made by scientists and policy makers.[20][23][45][51][54] Second, it requires operationalizing the principle of **Indigenous data sovereignty**—the right of Indigenous peoples to control data derived from their lands and peoples, including genomic data, and to determine how such data is used, shared, and applied.[23][45] Third, it requires that engagement processes with Indigenous communities be designed in ways that center Indigenous epistemologies, values, and decision-making practices rather than imposing Western frameworks and timelines.

The concept of **free, prior, and informed consent** (FPIC), developed in international Indigenous rights law, offers important guidance for how genuine Indigenous participation in genome editing governance might be structured.[51][52] FPIC requires that before any decision is made to implement a project that would affect Indigenous lands, species, or cultures—such as releasing gene drive organisms into wild ecosystems in Indigenous territories, or using genome editing to modify species sacred to Indigenous cultures—Indigenous peoples must be informed in advance, in their own languages and through culturally appropriate processes, about what is proposed, its potential consequences, and their rights; given adequate time to deliberate; and have the power to say no.[51][52] Importantly, FPIC is not merely about collecting Indigenous input or soliciting approval; it means that Indigenous peoples have the right to veto projects that they determine would violate their rights or values, and that decisions made in consultation with Indigenous communities should be binding on external actors.

Implementing FPIC in genome editing governance requires attention to historical power imbalances and structural inequalities that have characterized relationships between Indigenous communities and scientific institutions.[51][52][54] Many Indigenous communities have experienced research as exploitative and extractive—they have been used as research subjects, their biological samples have been taken and used without consent, and they have received no benefits from scientific knowledge produced using their data or conducted on their lands.[23][45][51] Building trust sufficient for genuine partnership requires not merely changing consultation procedures, but substantive changes to how scientific institutions operate, including acknowledging past harms, centering Indigenous peoples' interests and rights, and creating mechanisms for Indigenous communities to share in decision-making power and economic benefits from biotechnology applications.[23][45][51][54]

Several Indigenous frameworks for understanding ethical relationships with nature and other species have emerged as particularly relevant for developing genome editing ethics that move beyond anthropocentrism. In Aotearoa New Zealand, Māori concepts such as *whakapapa* (genealogy and kinship connections linking humans to all other beings), *mana* (authority and responsibility), *mauri* (life force and vitality), and *kaitiakitanga* (guardianship and stewardship) provide alternatives to Western anthropocentric frameworks.[20] From a Māori perspective, editing a species' genome affects not only the individual organisms or even the species, but the entire genealogical and relational network of which that species is a part; it impacts the *mana* of the species and the responsibilities of those who carry kaitiakitanga for it.[20] These concepts suggest that ethical decisions about genome editing should be made in consultation with those who hold responsibility for particular species, with attention to impacts on genealogical connections and relational networks, and with commitment to preserving or enhancing the *mauri* and vitality of affected beings and ecosystems.

Similarly, Indigenous scholars from other contexts have articulated frameworks grounded in concepts such as the Ubuntu principle (emphasizing interconnection and mutual obligation among all beings) and others that challenge the individualism and human exceptionalism of Western ethics.[1][11][54] These frameworks suggest that justice in genome editing requires attending to how decisions affect not only contemporary humans or individual organisms, but entire communities—understood as multispecies communities extending through time, including ancestors, future generations, and the non-human beings with which humans are in relationship.[11][54] They suggest that justice requires listening to and learning from those whose knowledge systems have sustained relationships with other species and ecosystems over generations, and being willing to accept limitations on what uses of genome editing are ethically acceptable, even if those uses might benefit humans.

## Operationalizing Multispecies Justice: Governance Mechanisms and Deliberative Processes

Translating multispecies justice principles into concrete governance mechanisms and deliberative processes for decisions about genome editing requires attention to several interconnected challenges. First, institutions that currently govern genome editing—scientific review committees, regulatory agencies, policy bodies—were designed to operate within anthropocentric ethical frameworks and to privilege Western scientific expertise.[12][27][51][52][54] Incorporating multispecies justice principles requires not merely adding new considerations to existing processes, but fundamentally restructuring institutions to distribute decision-making power more broadly, validate multiple knowledge systems, and center the interests and voices of those most directly affected by genome editing applications.

A central challenge is determining who should participate in deliberative processes about genome editing, and recognizing that different stakeholders may have fundamentally different relationships to the technologies and different stakes in outcomes.[12][27][51][52] Scientists and policy makers who develop genome editing technologies may see them as tools for advancing knowledge or solving human problems; affected communities and Indigenous peoples may see them as threats to their territories and ways of life; non-human organisms and future generations cannot directly participate in deliberations but are profoundly affected by decisions made.[12][27][51][52] True multispecies justice governance must find ways to incorporate perspectives of those who cannot directly speak, through proxy advocates, through extension of time horizons to consider impacts on future generations, and through recognition that some interests are so fundamental—the capacity of an ecosystem to sustain life, the right of an endangered species to exist—that they cannot be overridden by competing human interests.

Several scholars have argued that **community-based deliberation**, conducted from early stages of technology development and continuing through deployment, is essential for genuinely just governance of genome editing.[12][27][32][51][52] Such deliberation must be designed not to rubber-stamp decisions already made by experts, but to engage communities as genuine deliberators capable of shaping decisions about what applications should be permitted and under what conditions.[12][27][32][51][52] This requires providing adequate time for communities to learn about proposed applications and develop considered positions; ensuring that deliberation is conducted in accessible language and formats; and crucially, ensuring that community deliberation can actually influence decisions—that communities have power to shape outcomes, not merely to express opinions that are then ignored by experts and policy makers.[12][27][32][51][52]

Deliberative processes for genome editing governance must also attend specifically to how power dynamics shape participation and whose voices are heard.[12][27][51][52][54] Historically marginalized groups—Indigenous peoples, poor communities in the Global South, communities of color—have been systematically excluded from discussions about biotechnology and often bear disproportionate risks from technology deployment.[12][27][51][52] Genuine inclusion of these groups requires not merely inviting them to participate in existing deliberative spaces, but transforming those spaces to center their perspectives, values, and ways of knowing, and addressing the historical injustices and ongoing power imbalances that shape relationships between marginalized communities and scientific institutions.[12][27][51][52][54] This might involve deliberative processes conducted in Indigenous territories using Indigenous languages and protocols; it might involve centering questions about environmental justice and equity rather than purely technical questions about how genome editing works; and it might involve acknowledging that some communities may legitimately refuse participation in genome editing deliberations, viewing them as inherently unjust or inconsistent with their values.

## Disability Rights, Eugenics, and the Limits of "Therapeutic" Genome Editing

A crucial dimension of moving beyond anthropocentrism in genome editing ethics involves recognizing how frameworks that appear neutral or technically sound often embody particular value judgments about what kinds of human and non-human existence are worth preserving, and can reinforce existing patterns of marginalization and exclusion.[4][8][53] This issue emerges most starkly in discussions of using germline genome editing to prevent genetic diseases or disabilities. While therapeutic uses of genome editing are often represented as ethically straightforward—preventing suffering by eliminating disease-causing genetic variants—disability rights scholars and activists have raised profound concerns about how such applications might serve eugenic purposes and violate the rights and dignity of people living with disabilities.

The distinction between preventing disease and promoting eugenics is far less clear than proponents of germline editing often acknowledge.[4][8][53] Once heritable genome editing becomes technically feasible, the logic of choice expands: what begins as preventing serious, life-threatening genetic disorders can easily shift toward preventing conditions that cause less severe disability, or toward "enhancement" of traits considered desirable.[4][8][53] Moreover, decisions about which conditions are worth preventing reflect value judgments about what kinds of lives are worth living, and these judgments often reflect ableist assumptions that equate disability with suffering and reduced quality of life, rather than recognizing disability as a form of human difference and variation.[4][8][53] When germline editing becomes available, social pressure may develop on parents to use it, not because the condition being prevented is invariably devastating, but because it becomes possible to "prevent" it, and failure to do so may be seen as negligent.[53] This dynamic can lead to gradual narrowing of what kinds of genetic variation are considered acceptable in human populations, with profound implications for diversity, inclusion, and the flourishing of people with disabilities.

Disability justice frameworks offer crucial resources for developing genome editing ethics that move beyond anthropocentrism precisely because they challenge the assumption that human variation should be minimized and normal functioning maximized.[4][8] Disability justice perspectives recognize that disability is not merely a medical condition, but a social category shaped by how environments and social structures do or do not accommodate different ways of being in the world.[4][8][53] Many disabilities involve real physical or cognitive differences, but whether those differences constitute suffering or limitation depends significantly on social and environmental factors—the availability of accommodations, accessibility, social inclusion, and economic support.[4][8][53] From a disability justice perspective, using genome editing to prevent genetic disabilities reflects and reinforces ableist social arrangements rather than genuinely addressing suffering; it suggests that the solution to disability is to eliminate disabled people rather than to create social environments in which people with disabilities can flourish.

This disability justice critique extends to genome editing of non-human animals as well.[5][31][46] Proposals to create farm animals with reduced capacity to experience pain, for instance, reflect ableist assumptions that limitation of sentience is preferable to social change that would reduce suffering by transforming the conditions under which animals live.[5][31][46] From a multispecies justice perspective, such proposals are doubly problematic: they treat non-human animals as subjects of modification to serve human purposes, and they suggest that accepting limitations on what beings can sense and feel is ethically acceptable rather than fundamentally unjust.

## Environmental Justice and Benefit-Sharing in Agricultural Genome Editing

Applications of genome editing to crops and livestock raise distinct environmental and social justice issues, particularly regarding questions of access, benefit-sharing, and who bears the risks of technological failure or unintended consequences.[11][25][28][44][57] Agricultural genome editing is often presented as a technological solution to food security challenges, climate change adaptation, and pesticide reduction, with potential to increase yields while reducing environmental impacts.[25][30][35][43] However, who bears the costs of development and who receives benefits from these applications are questions fundamentally about justice, and current patterns suggest that benefits are likely to concentrate among wealthy agricultural corporations and farmers in industrialized nations, while risks and costs are borne by small-scale farmers, agricultural workers, and communities in the Global South.

One crucial environmental justice issue involves **access to and control of genome-edited seeds and agricultural technologies**.[11][25][28][44][57] Patent systems globally have been structured to concentrate control of agricultural biotechnology in the hands of large corporations, particularly in the Global North.[11][25][28][44] Genome editing technologies are expensive to develop; companies that invest in developing edited crops or livestock therefore seek to recoup costs through patents that give them exclusive control over the use of edited organisms.[11][25][28] This means that small-scale farmers, particularly in the Global South, may be unable to access benefits of genome editing applications unless they can afford licensing fees, and may face legal penalties for saving and replanting seeds, a practice that has sustained food security for millennia.[11][25][28][44][57] This pattern perpetuates global inequalities and undermines food sovereignty—the right of peoples and communities to determine their own agricultural and food systems.[11][25][28]

A second environmental justice concern involves the **distribution of environmental risks and benefits** from agricultural genome editing.[25][28][57] If genome editing is used to create crops more suitable for industrial monoculture agriculture, it may accelerate practices that reduce biodiversity, increase chemical inputs, and degrade soil health—practices that have significant environmental impacts, particularly in regions where such agriculture is concentrated.[25][28][30] Meanwhile, benefits of increased productivity concentrate among large-scale farmers and agribusiness corporations, while environmental harms are borne by the broader public, by ecosystems, and by future generations.[25][28][57] From an environmental justice perspective, this distribution of risks and benefits is deeply inequitable: those who benefit most from agricultural intensification bear least of the environmental costs, while communities and ecosystems bearing environmental risks receive few benefits.

A third dimension of environmental justice in agricultural genome editing involves questions of **epistemic justice and whose knowledge about sustainable agriculture is valued**.[25][28][54][57] Industrial agriculture, supported by biotechnology companies, is often presented as the most scientifically advanced and sustainable approach to food production, while traditional and Indigenous agricultural practices are dismissed as inefficient or outdated.[25][28][54][57] However, research has consistently shown that traditional and Indigenous agricultural practices, including crop diversification, agroforestry, and other landscape-level management approaches, often produce substantial food with lower environmental impact than industrial monoculture.[25][28][54][57] From an environmental justice perspective, genuine sustainability requires not merely developing new technologies, but valuing and supporting diverse agricultural knowledge systems, particularly those developed by Indigenous peoples and small-scale farmers who have sustained food production and environmental health across generations.[25][28][54][57]

Addressing environmental justice issues in agricultural genome editing requires several key changes to current governance approaches.[11][25][28][44] First, it requires ensuring that small-scale farmers and agricultural workers—those most directly affected by agricultural innovation—have meaningful participation in decisions about what agricultural applications of genome editing are developed and how they are deployed.[11][25][28] Second, it requires rethinking intellectual property systems for agricultural biotechnology to ensure that benefits of genome editing innovations can be accessed by small-scale farmers, particularly in the Global South, rather than being monopolized by wealthy corporations.[11][25][28][44] Third, it requires valuing and supporting diverse agricultural knowledge systems and practices, rather than assuming that industrial biotechnology represents the only or best path to sustainable agriculture.[25][28][54][57] Fourth, it requires conducting genuine environmental impact assessments that evaluate long-term, landscape-level impacts of agricultural genome editing, not merely narrow measures of crop yield or chemical inputs.

## Toward Justice: Synthesizing Multiple Frameworks and Operationalizing Change

Developing genome editing ethics that genuinely moves beyond anthropocentrism requires bringing together insights from multiple frameworks—multispecies justice, disability justice, environmental justice, Indigenous epistemologies, relational ethics, and vulnerability-centered approaches—and translating these insights into concrete institutions and practices that shape how genome editing is governed and deployed.[1][3][4][8][11][15][19][22][33][47][49][54] None of these frameworks alone is sufficient; each illuminates particular dimensions of justice and ethical obligation that a comprehensive approach must address.

A synthesized justice framework for genome editing ethics must rest on several foundational principles and commitments. First, it must **recognize the intrinsic value of non-human beings and ecosystems**, acknowledging that other species and ecological systems have worth and standing that does not derive from their utility to humans.[3][14][15][19][22][58] This does not mean that human interests never take priority, but it does mean that non-human interests must be genuinely considered and weighed against human interests, not simply dismissed because they are non-human. Second, it must **center relationships and interdependence** rather than treating individual humans or animals as isolated units; justice requires attending to how decisions about genome editing affect the webs of relationship that constitute ecosystems and human-nature relationships.[15][19][49][52] Third, it must **prioritize the voices and knowledge of those most directly affected**, particularly Indigenous peoples and historically marginalized communities whose perspectives have been systematically excluded from decision-making about biotechnology.[20][23][45][51][52][54]

Fourth, the framework must **attend explicitly to power and structural inequality**, recognizing that genome editing technologies are not merely technical tools but are embedded in existing systems of domination and exploitation.[8][11][12][27][44][51][52] Technologies can either challenge or reinforce existing patterns of inequality; justice frameworks must explicitly ask who controls genome editing technologies, who benefits, who bears risks, and whether particular applications serve to entrench or challenge existing injustices.[8][11][12][27][44] Fifth, the framework must **expand the temporal scope of justice** to include future generations—both human and non-human—who will inherit the world shaped by decisions made today about genome editing.[33][50][53] Concerns about unknown and unpredictable consequences of heritable genome editing, about ecosystem impacts of gene drives, or about social consequences of genetic stratification, are fundamentally about intergenerational justice.[33][53]

Operationalizing these principles requires **institution-building and procedural change** at multiple levels—from how research is conducted, to how regulatory decisions are made, to how international governance of biotechnology is structured.[12][27][32][44][51][52][54] At the institutional level, this requires ensuring that bodies responsible for governing genome editing include not merely scientists and experts, but also representatives of Indigenous communities, disability justice advocates, environmental justice activists, ethicists with diverse perspectives, and community members from populations likely to be affected by genome editing applications.[12][27][32][51][52] It requires changing how expertise is defined and valued—recognizing that Indigenous knowledge, community knowledge, and knowledge emerging from lived experience of marginalization are legitimate forms of expertise that should shape decisions.[12][27][51][52][54]

At the procedural level, operationalizing multispecies justice in genome editing governance requires establishing requirements for **early, meaningful, and genuinely deliberative engagement** with affected communities before decisions are made about whether particular applications should proceed.[12][27][32][51][52] Such engagement must operate from principles of reciprocity and respect: communities are not merely consulted for their input, but genuinely included as deliberators with power to shape outcomes.[12][27][32][51][52] Engagement must provide adequate time and resources for communities to understand proposed applications, to develop considered positions, and to participate in ongoing decisions as new information emerges.[12][27][32] It must be conducted in accessible formats and languages, and it must explicitly address power imbalances and historical injustices that have shaped relationships between scientific institutions and marginalized communities.[12][27][51][52][54]

At the policy and regulatory level, moving beyond anthropocentrism in genome editing ethics requires establishing clear **ethical boundaries and prohibitions** on applications that violate multispecies justice principles, even if such applications might benefit humans or advance scientific knowledge.[3][9][12][32][44] These might include prohibitions on applications that appear designed to adapt animals to unjust conditions rather than transforming the conditions; prohibitions on heritable germline editing for purposes other than preventing serious disease (with clear definitions of what counts as serious, developed in genuine partnership with disabled people and their communities); prohibitions on release of gene drive organisms without genuine free, prior, and informed consent from affected Indigenous communities and substantive environmental impact assessment.[9][12][32][44][53] Such boundaries are necessary precisely because once technologies become technically feasible, there are powerful economic and professional incentives to develop and deploy them, regardless of ethical concerns.

## Toward a Truly Just Future: Decolonization and Solidarity

The project of developing genome editing ethics that moves beyond anthropocentrism is ultimately inseparable from the broader project of decolonization—undoing the epistemic, political, and economic injustices that have flowed from European colonialism and that continue to structure relationships between the Global North and Global South, between dominant and marginalized communities, and between humans and the more-than-human world.[51][54] Colonialism has been founded on the assumption that some humans have the right to control and exploit the lands, resources, and peoples of others; that Western knowledge systems are superior to other ways of knowing; and that nature exists primarily as a resource to be exploited for economic benefit.[51][54] These same assumptions underlie many contemporary uses of genome editing technology—that scientists and technologically advanced nations have the right to modify other species and ecosystems; that scientific expertise is the only legitimate basis for decisions about biotechnology; and that the question of what uses of genome editing are permissible should be determined primarily by what is technically feasible and what benefits humans.

Decolonizing genome editing ethics means rejecting these assumptions and building instead on the foundations of genuine partnership, respect for diverse knowledge systems, recognition of Indigenous sovereignty and rights, and commitment to justice that extends across species boundaries and through time.[51][54] This is not a project that can be completed through modest reforms to existing institutions; it requires fundamental transformation of how scientific research is conducted, how decisions are made about technology development and deployment, and how humans understand their relationship to and responsibilities toward other species and ecosystems.[51][54] It requires not merely including Indigenous peoples and other marginalized groups in existing decision-making structures, but transforming those structures themselves to operate from different principles and values.

Ultimately, developing genome editing ethics that moves beyond anthropocentrism is a project rooted in solidarity—in the recognition that justice for humans is inseparable from justice for other species and ecosystems, that the flourishing of human communities depends upon the flourishing of the more-than-human worlds of which humans are part.[15][19][49][54][55][60] It requires rejecting the premise that human interests are naturally positioned at the center of ethical concern, and instead embracing the understanding that justice requires attending to the interests and flourishing of all beings whose lives are affected by our choices. Genome editing technologies have immense power to reshape living worlds; ensuring that this power is exercised justly requires building institutions, practices, and ethical frameworks grounded in multispecies solidarity and commitment to honoring the intrinsic value and agency of all beings.

## Conclusion

The ethics of genome editing, as currently constituted in scientific and policy institutions globally, remains fundamentally anthropocentric—structured around assumptions that human beings occupy a privileged moral position, that human interests take precedence in decisions about whether and how to edit genomes, and that other species matter primarily insofar as they serve human purposes or cause humans harm.[1][3][4] This anthropocentric framing is inadequate to the task of developing genuinely just frameworks for decisions about genome editing technologies, which have the power to reshape not only individual organisms but entire species, ecosystems, and the multispecies relationships that constitute the living world.[2][5][7][9][12][15][19]

Rebuilding justice foundations in genome editing ethics beyond anthropocentrism requires embracing emerging frameworks of multispecies justice, which recognize that non-human beings and ecosystems have intrinsic value and that their interests merit recognition and protection in decisions that affect them.[15][19][22] It requires operationalizing principles of ecological stewardship and reciprocity—recognizing that humans have obligations not merely to avoid harming other species, but to actively support their flourishing and to reciprocate the benefits we receive from ecosystem relationships.[9][12][32][33][49] It requires centering the perspectives, knowledge systems, and decision-making authority of Indigenous peoples and historically marginalized communities whose territories and species are most directly affected by genome editing applications, and acknowledging the epistemic injustices that have flowed from exclusion of non-Western knowledge from scientific and ethical deliberations.[20][23][45][51][54]

Operationalizing these principles requires substantial transformation of institutions that govern genome editing—restructuring regulatory bodies, research ethics committees, and policy-making processes to include diverse stakeholders with genuine power to shape decisions; ensuring that deliberative processes about genome editing applications are designed to center affected communities and draw on multiple knowledge systems; establishing clear ethical boundaries and prohibitions on applications that violate multispecies justice principles; and creating accountability mechanisms to ensure that commitments to justice are not merely rhetorical but are genuinely enacted in practice.[12][27][32][44][51][52][54]

The stakes of this work are profound. Genome editing technologies have emerged at a moment of ecological crisis—a moment when human activity is driving rapid biodiversity loss, ecosystem degradation, and climate change on an unprecedented scale.[33][49] How humans choose to use genome editing—whether to intensify existing patterns of domination and exploitation of other species and ecosystems, or to develop applications grounded in genuine respect for non-human beings and commitment to ecological restoration and justice—will significantly shape the world that emerges in coming decades and centuries.[33][49] Developing genome editing ethics that moves beyond anthropocentrism is not a peripheral concern for academic philosophers, but a central question for how humanity will navigate its relationship to the living world in an era of ecological crisis and technological power. The project of rebuilding justice foundations for genome editing offers an opportunity to fundamentally reconsider humanity's place in the natural world, to honor the intrinsic value of all beings, and to work toward a future in which technological power is wielded in service of genuine justice across the boundaries of species and toward the flourishing of all life.
