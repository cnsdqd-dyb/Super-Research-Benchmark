# Decision Priorities in Autonomous Weapon Systems: Frameworks for Civilian Protection

Autonomous weapon systems represent a fundamental shift in military operations, yet the mechanisms by which these systems establish decision priorities to protect civilian life remain contested, complex, and inadequately resolved in existing international legal frameworks. The central challenge lies in translating abstract principles of humanitarian law into operational protocols that autonomous systems can execute while maintaining the human judgment deemed essential for protecting civilians. Current approaches attempt to embed civilian protection priorities through multiple layered mechanisms including meaningful human control requirements, rules of engagement specifications, international humanitarian law compliance frameworks, and technical design constraints. However, the effectiveness of these mechanisms remains uncertain, as autonomous systems lack the contextual understanding, moral reasoning capacity, and adaptive judgment that international humanitarian law fundamentally demands for lawful civilian protection in armed conflict.

## The Foundation: International Humanitarian Law as the Basis for Civilian Protection Priorities

International humanitarian law establishes the bedrock upon which all decision priorities in autonomous weapon systems must theoretically be constructed. The principles of distinction and proportionality form the cornerstone of civilian protection, requiring military forces to differentiate between combatants and civilians and to ensure that expected civilian harm does not exceed anticipated military advantage.[9][31] These principles demand that commanders making targeting decisions possess comprehensive understanding of the operational environment, including the presence and location of civilians, the nature of military objectives, and the relationship between military advantage and civilian harm.[22][25] The principle of precautions in attack further obligates military forces to take all feasible measures to verify targets, minimize incidental civilian harm, and cancel or suspend attacks when the target status becomes uncertain.[4][31]

The ICRC has emphasized that these fundamental IHL obligations impose specific requirements on those who plan and decide upon attacks, creating what amounts to a legal framework that mandates human decision-making at critical junctures.[22] The principles of distinction and proportionality require distinctly human judgment because they involve not merely technical calculations but normative assessments of military necessity, contextual interpretation of battlefield situations, and moral evaluations of the relationship between military goals and civilian suffering. An attack that technically satisfies the principle of distinction by targeting a military objective might still violate the principle of proportionality if the expected civilian harm would be excessive relative to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.[12][37] This proportionality determination requires weighing incommensurable values and making judgments about acceptable levels of human suffering in pursuit of military objectives—determinations that existing autonomous systems cannot replicate through algorithmic decision-making.

The legal framework also includes the Martens Clause, which provides that civilians and combatants remain protected by the principles of humanity and dictates of the public conscience in situations not explicitly covered by treaty provisions.[22][27] This principle creates an ethical dimension to civilian protection that transcends specific legal rules and demands that military operations reflect humanitarian values. For autonomous systems, the invocation of the Martens Clause is particularly significant because it establishes that mere technical compliance with specified legal rules may be insufficient if the operation violates broader humanitarian principles. An autonomous system might technically comply with stated rules governing distinction and proportionality yet still violate the fundamental humanitarian imperative underlying international humanitarian law.

## Meaningful Human Control: The Central Mechanism for Establishing Civilian Protection Priorities

Meaningful human control has emerged as the primary mechanism through which civilian protection priorities are intended to be maintained in autonomous weapon systems, though considerable debate persists regarding what constitutes "meaningful" control and how it can be operationalized. The concept, which originated in policy discussions and has now become central to international negotiations, seeks to ensure that human judgment remains engaged in decisions about the use of force, particularly in targeting determinations that directly affect civilian safety.[1][7][10] Meaningful human control operates on the premise that certain decisions involving the application of lethal force cannot be delegated to autonomous systems without fundamentally compromising the legal and ethical requirements of international humanitarian law.

The substantive components of meaningful human control, as elaborated in policy discussions and expert analyses, include several critical elements that directly bear on civilian protection priorities.[4][7][10] First, operators and commanders must possess sufficient knowledge of how autonomous systems function, including their capabilities, limitations, and potential failure modes, to exercise informed judgment about their deployment and use. This knowledge requirement extends beyond technical understanding to encompass situational awareness of the operational environment, including comprehensive information about the presence, location, and movement of civilians and civilian objects. Second, humans must retain the ability to exercise judgment to the extent required by international humanitarian law, which means they must be capable of assessing whether targeting decisions comply with the principles of distinction and proportionality in the specific circumstances of each attack. Third, operators must maintain the ability to limit the types of tasks and targets engaged by autonomous systems, to impose geographical and temporal restrictions on system operations, and to intervene to override or terminate autonomous actions when circumstances change or when compliance with law of war obligations becomes questionable.

The practical implementation of meaningful human control encounters substantial obstacles, however, because autonomous systems by their nature reduce or eliminate human involvement in critical operational phases.[1][6][10] When autonomous systems select targets based on sensor inputs and preprogrammed target profiles without requiring human authorization for individual engagements, the human role shifts from active decision-making to passive monitoring or supervisory oversight. This shift fundamentally alters the nature of human engagement with targeting decisions and raises serious questions about whether humans can exercise meaningful control when they lack real-time information about the system's decision processes and when the speed of autonomous operation prevents practical human intervention.[3][6] The faster an autonomous system operates, the less opportunity exists for humans to understand its reasoning, question its conclusions, or intervene to prevent civilian harm.

Automation bias—the tendency of human operators to defer excessively to automated system recommendations without critical evaluation—compounds the challenges of meaningful human control.[6][18] Studies from other domains such as healthcare have demonstrated that experienced professionals often fail to critically challenge algorithmic outputs, particularly in time-critical situations where rapid decisions are required. In military contexts where targeting decisions must be made quickly to respond to battlefield developments, the risk that human operators will reflexively approve autonomous system recommendations without meaningful deliberation is substantial. This psychological phenomenon threatens to reduce meaningful human control from a substantive engagement with targeting decisions to a ritualistic approval process that merely creates an appearance of human decision-making authority while actual decision-making remains algorithmic.

## Rules of Engagement as Operational Frameworks for Civilian Protection

Rules of engagement represent an important mechanism through which civilian protection priorities can be operationalized within the broader framework of autonomous weapons deployment. Rules of engagement are a mix of military and political policy requirements bounded by international and domestic legal parameters that authorize and limit the use of force in specific operational contexts.[19] They provide detailed instructions to military personnel about when force may be used, against what targets, using what means, and under what constraints, thereby translating abstract legal principles into concrete operational guidance. For autonomous weapon systems, rules of engagement can potentially specify the conditions under which autonomous functions may operate, the targets they may engage, geographical and temporal limitations on their use, and the circumstances under which human authorization must be obtained before engagement.[19]

An advantage of using rules of engagement to govern autonomous weapons is that they can be specifically tailored to particular missions and operational environments, allowing commanders to impose constraints that reflect the unique humanitarian and military considerations of each situation.[19] Rules of engagement can specify that autonomous systems must not operate in areas with high concentrations of civilians, that they may only engage targets identified through multiple independent sensors, that they must maintain supervisory oversight by human operators at all times, or that they must provide warnings before engaging targets in civilian areas. They can also define escape clauses and exceptions that apply when circumstances change or when unanticipated situations arise. This flexibility allows rules of engagement to adapt to the reality that no autonomous system can be designed to handle all possible scenarios that might arise during military operations.

However, the effectiveness of rules of engagement in protecting civilians depends heavily on the capacity of autonomous systems to actually comply with the operational constraints specified. If an autonomous system cannot reliably execute the constraints established in rules of engagement—for instance, if it cannot accurately detect civilians in complex urban environments or cannot process the contextual information necessary to determine whether a target meets the criteria for lawful engagement—then rules of engagement become mere aspirational statements rather than enforceable constraints on autonomous action. The challenge of ensuring that autonomous systems can actually comply with rules of engagement is therefore a central concern in debates about civilian protection. Moreover, rules of engagement themselves require interpretation and judgment in their application; an autonomous system must not only receive rules of engagement but must understand and apply them contextually in specific situations, a capability that current systems demonstrably lack.

## International Humanitarian Law Principles: Distinction and Proportionality as Decision Constraints

The principles of distinction and proportionality, while established as fundamental obligations under international humanitarian law, present immense challenges for autonomous system decision-making and remain central to debates about whether autonomous weapons can ever be programmed to protect civilians adequately.[2][4][25] The principle of distinction requires military forces to distinguish at all times between military objectives and civilians, directing attacks only at military objectives and refraining from attacks on civilians and civilian objects.[4][9][31] This principle appears deceptively straightforward until one confronts the actual complexity of making distinctions in modern warfare, where combatants often operate within civilian populations, where military objectives may be located adjacent to civilian areas, and where distinguishing between combatants and civilians requires interpretation of human behavior, clothing, equipment, and context that defies algorithmic classification.

Autonomous systems attempting to operationalize the principle of distinction must make target identification determinations based on sensor inputs—video, radar, thermal imagery, and other signals—combined with algorithms trained on datasets of combatants and civilians. However, the quality and comprehensiveness of training data available for military applications is often limited, drawn from specific conflicts or operational contexts that may not generalize to other environments.[15][18] This limitation creates substantial risk that autonomous systems will misidentify persons or objects as lawful military targets when they are in fact civilians. The risk intensifies when one considers the possibility of algorithmic bias, where training data contains implicit biases that cause systems to disproportionately misidentify individuals from particular demographic groups as combatants.[15][18] A system trained predominantly on images of young adult males might systematically fail to recognize civilian women, children, or elderly persons, or a system trained in one geographic region might perform poorly when deployed in a different environment with different architecture, vegetation, or climate.

The principle of proportionality requires that attacks be evaluated to determine whether expected civilian harm would be excessive in relation to the anticipated military advantage.[12][31][37] This principle explicitly demands human judgment comparing incommensurable values: the civilian casualties and damage likely to result from an attack versus the military advantage expected to flow from destroying or degrading a military objective. Proportionality assessments require understanding not merely the direct effects of an attack but also the secondary and cascading effects, including how destruction of particular targets might affect access to food, water, medical care, or shelter for civilian populations. An autonomous system would need to make probabilistic estimates of civilian harm, assess military advantage in concrete terms, and compare these estimates according to legal standards that are themselves somewhat indeterminate. International humanitarian law provides no mathematical formula or precise threshold for determining when civilian harm becomes excessive; the assessment must be made contextually by decision-makers applying their judgment to the specific circumstances of each prospective attack.

Recent scholarship and policy discussions have acknowledged that autonomous systems are unlikely to develop capabilities adequate to conduct proportionality assessments according to the legal standards required.[2][4][25] The problem is not merely technical but fundamentally epistemological: proportionality determinations require weighing values that cannot be reduced to numerical equivalents, assessing military advantage that must be understood in relation to overall campaign objectives and long-term strategic considerations, and making judgments about acceptable levels of civilian suffering that depend on normative commitments rather than empirical facts. An autonomous system might be programmed with rules approximating proportionality analysis, but such rules would necessarily oversimplify the determination and risk either being too permissive (allowing attacks with excessive civilian harm) or too restrictive (prohibiting militarily necessary attacks).

## Technological Design Mechanisms for Embedding Civilian Protection

At the technical level, several design mechanisms attempt to embed civilian protection priorities into autonomous weapon systems, though the adequacy of these mechanisms remains hotly contested. Decision support systems, which use artificial intelligence and data analysis to inform human decision-makers about targeting possibilities rather than making targeting decisions autonomously, represent one approach to maintaining human control while leveraging technology to improve decision quality.[6][14][17] These systems can analyze large datasets to identify potential targets, assess the presence of civilians in target areas using open-source information and sensor data, and flag factors relevant to proportionality determinations, leaving the final targeting decision to human operators. The theory underlying decision support systems is that technology can enhance human decision-making by providing more comprehensive information and analytical capabilities while preserving human judgment as the ultimate arbiter of targeting decisions.

However, decision support systems present their own challenges for civilian protection.[5][6] When these systems present their recommendations to human operators, operators may exhibit automation bias by over-relying on system recommendations without adequately scrutinizing the underlying data or reasoning.[6][18] Additionally, the speed at which decision support systems can generate recommendations creates pressure to act quickly, potentially compressing the time available for thorough human deliberation about civilian protection implications. The more capable and user-friendly these systems become, the more powerful the cognitive pressure they create on human operators to accept their recommendations, and thus the more substantial becomes the risk that human judgment will become subordinated to algorithmic analysis.

At the design stage, attempts to embed civilian protection priorities include requirements for transparency, auditability, and explainability in autonomous system decision-making.[35][44] These requirements seek to ensure that the reasoning processes underlying autonomous targeting decisions can be examined and understood by human operators, commanders, and legal reviewers, making it possible to assess whether decisions comply with international humanitarian law and enabling accountability mechanisms if violations occur. Transparency requirements might mandate that autonomous systems provide information about what data inputs were considered, what algorithmic processes were applied, and what factors led to particular targeting conclusions. Auditability requirements ensure that complete records are maintained of autonomous system decisions, including supporting data and reasoning, facilitating investigation of civilian casualties and determination of accountability.

Explainability presents particular challenges for autonomous systems that employ machine learning algorithms, which often function as "black boxes" where the relationship between inputs and outputs cannot be readily explained even by the engineers who designed the systems.[39][56] A neural network trained through machine learning might achieve high accuracy in target classification while remaining opaque regarding why particular classifications were made. Current explainability techniques such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) provide methods for post-hoc explanation of machine learning model outputs, but these techniques have limitations and may not fully resolve the explainability challenge for complex systems in high-stakes targeting contexts.

## Testing, Verification, and Validation: Establishing Confidence in Civilian Protection Capabilities

A critical mechanism for establishing civilian protection priorities in autonomous weapon systems is rigorous testing, verification, and validation throughout the system lifecycle to ensure that autonomous systems can actually comply with international humanitarian law in realistic operational conditions. The U.S. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09 and subsequent guidance documents emphasize that autonomous systems must demonstrate through developmental testing and operational testing that they can reliably perform their intended functions within specified parameters and constraints, and that they will not engage in unintended engagements that could harm civilians.[8][35][55] Verification involves confirming that autonomous systems do what they were designed to do, while validation involves assessing whether the system design actually achieves the intended operational objectives while meeting legal obligations.

Testing autonomous systems to establish confidence in civilian protection capabilities must address both routine scenarios and edge cases where systems operate at the boundaries of their designed capabilities or where unanticipated circumstances arise.[38][39][55] A system that performs reliably in controlled testing scenarios might behave unpredictably in actual operational environments with weather conditions, electromagnetic interference, or tactical situations not represented in training data. Testing must therefore simulate realistic operational environments and must include scenarios involving civilian presence and movement to assess whether systems correctly distinguish civilians from combatants and refrain from engaging civilian targets. However, testing autonomous systems raises profound practical challenges because the space of possible situations that might arise during military operations is vast, and testing cannot feasibly cover all possible scenarios.

The SIPRI report on autonomous weapon systems and international humanitarian law notes that testing for IHL compliance presents unique challenges because the principles of distinction and proportionality require contextual judgment that is difficult to specify in advance and even more difficult to verify through testing.[41] How can one test whether an autonomous system makes proportionality determinations correctly when proportionality assessments involve subjective judgments about military advantage and acceptable levels of civilian harm? Testing procedures might establish that systems meet specified technical performance standards—achieving a certain accuracy rate in target identification, for example—but such standards may not translate directly into compliance with the normative standards demanded by international humanitarian law.

Another significant testing challenge involves what might be called predictability requirements, which demand that commanders and operators maintain reasonable confidence that autonomous systems will operate as intended in the circumstances where they will actually be deployed.[38] Predictability is particularly difficult to establish for systems employing machine learning, which can adapt their behavior in response to new data and changing environments in ways that may be difficult to anticipate. A machine learning system that "learns" from its operational experience by refining its algorithms might develop unexpected behaviors that were not evident during testing. The greater the autonomy and adaptive capacity of a system, the greater the challenge in predicting its behavior reliably and maintaining the confidence necessary to comply with legal obligations to take all feasible precautions to protect civilians.

## Article 36 Weapon Reviews: Legal Assessment of Civilian Protection Capabilities

International humanitarian law establishes through Article 36 of Additional Protocol I a requirement that states conduct legal reviews of all new weapons, means, and methods of warfare before development and deployment to determine whether they comply with international law.[57][60] For autonomous weapon systems, Article 36 reviews should assess whether systems can reliably comply with the principles of distinction and proportionality, can maintain predictability and reliability in realistic operational conditions, and can operate within meaningful human control frameworks. The ICRC guidance on Article 36 reviews emphasizes that reviews must examine not merely the technical capabilities of weapon systems but also the doctrine, training, and operational procedures for their use to assess whether the complete package of technical system design, operational concepts, and human factors would permit lawful use.[60]

Article 36 reviews of autonomous weapon systems should specifically address civilian protection capabilities, assessing whether systems can distinguish civilians from combatants with sufficient reliability, whether they can respect geographical and temporal limitations on their use, whether they can be deactivated if circumstances change, and whether operators and commanders can exercise meaningful oversight of system operations.[22] However, Article 36 reviews face challenges in assessing systems that employ machine learning or other adaptive technologies, whose behavior may change over time and whose performance in novel situations cannot be fully predicted in advance. Additionally, Article 36 reviews depend on the willingness of military and political leaders to actually implement the reviews rigorously and to refrain from deploying systems that cannot be certified as compliant with international humanitarian law, and evidence suggests that such willingness is not uniform across states.

## The Accountability Challenge: Who Bears Responsibility for Civilian Harm?

A critical issue complicating the establishment of civilian protection priorities in autonomous weapon systems is the accountability gap, which refers to the difficulty of assigning criminal responsibility for war crimes or violations of international humanitarian law when autonomous systems make independent targeting decisions.[2][5][13][16][42] International humanitarian law depends fundamentally on the principle that identifiable human actors can be held accountable for violations, either through criminal prosecution or other accountability mechanisms. However, when autonomous systems make targeting decisions based on complex algorithmic processes involving contributions from programmers, system designers, operators, commanders, and other actors in a distributed decision-making chain, assigning responsibility becomes extraordinarily difficult.

If an autonomous system targets and kills civilians, determining who bears legal responsibility involves tracing the decision-making process backward through multiple actors and layers. Did the programmer write code that was flawed? Did the system designer fail to test the system adequately? Did the operator fail to monitor the system or to recognize signs of malfunction? Did the commander fail to understand the system's limitations or to deploy it appropriately given the operational environment? Did military leadership fail to establish adequate rules of engagement or meaningful human control requirements? Each of these individuals could plausibly claim that their role was not directly responsible for the targeting decision, with responsibility for the ultimate outcome diffused across the entire chain.[5] This diffusion of responsibility creates what scholars describe as an "accountability gap," where harmful outcomes occur but no individual can be held accountable because each person in the decision-making chain can argue that they did not directly cause the harm and that responsibility lies elsewhere.

The accountability gap has profound implications for civilian protection priorities because it undermines the deterrent and preventive functions that accountability mechanisms are supposed to serve. If military personnel involved in autonomous weapons development and deployment believe that accountability for civilian harm will be difficult to assign, they may have reduced incentives to implement robust safeguards protecting civilians. Conversely, if accountability cannot be clearly assigned and individual perpetrators identified, the accountability mechanisms that exist under international criminal law become practically ineffective, leaving victims without meaningful recourse and perpetrators potentially escaping responsibility.

Recent scholarship and expert assessments have proposed that commanders who order the deployment of autonomous systems should bear primary accountability for the systems' actions, on the theory that commanders retain overall responsibility for ensuring their forces comply with international humanitarian law regardless of the means and methods used.[13][42] Under this approach, if an autonomous system causes civilian harm, the commanding officer who deployed the system would be held responsible for the violation, even if the system's behavior was not directly foreseeable. This approach preserves the principle of accountability by anchoring responsibility to an identifiable human decision-maker, but it creates the perverse incentive that commanders facing substantial personal accountability risk might avoid deploying autonomous systems even when they could reliably protect civilians better than alternative means of warfare.

## Civilian Casualties and AI-Enabled Targeting: The Role of Decision Support Systems

Contemporary military practice increasingly employs AI-enabled decision support systems that assist human targeting personnel in analyzing information relevant to targeting decisions while preserving human authority over engagement decisions. These systems, such as the U.S. Project Maven and Palantir's targeting tools, represent an intermediate approach between fully autonomous weapons and purely human targeting, where technology augments human decision-making without replacing it.[14][17] Decision support systems can analyze massive datasets to identify potential targets, cross-reference open-source information about civilian locations such as hospitals and schools, model potential collateral damage from different weapon choices, and flag factors relevant to proportionality assessments. By presenting comprehensive information and analytical insights to human targeteers, these systems can theoretically improve targeting decisions and reduce civilian harm.

However, the relationship between AI-enabled decision support and civilian protection is complex and contested. While better information can improve targeting decisions in theory, the practical effects depend on how human operators use decision support system recommendations and whether the systems themselves embody biases or limitations that degrade targeting accuracy. Studies of human-machine interaction demonstrate that decision support systems can improve decision quality when operators understand the systems' capabilities and limitations and actively engage with the analytical process, but can degrade decision quality when operators defer excessively to system recommendations without critical evaluation or when operators lack understanding of how systems reach their conclusions.[6][39] Additionally, the speed at which decision support systems can generate recommendations creates pressure to act quickly, potentially reducing the time available for careful deliberation about civilian protection implications.

The ICRC has emphasized that decision support systems themselves do not make targeting decisions but significantly influence the decisions of human operators, with implications for international humanitarian law compliance.[6] A human operator using a decision support system to make a targeting decision bears responsibility for the decision, including responsibility for assessing whether the system's recommendations are reliable and whether the decision complies with international humanitarian law. This responsibility cannot be delegated to the system; the human operator must exercise judgment about the decision support system's output and must not reflexively accept system recommendations without critical evaluation. However, the reality of human-machine interaction suggests that operators often do defer excessively to system recommendations, particularly in time-critical situations, and this tendency threatens to subordinate human judgment to algorithmic analysis even in contexts designed to preserve human decision-making authority.

## Algorithmic Bias and Civilian Protection: Disparate Impact of Autonomous Systems

A particularly concerning mechanism through which autonomous systems might fail to protect civilians adequately involves algorithmic bias, where systematic biases in training data or system design cause autonomous systems to misidentify certain categories of persons or objects as lawful military targets.[15][18] Algorithmic bias can arise through multiple pathways: training data may underrepresent certain demographics or may contain stereotypical associations that reflect societal biases; design decisions made by programmers and engineers may inadvertently encode biases; or feedback mechanisms may amplify biases over time as the system learns from its own previous decisions and data patterns.

The humanitarian consequences of algorithmic bias in military targeting are severe. If an autonomous targeting system disproportionately misidentifies civilians of particular ethnicities, ages, genders, or other demographic characteristics as combatants, the system will preferentially harm those groups.[15] This disparate impact violates both the principle of distinction, which requires respect for the protected status of all civilians regardless of other characteristics, and broader human rights principles protecting freedom from discrimination. Moreover, algorithmic bias could interact with existing patterns of military targeting that have historically disproportionately affected particular populations, amplifying existing inequities in harm distribution across civilian populations.

Efforts to mitigate algorithmic bias must address bias at multiple stages of system development: in data collection and selection, where efforts must ensure that training datasets adequately represent diverse populations and geographic contexts; in system design, where developers must consciously avoid encoding biases in algorithmic processes; in testing and validation, where systems must be evaluated across diverse scenarios to identify performance disparities; and in operational monitoring, where the performance of systems across different demographic groups must be continuously assessed to detect emerging biases.[15][18] However, complete elimination of bias from autonomous systems is likely impossible, suggesting that the risks of bias-driven civilian harm must be accepted as inherent to autonomous weapons deployment.

## The Speed-Accuracy Tradeoff: Tempo and Civilian Protection

An important tension in autonomous weapons design for civilian protection involves the tradeoff between operational speed and the deliberation necessary for lawful targeting decisions. Proponents of autonomous weapons argue that automated systems can respond to threats more quickly than human operators, enabling faster targeting cycles and potentially reducing overall casualties by preventing attacks that might otherwise occur.[3][6][14] However, the ICRC and other expert bodies have emphasized that adequate protection of civilians often requires slowing the targeting tempo to allow time for careful consideration of civilian presence, proportionality implications, and alternative means of achieving military objectives.[6] Tactical patience, the deliberate slowing of decision-making to allow time for gathering more information and considering implications, is recognized as a valuable technique for reducing civilian casualties.

Autonomous systems designed for speed may directly undermine opportunities for tactical patience by accelerating the targeting cycle and creating pressure on human operators to act quickly based on system recommendations. Additionally, some of the most important information for civilian protection—presence of civilians and implications for proportionality—cannot be gathered or analyzed quickly. Pattern of life analysis, the assessment of civilian presence and movement in target areas, requires sustained observation over time and cannot be compressed without sacrificing accuracy. An autonomous system designed for rapid target engagement might skip crucial phases of analysis necessary for civilian protection, increasing the risk of civilian harm in the pursuit of operational speed.

Recent research and doctrine have questioned whether autonomous systems can actually provide faster engagement cycles while maintaining the time necessary for civilian protection assessments. The tension between speed and deliberation represents a fundamental constraint on the ability to combine autonomous weapons with adequate civilian protection, suggesting that civilian protection requirements may inherently limit the pace at which autonomous targeting systems can operate.

## International Legal Negotiations and Emerging Frameworks

The international community has recognized the urgent need for legal frameworks governing autonomous weapons, with recent United Nations General Assembly meetings and ongoing Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons discussions reflecting broad consensus that new international legal instruments are necessary.[45][48] Proposals under discussion at the United Nations focus on a two-tiered approach that would prohibit certain types of autonomous weapons systems—particularly those that would operate without meaningful human control or that would target people—while establishing regulatory requirements for other autonomous systems that are permitted.[4][30][45][48]

The proposed framework suggests that autonomous systems should be prohibited if they cannot be directed at specific military objectives, cannot be limited in their effects as required by international humanitarian law, or cannot operate with meaningful human control over critical targeting decisions.[4][30] Regulations for permitted systems should establish requirements for meaningful human control, ensure compliance with international humanitarian law principles, provide for training and understanding of system capabilities and limitations, maintain transparency and auditability, and establish accountability mechanisms for violations. The U.S. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, updated in 2023, represents an attempt to establish national-level policy guidelines that reflect some of these international consensus principles, including explicit requirements for meaningful human control, transparent and auditable design methodologies, and lawful use consistent with international humanitarian law.

However, significant obstacles remain to achieving binding international legal instruments protecting civilians from autonomous weapons. The consensus model for decision-making at the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, which requires agreement by all states parties, has allowed a small number of militarily powerful states to block progress toward legally binding agreements.[30][45][48] The shift toward United Nations General Assembly discussions provides a more inclusive forum where a broader range of states can participate in shaping frameworks, but translating United Nations resolutions into binding international law requires sustained political commitment and willingness by powerful military states to accept constraints on autonomous weapons development.

## Conclusion: The Fundamental Limits of Technical Solutions for Civilian Protection

The examination of mechanisms through which decision priorities are established in autonomous weapon systems to protect civilian life reveals a fundamental tension between the technical capabilities of autonomous systems and the legal, ethical, and practical requirements for adequate civilian protection. While multiple mechanisms—meaningful human control, rules of engagement, international humanitarian law principles, technical design features, testing and validation procedures, Article 36 reviews, and emerging international frameworks—exist or are being developed to embed civilian protection priorities into autonomous weapons, the adequacy of these mechanisms remains deeply uncertain and contested.

The core problem is that international humanitarian law, particularly the principles of distinction and proportionality, requires contextual judgment, moral reasoning, and understanding of human circumstances that existing autonomous systems demonstrably cannot replicate. Algorithms can be designed to execute rules, process information, and make decisions based on specified criteria, but they cannot understand context in the way that human combatants guided by moral and ethical codes understand it.[3][1] They cannot recognize the subtle cues of human behavior that indicate whether a person is a combatant or a civilian. They cannot weigh incommensurable values in proportionality determinations. They cannot adapt their decisions to unforeseen circumstances in ways that preserve the fundamental humanitarian imperatives underlying international humanitarian law.

Additionally, the accountability mechanisms that international humanitarian law depends upon—identifying responsible human decision-makers and holding them accountable for violations—become attenuated when targeting decisions are distributed across technical systems, programmer designers, operators, and commanders, with responsibility diffused such that no individual can be clearly held accountable for harmful outcomes. This accountability gap undermines the deterrent effect that accountability mechanisms are designed to provide and leaves victims without meaningful recourse when autonomous systems cause civilian harm.

The path forward requires international commitment to establishing legally binding frameworks that either prohibit autonomous weapons systems that cannot demonstrably comply with international humanitarian law or, for systems where such compliance is possible, establish robust regulations ensuring meaningful human control, transparent and auditable decision-making, comprehensive testing and validation, and clear accountability mechanisms. However, the technical realities of autonomous systems suggest that truly adequate civilian protection may require limiting autonomy more strictly than military efficiency considerations would prefer, accepting slower decision-making cycles and more constrained autonomous capabilities to preserve human judgment at critical decision points. Ultimately, civilian protection in autonomous weapons depends not primarily on technical solutions but on political will to establish and enforce international legal constraints that prioritize civilian life over military operational convenience.
