## Foundational Regulatory Frameworks That Establish Qualification Standards

The determination of merchant onboarding qualification standards originates from multiple regulatory frameworks that collectively define the baseline requirements all payment processors and acquiring banks must follow. The Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), enacted in the United States, serves as a foundational legal requirement that mandates financial institutions develop and maintain comprehensive know-your-customer (KYC) and anti-money laundering (AML) programs[20][45]. This legislation establishes that institutions must verify customer identities, understand the nature and purpose of customer relationships, and conduct ongoing monitoring to detect and report suspicious activities[20]. The Customer Due Diligence (CDD) Rule, which amends Bank Secrecy Act regulations, further clarifies and strengthens these requirements by requiring covered financial institutions to identify and verify the identity of beneficial owners of legal entity customers who own, control, and profit from companies[48]. Under the CDD Rule, financial institutions must identify and verify the identity of any individual who owns twenty-five percent or more of a legal entity, and any individual who controls the legal entity, establishing a clear threshold that shapes how merchants must be vetted[48].

Beyond the Bank Secrecy Act framework, the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) represents another critical pillar in establishing qualification requirements[16][57]. Developed by the Payment Card Industry Security Standards Council, which includes the founding payment brand members of American Express, Discover, JCB International, Mastercard, and Visa, PCI DSS establishes baseline technical and operational requirements designed to protect payment account data[16][57]. This standard applies to all entities that store, process, or transmit cardholder data, including merchants regardless of their size or transaction volume[16][59]. The qualification standards embedded within PCI DSS requirements determine not only what merchants must do to comply with security standards, but also establish different validation requirements based on merchant classification levels, which directly impact the rigor of onboarding audits[16][57]. The PCI DSS framework establishes four merchant levels based on transaction volume, with Level 1 merchants processing over six million transactions annually facing the most stringent requirements, while Level 4 merchants processing fewer than twenty thousand transactions annually face simplified requirements[21][57]. However, it is important to note that transaction volume calculations are performed per card brand, meaning a merchant processing five hundred thousand Visa transactions and five hundred thousand Mastercard transactions would be classified at Level 3 rather than Level 4, as the total volume across brands exceeds the Level 4 threshold[21][57].

The Anti-Money Laundering (AML) regulatory framework further establishes qualification standards by requiring institutions to implement comprehensive screening protocols and ongoing monitoring procedures[20][48]. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) enforces these requirements and, as of January 1, 2024, began compiling a database of corporate beneficial ownership information that financial institutions can use to verify information provided by potential merchants[20]. These regulatory frameworks collectively establish the legal minimum standards that all payment processors must incorporate into their merchant onboarding qualification requirements, creating a baseline from which individual institutions can implement additional, more stringent standards based on their specific risk tolerance and business models[5][20].

## Risk-Based Approaches to Determining Qualification Standards

The determination of merchant onboarding qualification standards has increasingly shifted toward risk-based frameworks that tailor the stringency of requirements to the specific risk profile of each merchant applicant[5][15][25]. Rather than applying uniform qualification requirements to all merchant applicants, risk-based approaches segment merchants into cohorts based on predefined risk factors, allowing organizations to allocate compliance resources more efficiently while still maintaining strong risk controls[5][15]. This approach recognizes that merchants operating low-risk businesses, such as established retailers or grocery stores, present fundamentally different risk profiles than merchants operating in high-risk industries such as online gambling, cryptocurrency exchanges, or adult entertainment services[4][5][15][52]. The risk-based framework establishes that low-risk merchants may proceed through streamlined onboarding processes with simplified documentation requirements, while medium-risk and high-risk merchants must undergo more comprehensive due diligence and provide additional supporting documentation[5][15][52].

The determination of risk levels incorporates multiple dimensions of risk assessment that extend beyond simple industry classification[52]. Financial risk factors are evaluated through examination of the merchant's creditworthiness, credit history, bank account stability, and financial statements to determine the likelihood the merchant can manage chargebacks and processing responsibilities[19][52]. Transactional risk is assessed by examining the merchant's historical chargeback rates, fraud patterns, processing volume trends, and average transaction sizes to identify merchants with elevated exposure to fraud or customer disputes[30][36][52]. Compliance and regulatory risk factors are evaluated by determining whether the merchant operates in jurisdictions with strong regulatory frameworks and whether the merchant has demonstrated adherence to relevant regulations such as PCI DSS, KYC, AML, and industry-specific requirements[5][19][30]. Reputational risk is assessed by examining adverse media coverage, customer complaints, legal disputes, and industry reputation to identify merchants whose business practices might damage the payment provider's brand or expose it to regulatory scrutiny[5][52][54].

The risk-based qualification framework establishes dynamic segmentation criteria that allow payment providers to adjust their approach based on real-time risk assessment[5][15][25]. Some organizations employ adaptive verification processes where the requirements escalate based on the merchant's answers to application questions, effectively creating a branching decision tree where merchants identified as higher-risk based on initial responses are then required to provide additional documentation and undergo enhanced due diligence[15]. Visa's Acceptance Risk Standards framework specifically mandates that acquirers utilize automated systems and enhanced due diligence review processes to manage risks associated with onboarding, with the policy and process being reviewed and updated periodically to reflect changes in regulatory requirements, industry best practices, and organizational risk appetite[43]. This approach allows organizations to identify high-risk applicants early in the funnel and redirect them toward more appropriate verification pathways rather than allowing them to proceed through standardized processes where their risk factors might not be properly evaluated[15][49].

Ownership and beneficial ownership documentation requirements are established to ensure that payment processors understand who controls the merchant entity and to identify individuals with significant ownership stakes who may pose compliance or fraud risks[1][4][9][20][48]. The Customer Due Diligence Rule requires identification and verification of beneficial owners defined as any individual who owns twenty-five percent or more of the legal entity or who controls the entity through other means[48]. For business owners and directors, merchants must provide personal identification documents, names, roles, titles, dates of birth, and addresses[1][4]. The documentation requirements extend to identifying ultimate beneficial owners (UBOs) and include cross-checking these individuals against sanctions lists, politically exposed persons (PEPs) databases, and watchlists to identify any individuals with known associations to criminal activity, money laundering, or terrorist financing[1][4][9][20].

Financial information requirements are determined based on regulatory mandates and the card networks' need to assess the merchant's financial stability and ability to manage payment processing responsibilities[1][6][19][50]. Merchants must provide bank statements covering periods typically ranging from three to six months to demonstrate consistent account balances, transaction volumes, and the absence of unusual account activity[19][50]. Complete financial statements including profit and loss statements, balance sheets, and cash flow statements are required to assess the merchant's profitability, liquidity, and overall financial health[19][50]. For merchants with existing payment processing history, chargeback reports, dispute history, and transaction volume documentation must be provided to allow payment processors to evaluate the merchant's track record and identify any patterns of excessive chargebacks or fraud[19][50].

Compliance documentation requirements are established to ensure merchants meet applicable regulatory standards before payment processing begins[1][6][9]. Merchants must demonstrate adherence to Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requirements by providing documentation of their security infrastructure and compliance efforts[6][18][19]. For industries subject to specific regulatory oversight, such as money transmitters, financial services, or healthcare providers, merchants must provide evidence of requisite licensing and regulatory compliance[1][4][6]. Privacy policies, data protection procedures, and documentation of compliance with regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) or regional equivalents must be submitted and reviewed[6][19]. ## Know Your Customer and Know Your Business Requirements

The Know Your Customer (KYC) and Know Your Business (KYB) requirements that form part of merchant qualification standards are established through regulatory mandates, card network rules, and industry best practices designed to verify merchant identity and assess compliance and fraud risks[1][4][5][9][20][23]. KYC processes focus on verifying the identities of individuals associated with the merchant entity and conducting background checks to identify any individuals with known associations to fraud, money laundering, terrorist financing, or sanctions violations[1][4][5][9]. KYB processes focus specifically on verifying that the merchant entity itself is legitimate, legally registered, and operating in accordance with relevant regulations[1][4][5][9][23]. Together, KYC and KYB requirements establish a comprehensive framework for identity verification and compliance risk assessment that must be completed before merchants are approved for payment processing[1][5][23].

The KYC and KYB verification requirements are established to answer two fundamental questions: "Is this company real and properly registered?" and "Are the people running it real, legitimate individuals without known associations to criminal activity?"[4]. The KYB process requires verification of the business's existence and legitimacy by collecting and reviewing official business registration documents such as articles of incorporation, certificates of formation, business licenses, and tax identification numbers[4][9][23]. These documents must be verified as genuine through comparison against official government databases to confirm the business is in good standing and has not been dissolved, suspended, or placed under adverse regulatory action[4][9]. The verification process requires cross-checking business registration information with multiple independent sources to ensure consistency and identify any discrepancies that might indicate fraudulent applications[1][4][23].

The verification of business owners and ultimate beneficial owners (UBOs) represents a critical component of KYC requirements established through regulatory mandates and card network standards[1][4][9][20][48]. The KYC process requires collection of personal identification documents for all owners with significant ownership stakes, typically defined as twenty-five percent or more of the business entity[4][20][48]. Personal identification verification must be conducted using reliable and independent sources such as government-issued identification documents, comparing information provided against reference databases to confirm the identity of individuals is accurate[1][4][9][23]. The verification process must identify any ownership changes, transfers of control, or modifications to business structure that might impact the merchant's risk profile or compliance status[52]. Watchlist and sanctions screening must be conducted on all owners and beneficial owners to identify any individuals with known associations to money laundering, terrorist financing, fraud, or violations of international sanctions[1][4][9][20].

The determination of which specific KYC and KYB checks must be performed is influenced by the merchant's risk profile, industry classification, and jurisdiction[5][15][25][43]. Low-risk merchants may proceed through streamlined KYC verification with basic document review and standard database checks, while medium-risk and high-risk merchants must undergo enhanced due diligence (EDD) involving comprehensive information gathering, verification of funding sources, detailed beneficial ownership tracing, and more extensive watchlist and sanctions screening[5][9][15][23]. Enhanced due diligence processes particularly focus on identifying high-risk customer profiles such as politically exposed persons, customers from jurisdictions with weak anti-money laundering controls, and customers with questionable financial histories[9][20][23]. The determination of what constitutes adequate KYC and KYB verification is guided by regulatory requirements and card network standards, but also by lessons learned from previous fraud cases and compliance breaches[1][5][49].
