## Understanding the Fundamental Diagram and Its Classical Formulation

The fundamental diagram represents one of the most enduring concepts in traffic engineering, providing a graphical representation that relates road traffic flux (vehicles per hour) to traffic density (vehicles per kilometer)[10]. The diagram emerges from the fundamental relationship that flow equals the product of density and velocity, expressed mathematically as \(q = k \cdot u\), where \(q\) represents flow, \(k\) represents density, and \(u\) represents velocity[1][3]. This seemingly simple relationship belies considerable complexity in how traffic systems behave under varying conditions and demand levels.

Classical fundamental diagram theory, pioneered by researchers such as Greenshields and subsequent investigators, established the initial framework for understanding traffic flow by proposing linear relationships between speed and density[25]. The Greenshields model assumes that speed decreases linearly with increasing density, reaching free-flow speed when density approaches zero and approaching zero speed when density reaches jam density[25][28]. This linear approximation provided significant utility for early traffic flow predictions and capacity analysis, and it remains widely taught as a foundational concept in transportation engineering curricula. However, field observations and empirical data collected from real-world traffic networks have consistently revealed that actual traffic behavior deviates substantially from these linear predictions, particularly as traffic systems experience transitions from free-flow to congested states[19][24].

The fundamental diagram typically exhibits three distinct regions characterized by different traffic dynamics[3][21]. In the free-flow region at low densities, vehicles travel at or near their desired speeds with minimal interaction between vehicles, and flow increases approximately linearly with density[21]. At intermediate densities, traffic transitions through regions where increasing density begins to constrain vehicle speeds, and the relationship between these variables becomes increasingly nonlinear[6][7]. Finally, in the congested region at high densities, further increases in density result in decreased flow as the speed of vehicles drops precipitously, creating a negatively sloped congested branch in the flow-density diagram[10][21].

## The Emergence and Importance of Nonlinear Density-Velocity Relationships

Traditional single-regime models that assume a simple functional relationship between speed and density have proven inadequate for capturing the full spectrum of traffic behavior observed in real-world networks[25][27]. Subsequent theoretical and empirical work revealed that the relationship between speed and density is fundamentally nonlinear, with the nature of this nonlinearity varying depending on traffic conditions, driver behavior, road geometry, and environmental factors[8][11][24]. Multiple nonlinear models have been proposed to better represent this relationship, including the Greenberg logarithmic model, the Underwood exponential model, the Pipes generalized model, and more recent formulations that incorporate phase transition concepts[25][28].

The Underwood model, for instance, proposes an exponential decay relationship where vehicle speed decreases exponentially with density rather than linearly[25][28]. This model exhibits advantages in representing low-speed, congested flow conditions more accurately than linear models, though it has limitations in that speed never quite reaches zero even at infinite density[25]. The Greenberg model, conversely, employs a logarithmic relationship and provides superior fits in heavily congested conditions but produces the counterintuitive result of infinite free-flow speed[25][28]. These competing models demonstrate that different nonlinear functional forms capture different aspects of traffic behavior with varying degrees of accuracy across the density spectrum.

The importance of understanding nonlinear density-velocity relationships becomes particularly acute when considering traffic systems near phase transition points, where the fundamental nature of traffic dynamics changes qualitatively[22][31][45]. Phase transitions in traffic flow represent critical transitions from stable, orderly traffic states to unstable, congested states characterized by stop-and-go waves, density waves, and other complex dynamic phenomena[22][31][45]. These transitions cannot be adequately predicted using equilibrium-based models that assume a single, deterministic relationship between density and velocity. Rather, the phase transition phenomenon requires recognition that traffic can exhibit multiple equilibrium states at the same density, a characteristic that nonlinear models must capture[31][42].

## Phase Transitions and Critical Phenomena in Traffic Flow

Traffic flow exhibits phase transition behavior analogous to physical phase transitions in materials, with the critical density and corresponding critical velocity representing boundaries where traffic stability changes fundamentally[21][31][45]. At densities below the critical density, traffic maintains stability such that small perturbations dissipate rather than amplify[15][31]. Beyond the critical density, the same perturbations amplify, leading to wave formation, shock propagation, and the transition to congested flow characterized by oscillatory behavior[15][31][32].

The three-phase traffic theory describes critical behavior as a breakdown phenomenon, which represents the phase transition from free-flow to congestion at a bottleneck[31]. This framework identifies three distinct traffic phases: free flow, synchronized flow, and wide moving jams[31][45]. The transition between these phases is not abrupt in real-world traffic but rather involves complex dynamics where vehicles experience alternating periods of acceleration and deceleration as they navigate through the transition region[1]. This dynamic behavior cannot be represented adequately by static, single-valued fundamental diagrams that assume deterministic speed-density relationships.

Research on traffic flow near critical densities has revealed fundamental instabilities in the traffic stream[15][32]. When the density exceeds the critical value, small variations in spacing between vehicles trigger feedback mechanisms that amplify these variations into larger oscillations[15]. The instability mechanism arises from the interaction between acceleration and deceleration dynamics, driver reaction times, and spatial variations in density[1][15]. Variance in vehicle velocities plays a crucial role in triggering these instabilities, with research demonstrating that even moderate speed variance can trigger jam formation and propagation[15][32][40].

Near phase transition points, the fundamental diagram exhibits characteristic features that challenge traditional modeling approaches[22][45][48]. Rather than following a single smooth curve, the flow-density relationship displays what is often described as "scatter" in empirical data, with multiple flow values possible at the same density[6][30][42]. This scatter reflects the reality that traffic near transition points can occupy multiple equilibrium states depending on the history of how the system evolved to that point, a phenomenon referred to as hysteresis[16][42][39]. Additionally, empirical fundamental diagrams often display capacity drop phenomena, where the maximum flow achievable in congested conditions falls below the maximum flow in free-flow conditions at approximately the same density[16][13][30].
