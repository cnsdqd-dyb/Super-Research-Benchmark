# The Influence of Social Network Rewiring Probability on Spectral Clustering Structure of Collective Identity Vectors in Urbanization Models

Social networks in urban environments undergo continuous transformation through mechanisms of relationship formation and dissolution. This report examines how the probability of network rewiring fundamentally alters the spectral clustering structure that emerges when analyzing collective identity vectors in urbanized populations. The key finding is that rewiring probability acts as a bifurcation parameter that controls transitions between distinct topological regimes in urban social networks. At low rewiring probabilities, network structure remains locally cohesive with high clustering coefficients, preserving geographic and homophilic community identity markers that appear as well-separated eigenvectors in spectral decompositions. As rewiring probability increases toward intermediate values, the network enters a regime where spectral clustering becomes maximally sensitive to detecting bridging structures and identity transitions across urban zones, a phenomenon particularly relevant for understanding how diverse populations organize themselves spatially. At high rewiring probabilities, networks approach random Erdős–Rényi configurations where collective identity vectors become increasingly diffuse and global in character, with spectral properties dominated by universal eigenvalue distributions rather than meaningful community structure. This phenomenon has profound implications for understanding how cities sustain or dissolve collective identities as their social connectivity patterns evolve.

## Social Network Rewiring Mechanisms and Rewiring Probability in Urban Contexts

The concept of network rewiring represents one of the most fundamental processes through which urban social networks evolve and adapt. Understanding rewiring requires grasping both the mechanical process of edge modification and the probability distribution governing which connections are selected for rewiring in a given time period. In the context of urbanization, rewiring refers to the stochastic process by which individuals sever existing social connections and form new ones, a phenomenon that characterizes how cities dynamically reorganize their social fabric in response to migration, economic pressures, changing built environments, and evolving cultural preferences.[2][24]

The Watts-Strogatz model provides perhaps the most intuitive formulation for understanding network rewiring in urban systems.[13][15][16] In this framework, a network begins in a highly structured state—typically a ring lattice where each node connects only to its nearest neighbors—representing the initial geographic clustering of social ties in urban neighborhoods. The rewiring process then selects each edge with probability p and redirects one end of the connection to a uniformly random node elsewhere in the network. This parameter p, the rewiring probability, thus controls the degree to which the network transitions from rigid local structure to more globally connected randomness. When p equals zero, the network remains entirely locally clustered; when p equals one, the network becomes completely random; and for intermediate values of p, small-world networks emerge that combine high local clustering with short average path lengths.[13][15][16] These small-world properties are empirically observed in many real urban social networks, where people maintain tight-knit local communities while nonetheless remaining connected to distant acquaintances through occasional long-range ties.

Beyond the classical Watts-Strogatz formulation, more sophisticated rewiring models have been developed that incorporate spatial constraints reflecting the geographic reality of urban systems.[33][36][44] Spatially constrained adaptive rewiring, for instance, allows edges to be severed and reformed but restricts new connections to nodes within a specified distance threshold, modeling the constraint that people are more likely to form ties with others they can physically encounter.[33][36][44] This spatially local bias in rewiring fundamentally alters the resulting network topology compared to purely random rewiring. When nodes can only rewire to nearby neighbors, the network maintains stronger geographic clustering while still achieving some of the connectivity benefits of random rewiring. Conversely, when rewiring allows completely unrestricted reconnection, it more rapidly destroys the geographic scaffold of urban social structure.

The dynamics of rewiring in real urban systems are far more complex than any simple stochastic model can capture. Research on actual social networks in cities reveals that rewiring probability itself is heterogeneous across the urban population, varying with socioeconomic status, age, digital connectivity, and access to transportation infrastructure.[2][24] MIT researchers studying mobile phone data from over 25 million users across three countries found that urban networks are structured primarily by social distance rather than geographic proximity, suggesting that the effective rewiring probability varies substantially based on whether potential new connections share relevant social attributes.[2] This finding implies that any accurate model of urban network rewiring must account for homophilic preferences—the tendency of similar individuals to connect with one another—as a force that modulates effective rewiring probabilities.

In the context of urbanization more broadly, rewiring probability relates to structural transitions in how cities organize themselves socially and economically. As cities grow and urbanize, migration patterns bring in new residents who must integrate into existing social networks, effectively increasing the rewiring probability of the entire system. The spatial-temporal evolution of urban networks in China, studied across 269 cities from 2002 to 2019, reveals that cities exhibit priority linkages and path dependence in their network evolution, suggesting that rewiring probability is not uniform across time but rather increases during periods of rapid urban growth and development.[39] During such periods of elevated rewiring probability, urban social structures undergo reorganization, existing community identities may dissolve or merge, and new collective identities emerge at different spatial scales.

## Spectral Clustering Fundamentals and Eigenstructure Analysis

Spectral clustering represents a sophisticated mathematical approach to identifying community structure in networks by exploiting the eigenvalue decomposition of matrices associated with the network.[4][9][20][28][55] The fundamental insight underlying spectral clustering is that the eigenvectors of network matrices encode essential information about the network's community structure in a form that makes clustering tasks easier to solve than working directly with the adjacency matrix or similarity matrix. The approach has deep roots in algebraic graph theory and linear algebra, leveraging the intimate connection between graph structure and the spectrum of its associated matrices.

The standard approach to spectral clustering begins with the construction of a Laplacian matrix from the adjacency matrix A of the network.[4][38][41] The unnormalized Laplacian is defined as \(L = D - A\), where D is the diagonal degree matrix with entries \(D_{ii}\) equal to the degree of node i and all other entries zero.[41] This matrix has the elegant property that its eigenvalues and eigenvectors directly reveal the structure of the network. The smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian is always zero, and its associated eigenvector is the constant vector of all ones, reflecting the fact that the Laplacian is singular. The multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue equals the number of connected components in the graph.[4][38] For clustering problems, the key vectors are the eigenvectors associated with the smallest non-zero eigenvalues, particularly the Fiedler vector associated with the second-smallest eigenvalue, often denoted \(\lambda_2\) or the algebraic connectivity.[38][41]

The Fiedler vector possesses remarkable properties that make it ideal for bipartitioning graphs.[38][41][55] In a spectral partition, nodes are divided into two groups based on the sign of their components in the Fiedler vector: nodes with positive components form one cluster, while nodes with negative components form another. This seemingly simple procedure actually solves a relaxation of the normalized cut problem, which aims to partition the graph into balanced clusters while minimizing the number of edges cut between clusters.[38][41][55] The quality of the partition obtained through the Fiedler vector is bounded by Cheeger's inequality, which guarantees that the spectral partition achieves a conductance roughly equal to the square root of the optimal partition's conductance.[38][41]

For partitioning networks into more than two clusters, the spectral approach generalizes naturally by considering multiple eigenvectors.[4][20][28][38][41][55] Instead of using only the Fiedler vector, one selects the eigenvectors corresponding to the k smallest eigenvalues of the Laplacian, where k is the desired number of clusters. These k eigenvectors form an n-by-k matrix, where n is the number of nodes, and each row of this matrix represents a node in a k-dimensional spectral embedding space.[4][20][28][38][41][55] The nodes are then clustered in this reduced-dimensional space using standard clustering algorithms, typically k-means, which identifies nodes with similar positions in the spectral embedding.[4][20][28][38][41][55]

A normalized variant of spectral clustering, often implemented in practice, uses the normalized Laplacian defined as \(\mathcal{L} = I - D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}\), where I is the identity matrix.[4][55] This normalization provides better numerical stability, particularly for networks with heterogeneous degree distributions where some nodes have substantially different numbers of connections than others. The normalized Laplacian's eigenvectors are conceptually equivalent to weighted kernel k-means applied to the rows of the eigenvector matrix, providing a bridge between spectral methods and clustering theory.[4][20][55]

The mathematical beauty of spectral clustering lies in how it transforms an NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem into a convex relaxation amenable to efficient solution through eigendecomposition.[4][20][38][41][55] The spectral approach effectively maps the network into a low-dimensional Euclidean space where the underlying cluster structure becomes more apparent and separable, making subsequent clustering steps more robust and accurate. This dimensionality reduction is achieved without loss of essential topological information about the network, as the selected eigenvectors capture the most significant structural patterns.

For networks with true community structure generated by stochastic block models, spectral clustering achieves near-optimal recovery when certain statistical conditions are met.[37][40] The stochastic block model assumes that nodes belong to latent blocks or communities, and edge probabilities depend only on block membership. When the block structure is sufficiently strong relative to random noise, spectral clustering methods can provably recover the true communities with high probability.[37][40] This theoretical foundation provides confidence that spectral clustering is not merely a heuristic but a principled approach grounded in statistical inference theory.

## Collective Identity Vectors and Their Spectral Representation

Collective identity in urban contexts refers to the shared sense of belonging and cultural orientation that emerges among groups of individuals who perceive themselves as members of a common community within a city.[7][10] This identity is not monolithic or static but rather emerges from the interplay of multiple social networks, cultural expressions, spatial locations, and shared meanings that individuals negotiate together. Understanding collective identity requires moving beyond individual characteristics and examining the relational structures through which people construct shared meaning and solidarity.

In network terms, collective identity can be represented as a vector where each element corresponds to an individual and the magnitude of that element reflects the strength of that individual's identification with the collective.[32][35] When considering multiple overlapping identities within a city—based on neighborhood, profession, age cohort, cultural background, or political affiliation—we can represent the urban social fabric as a set of collective identity vectors, each capturing a different dimension of shared belonging. These vectors are not arbitrary assignments but rather emergent properties of the underlying social network structure, arising from patterns of communication, collaboration, and interaction among urban residents.

The relationship between network structure and collective identity vectors is particularly close in spectral terms. The eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian or adjacency matrix of an urban social network can be interpreted as collective identity vectors because they capture patterns of coordinated activity across the network.[6][20][27][43] The largest eigenvectors, corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues of the Laplacian, reveal the dominant modes of community organization—the primary divisions along which the urban population naturally clusters. Eigenvectors associated with larger eigenvalues capture finer-grained community structures and within-community variations.

Empirical studies of urban social networks reveal that these spectral identities have real meaning in terms of how people actually organize themselves in cities.[2][5][21][24] Research using mobile phone data from major metropolitan areas found that social networks cluster according to patterns that reflect both geographic proximity and social homophily, with the strongest clusters often corresponding to neighborhoods or populations sharing particular characteristics.[2][24] When visualized in spectral space—using eigenvectors of the network Laplacian as coordinates—these natural clusters become maximally separated and most easily identified. The fact that multiple eigenvectors are needed to adequately represent urban social structure suggests that collective identity in cities is inherently multidimensional, with people embedded simultaneously in multiple overlapping communities of identity.

The dimensionality of the spectral representation of collective identity vectors has profound implications for understanding urban social structure. A low-dimensional spectral embedding suggests that the urban population naturally organizes into a small number of distinct identity groups with relatively weak bridging between them. A high-dimensional spectral embedding, by contrast, indicates fragmented or continuous variation in identity across the urban population, with many transitional identities and less clear community boundaries. The number of significant eigenvectors thus serves as a quantitative measure of the complexity and fragmentation of collective identity structure within a city.

## How Rewiring Probability Transforms Spectral Properties

The influence of rewiring probability on spectral properties of networks has been studied extensively, particularly in the context of small-world networks and network transitions.[13][15][16][18] The spectral properties of networks undergo dramatic transformations as rewiring probability increases from zero to one. At zero rewiring probability, a ring lattice network has very specific spectral properties: the eigenvalue spectrum exhibits circulant structure with eigenvalues determined by circular convolutions.[15][18] The eigenvectors of the circulant Laplacian correspond to Fourier modes, and there is strong geometric clustering in the eigenvalue distribution.[15][18]

As rewiring probability increases from zero, the spectrum gradually transforms.[15][18] At very low rewiring probabilities, when only a small fraction of edges have been randomly redirected, the eigenvalue spectrum remains dominated by the structure inherited from the original ring lattice. The smallest non-zero eigenvalues remain sparse and well-separated, corresponding to the strong local clustering structure that remains mostly intact. In spectral clustering terms, this means that the Fiedler vector and the first few spectral eigenvectors accurately identify the underlying modular structure of the network, as most of the network's edges still respect the original community divisions.

A crucial transition occurs at intermediate rewiring probabilities, typically in the range of p between 0.01 and 0.1 for networks of realistic size.[13][15][16][18] This is where small-world networks emerge, with their characteristic combination of high clustering and short average path length. In spectral terms, this transition manifests as the emergence of new structure in the eigenvalue spectrum. The spectral gap—the difference between the largest and smallest non-zero eigenvalues—changes non-monotonically as a function of rewiring probability, initially narrowing and then broadening. This spectral reorganization has profound implications for spectral clustering performance. During the small-world regime, spectral clustering often achieves superior performance at detecting the true underlying community structure, as the rewiring has injected enough global connectivity to accurately represent long-range correlations while maintaining enough local structure to preserve community identity.

As rewiring probability continues to increase toward one, the network approaches the Erdős–Rényi random graph limit.[13][15][16] The spectrum of the random graph Laplacian has well-characterized properties: eigenvalues become increasingly densely distributed according to the circular law distribution, with most eigenvalues clustering near a characteristic value.[15][16] In this regime, the Fiedler vector becomes increasingly uninformative for identifying community structure, as the network no longer possesses genuine communities but instead comprises edges distributed randomly according to fixed connection probabilities. Spectral clustering applied to random networks produces partitions of poor quality or finds spurious communities that reflect random fluctuations rather than true structural patterns.[4][20][28][55]

For urban social networks specifically, which exhibit spatial constraints on rewiring, the spectral transformation follows a somewhat different trajectory than for unconstrained random rewiring.[33][36][44] When rewiring is constrained to respect spatial proximity, the network maintains stronger locality properties even at moderately high rewiring probabilities. Spatially constrained rewiring produces networks that lie partway between the two extremes of pure lattice and pure random graph, exhibiting small-world properties across a wider range of rewiring probabilities than unconstrained rewiring.[33][36][44] In spectral terms, this means that the spectral gap and the quality of spectral clustering remain robust to increases in rewiring probability when spatial constraints are present.

The spectral clustering of networks with adaptive rewiring—where edges are preferentially rewired based on similarity between nodes—shows yet another pattern.[44][36][33] When nodes preferentially sever connections to dissimilar neighbors and form new connections to similar nodes, the rewiring process actually drives the network toward more pronounced community structure. The spectral properties improve in terms of producing clearer community divisions, with the Fiedler vector becoming more strongly polarized and higher-order eigenvectors capturing finer distinctions between and within communities.[44][36][33] This adaptive rewiring mechanism is particularly relevant for understanding urban social networks, where individuals constantly reevaluate their social connections and preferentially maintain or strengthen ties with compatible others while weakening ties with incompatible individuals.

## Rewiring Probability Effects on Collective Identity Vector Clustering

The most direct impact of rewiring probability on the spectral clustering of collective identity vectors manifests as changes in the clarity and stability of spectral partition structure. At low rewiring probabilities, characteristic of tightly-knit urban neighborhoods with stable social structures, the spectral clustering of collective identity vectors reveals multiple well-defined communities corresponding to distinct identity groups. As rewiring probability increases to intermediate levels, typical of rapidly urbanizing areas where migration brings constant influx of new residents and existing social networks must accommodate newcomers, the structure of collective identity vectors becomes more complex. The spectral eigenvalues become less well-separated, requiring examination of multiple eigenvectors to fully characterize the identity structure. Individuals transition from belonging to clearly defined identity groups toward occupying transitional positions between identities. The Fiedler vector, while still revealing meaningful structure, becomes less bipolar and shows more gradient variation. Importantly, this intermediate regime often produces the most detailed and nuanced spectral clustering structure, as the rewiring has been sufficient to create bridging connections between communities without yet destroying community structure altogether.

At high rewiring probabilities, characteristic of maximally mixed urban populations with highly fluid social connections, collective identity vectors become increasingly diffuse and multidimensional. The spectral clustering becomes less useful for identifying discrete identity groups because the network lacks true community structure to detect. Individuals' identities become more independent and idiosyncratic, less constrained by membership in cohesive groups. The eigenvalue spectrum approaches that of a random graph, with multiple eigenvalues close together and eigenvectors showing no clear interpretable structure. In this regime, traditional community detection and identity clustering become problematic, and alternative approaches recognizing continuous variation rather than discrete categories become more appropriate.

The practical implication of these observations for understanding urban collective identity is profound. Cities that maintain low rewiring probabilities—through strong neighborhood boundaries, homogeneous populations, or limited social mobility—exhibit clear, stable collective identity structures that are readily captured by spectral clustering methods. These cities have strong neighborhoods or ethnic communities with clear identity markers. Cities experiencing rapid urbanization and high rewiring probabilities by contrast exhibit diffuse, continuously varying collective identities that resist partitioning into discrete groups. Understanding which regime a particular city occupies is essential for designing appropriate social policies, planning urban interventions, and predicting how collective identity will respond to changes in network structure.

## Modularity and Community Detection Quality in Rewiring Regimes

The modularity matrix approach to community detection provides another perspective on how rewiring probability affects spectral clustering of collective identity.[20][23][28][40] Modularity measures the extent to which edges within communities exceed what would be expected by chance in a random graph with the same degree sequence. A network with high modularity exhibits clear community structure with many within-community edges and few between-community edges. Spectral analysis of the modularity matrix reveals community structure by decomposing the modularity into eigenvector components.

The modularity-based approach reveals that rewiring probability non-monotonically affects community quality. At zero rewiring probability, modularity can be perfect if the network truly consists of disconnected components, but typically achieves high values because local clustering in the lattice structure is strong. As rewiring probability increases to small values, modularity first increases because the random rewiring introduces long-range connections that serve as bridges between components, actually clarifying the underlying modular structure by connecting communities in ways that make them more distinct from each other. At intermediate rewiring probabilities, modularity reaches an optimum: communities are clear enough to be detected but sufficiently interconnected to be meaningful in terms of network function.

As rewiring probability continues to increase, modularity declines because the network increasingly approximates a random graph where true community structure dissolves into noise.[20][23][28][40] In the fully random regime, modularity approaches zero, indicating no meaningful community structure. Importantly, the resolution limit of modularity detection—the smallest communities that can be reliably detected—also depends on rewiring probability. At low rewiring probability, very small communities can be detected. As rewiring probability increases, the resolution limit increases, meaning only larger aggregated communities can be reliably identified.[20][23][28][40]

These modularity insights have direct implications for detecting collective urban identity. In low-rewiring cities, fine-grained identity distinctions can be detected and analyzed. In high-rewiring cities, only broad identity categories remain detectable. The practical implication is that identifying and understanding collective identity becomes progressively harder as urban rewiring rates increase, requiring larger sample sizes and more sophisticated analysis to detect meaningful patterns in increasingly diffuse data.

## Adaptive and State-Dependent Rewiring in Identity Contexts

Beyond the simple random rewiring models, more recent research has explored adaptive rewiring where the probability of removing or adding specific edges depends on properties of the nodes involved.[36][44][33] In homophilic adaptive rewiring, individuals preferentially sever connections to dissimilar others while forming new connections to similar others. This mechanism is particularly relevant for understanding collective identity because identity similarity is a major driver of actual social bonding in real urban networks.

Under homophilic adaptive rewiring, the spectral clustering structure becomes progressively clearer as the rewiring process unfolds, even if the baseline rewiring probability is quite high.[44][36][33] The reason is that homophilic rewiring acts as a self-reinforcing mechanism: as individuals sever ties to those unlike themselves and form ties to those like themselves, homogeneous groups coalesce, and the spectral community structure strengthens. The eigenvalue gap between the smallest and second-smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian increases over time under homophilic rewiring, making the community structure progressively more pronounced.[44][36][33]

Conversely, heterophilic rewiring—where individuals prefer to connect to dissimilar others—has the opposite effect.[44][36][33] Under heterophilic preferences, the network rewires toward more random configurations even without increasing the base rewiring probability. Communities dissolve and collective identity becomes fragmented. The spectral clustering structure degrades, with multiple eigenvalues clustering together and no clear community divisions emerging.

These adaptive rewiring mechanisms suggest that understanding urban collective identity requires not just knowing the rewiring probability but understanding the underlying preferences and mechanisms driving rewiring. A city with moderate rewiring probability but strong homophilic preferences might maintain clear collective identity structure, while a city with low rewiring probability but heterophilic preferences might exhibit fragmented identity. The interaction between base rewiring rate and identity-based rewiring preferences thus jointly determines spectral clustering properties.

## Integration of Spectral Clustering with Stochastic Block Models

To move beyond purely descriptive spectral clustering toward statistical inference about community structure, stochastic block models provide a principled generative framework.[37][40] In an SBM, nodes belong to latent blocks or communities, and the probability of an edge between any two nodes depends only on their block membership. Spectral clustering can be understood as an approximate solution to the maximum likelihood inference problem under an SBM, and examining when spectral methods succeed or fail provides insights into their behavior under different rewiring regimes.

Under an SBM with fixed community structure, the optimal block recovery requires that the between-block edge density be sufficiently lower than the within-block edge density.[37][40] Rewiring probability affects how much this signal is degraded. At low rewiring probability, a network generated from an SBM maintains clear signal because the random rewiring has not yet disrupted the community structure. As rewiring probability increases, edges are increasingly placed randomly rather than according to block membership, degrading the signal-to-noise ratio. There exists a detectability threshold beyond which no algorithm can reliably recover communities from network data alone, and this threshold depends on the balance between rewiring probability, community structure strength, and network size.[37][40]

The interesting regime for urban identity is where communities are partially obscured by randomness but not entirely destroyed—exactly the intermediate rewiring regime. In this regime, spectral clustering methods succeed at block recovery better than simpler approaches because they effectively filter out random noise while preserving signal structure. As rewiring probability increases beyond the detectability threshold, even spectral methods fail, a limitation that applies to all inference-based approaches.[37][40]

## Discussion: Synthesis and Implications

The literature synthesized in this analysis reveals several key insights about how rewiring probability influences spectral clustering structure of collective identity vectors in urban systems. First, rewiring probability acts as a structural bifurcation parameter that controls transitions between distinct topological regimes. The network does not gradually morph continuously as rewiring probability increases but rather undergoes qualitative phase transitions at critical threshold values. The small-world regime at intermediate rewiring probabilities represents a sweet spot where spectral clustering performance is often optimal because the network maintains sufficient structure to be analyzed but enough connectivity to be globally coherent.

Second, the relationship between rewiring probability and identity clustering is non-monotonic: community quality initially improves as rewiring probability increases from zero, then deteriorates as rewiring becomes too extensive. This implies that moderate urban dynamics with steady but not excessive social network rewiring create optimal conditions for maintaining clearly identifiable collective identities. Cities that are either completely static or undergoing chaotic transformation exhibit less clear collective identity structure.

Third, the mechanism of rewiring—whether it is random, spatially-constrained, or adaptive based on similarity—matters as much as the overall rewiring probability. Homophilic adaptive rewiring strengthens community structure and collective identity even at relatively high baseline rewiring probabilities. This suggests that cities where residents actively seek out similar others and form strong bonds with compatible individuals will maintain clearer collective identities despite high social network dynamism.

Fourth, urban collective identity is inherently multidimensional and multiscale, requiring multiple eigenvectors to capture adequately. The first few eigenvectors reveal the broadest identity divisions (perhaps socioeconomic class, major ethnic or geographic divisions), while higher-order eigenvectors reveal more refined identity structures. The appropriate number of dimensions needed reflects the complexity of identity organization in the specific city.

Fifth, understanding rewiring probability requires examining not just network structure but also the underlying drivers of rewiring: migration patterns, transportation infrastructure, economic opportunities, and individual preferences. The effective rewiring probability observed in a city's social network emerges from these multiple factors, and understanding identity requires understanding how these drivers combine to produce network dynamics.

Application of these frameworks to understanding segregation, information diffusion, and policy impacts could yield substantial practical benefits for urban planning and social policy.

The mathematical framework uniting rewiring probability, spectral clustering, and collective identity provides a scientific language for discussing fundamental questions about how cities organize themselves and how diverse populations maintain both individual distinctiveness and collective solidarity. As urbanization accelerates globally and cities become increasingly complex and diverse, understanding these processes becomes ever more critical for creating livable, inclusive urban futures.
