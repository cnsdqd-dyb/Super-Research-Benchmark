# Institutional Guarantees for Balancing Economic Benefits and Cultural Heritage in Creative Industry Value Chains

Institutional frameworks designed to ensure long-term value chains in creative industries that successfully balance economic benefits with cultural heritage preservation represent one of the most critical and complex policy challenges of contemporary development. The creative industries, which encompass everything from visual and performing arts to design, fashion, music, and digital media, generate an estimated global worth of 4.3 trillion USD per year and account for 6.1 percent of the global economy, employing nearly 30 million people worldwide[19]. However, these same industries face persistent tensions between the imperative for profit maximization and the need to preserve authentic cultural expressions, protect the rights of original creators and communities, and maintain the integrity of cultural heritage. This report examines the multifaceted institutional guarantees that structure creative industry value chains, analyzes how these mechanisms attempt to reconcile economic imperatives with cultural stewardship, and evaluates the effectiveness of existing frameworks in achieving this delicate balance across different contexts and sectors.

## Intellectual Property Rights Frameworks as Foundational Institutional Guarantees

Intellectual property rights represent perhaps the most fundamental institutional guarantee for protecting creative works and ensuring that economic value generated through creative activities flows to original creators and cultural communities. The copyright system, dating back to the United States Copyright Act of 1790, establishes exclusive rights for authors and creators, enabling them to control the use and distribution of their creative expression and determine the terms under which their works are made available to the public[5]. This foundational legal structure has evolved significantly to address contemporary challenges, including digital distribution and the commodification of cultural expressions across international borders. The robustness of copyright protection directly impacts the sustainability of creative value chains because it provides legal certainty that investment in creative production will yield returns to those who conceive, develop, and commercialize creative works.

The economic significance of copyright-based industries cannot be overstated. In the United States alone, copyright industries are responsible for an estimated 2.9 trillion dollars of gross domestic product, representing 12.52 percent of the economy, and employ nearly 16.1 million workers[5]. However, the effectiveness of copyright as an institutional guarantee depends critically on enforcement mechanisms that prevent unauthorized appropriation and ensure that market conditions reward creativity proportionally to its contribution to value creation. The challenge of balancing economic protection with cultural access has become increasingly acute in the digital environment, where the ease of reproduction and distribution fundamentally alters traditional copyright economics. Digital technologies have created situations where copyright protection mechanisms must now address unprecedented forms of infringement and develop new approaches to managing intellectual property in contexts where physical scarcity no longer constrains reproduction[4].

Beyond traditional copyright frameworks, institutional guarantees also encompass collective management organizations, which represent an alternative mechanism for administering creators' rights at scale[20]. Collective Management Organizations function as intermediaries that enable individual creators—who would be impractical to contact individually for licensing permission—to have their rights managed collectively, facilitating efficient licensing, royalty collection, and distribution[23]. In the context of cultural industries, particularly in developing countries, these organizations serve social and cultural functions that extend beyond mere rights administration, including advancing the interests of cultural communities and supporting the preservation of national cultural identity[20]. The institutional structure of CMOs typically involves governance arrangements that provide representation to member creators, establishing accountability mechanisms that attempt to ensure fair treatment and equitable distribution of royalties. However, the effectiveness of these institutions varies significantly across jurisdictions, with developing countries frequently facing challenges in establishing transparent, member-responsive CMO structures.

## Legal Frameworks Protecting Cultural Heritage and Traditional Cultural Expressions

Institutional guarantees designed specifically to protect cultural heritage and traditional cultural expressions represent a critical dimension of creative value chain governance that extends beyond conventional intellectual property law. The recognition that traditional knowledge and cultural expressions constitute valuable intellectual property requiring sui generis protection frameworks has emerged from indigenous peoples' movements and international agreements acknowledging the rights of cultural communities over their heritage[8]. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples explicitly recognizes indigenous peoples' rights to maintain, control, protect, and develop intellectual property over their cultural heritage, traditional knowledge, and traditional cultural expressions, establishing a normative framework that many states are now attempting to operationalize through domestic legislation[8].

The Nagoya Protocol and the Convention on Biological Diversity provide international institutional frameworks requiring that anyone seeking to access genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge obtain Prior Informed Consent from indigenous communities and establish mutually agreed terms for benefit-sharing[39]. These frameworks institutionalize a recognition that traditional ecological knowledge and cultural practices represent intellectual property with profound cultural and commercial value, and that the original knowledge-holders and cultural communities must maintain agency over how their knowledge is utilized commercially[39]. This represents a fundamental shift in how institutional guarantees operate—rather than simply protecting individual creators' rights to profit from their creations, these frameworks explicitly recognize collective rights and mandate institutional processes through which communities can exercise control over their cultural resources. The effectiveness of benefit-sharing arrangements depends critically on institutional capacity within cultural communities to negotiate fair terms, monitor compliance, and ensure that financial or non-financial benefits are reinvested in cultural preservation activities[39].

Several countries have implemented comprehensive legal frameworks to protect cultural heritage in the context of creative industries. Indonesia's Law 5 of 2017 on the Advancement of Culture established a comprehensive national framework encompassing protection, development, and utilization of cultural heritage through specific institutional mechanisms including cultural inventories, maintenance protocols, and systems for fostering cultural human resources[2]. Similarly, Indonesia's Law 24 of 2019 on Creative Economy specifically regulates the creative economy sector from upstream production through downstream commercialization, establishing incentives for creative practitioners, capacity-building requirements, and institutional structures for protecting intellectual property while supporting sustainable livelihoods for creative workers[2]. These legal frameworks institutionalize cultural stewardship as a state responsibility, creating governmental institutions tasked with balancing economic development in creative sectors with preservation of authentic cultural expressions.

## Certification and Standards Systems as Institutional Guarantees for Authenticity and Fair Practice

Certification and verification systems represent an increasingly important category of institutional guarantee through which third-party assessments establish that products and services meet established standards for quality, authenticity, and ethical production practices. The UNESCO Award of Excellence for Handicrafts demonstrates how international certification frameworks can establish rigorous standards for handicraft production while encouraging innovation within tradition[14]. Established jointly by UNESCO and the ASEAN Handicraft Promotion and Development Association, this program aims to promote quality crafts that uphold rigorous standards of excellence, ensuring that consumers purchasing award-designated handicrafts receive high-quality, culturally authentic products manufactured in socially responsible manners with environmental respect[14]. By establishing these standards and providing third-party certification, the program institutionalizes recognition of excellence while creating market differentiation that can command premium prices for authentic, ethically produced cultural products.

Similarly, W.A.G.E. Certification represents a sector-wide institutional guarantee for artist compensation in the nonprofit arts sector, establishing minimum payment standards calculated using a formula based on an institution's annual operating expenses[29]. Launched in 2014 as the first program of its kind, W.A.G.E. Certification has resulted in more than 24 million dollars being paid directly to artists through its administration, establishing institutional mechanisms through which artistic labor receives appropriate compensation rather than being treated as a donation[29]. This framework institutionalizes recognition that artists' contributions constitute labor worthy of remuneration calibrated to institutional resources and market conditions, creating accountability mechanisms that certify institutions' commitment to fair compensation practices.

Fair Trade Certification systems provide another institutional mechanism through which value chain transparency and ethical standards are institutionalized. Fair Trade certification ensures that workers, farmers, and artisans in developing countries receive fair compensation, work in safe conditions, and maintain agency over their economic futures[12]. By establishing clear standards for ethical sourcing, fair wages, and sustainable production methods, Fair Trade certification creates institutional guarantees that producers participating in global value chains receive equitable compensation and that supply chains maintain transparency regarding production conditions. The certification process involves third-party auditing and compliance verification, creating institutional mechanisms through which consumers and businesses can have confidence that products meeting Fair Trade standards reflect the stated commitment to ethical practices[12]. For creative industries, particularly those involving artisanal production in developing countries, Fair Trade certification has become an important mechanism through which cultural integrity and equitable economic benefit can be institutionally guaranteed.

## Governance Models Balancing Multiple Stakeholder Interests

Institutional frameworks structuring creative industry value chains increasingly incorporate multi-stakeholder governance models designed to balance the interests of artists, cultural communities, commercial enterprises, government agencies, and civil society organizations. Public-private partnerships represent one important governance innovation through which cultural institutions can access both public resources and private investment while maintaining alignment with public benefit objectives[51]. The High Line in New York exemplifies successful PPP governance in which the city maintains ownership of the park while nearly 100 percent of annual operating costs come through private sector donations, creating institutional arrangements that secure long-term public control while leveraging private sector funding and expertise[51]. Similarly, the Pittsburgh Cultural Trust demonstrates how PPP governance can coordinate cross-sector investment to revitalize culturally significant urban areas, generating significant economic impact (estimated at 303 million dollars) while preserving and advancing cultural activities[51].

These governance models institutionalize accountability to multiple constituencies by requiring formal coordination mechanisms through which public sector representatives, private investors, nonprofit organizations, and community stakeholders negotiate priorities and ensure that economic development objectives align with cultural stewardship responsibilities. The institutional structures through which PPPs operate typically include governance boards with representation from multiple sectors, establishing formal decision-making processes through which conflicts between economic efficiency and cultural preservation can be negotiated. The effectiveness of these governance structures depends critically on the clarity of roles, conflict-resolution protocols, and mechanisms through which subordinated stakeholders—particularly artists and cultural communities whose labor and cultural expression generate the economic value—maintain meaningful voice in decisions affecting their work and cultural heritage[16].

Multi-stakeholder processes more broadly represent institutional frameworks through which diverse actors coordinate efforts to achieve sustainable development objectives while maintaining inclusivity and addressing power imbalances[58]. Effective multi-stakeholder governance requires that representation be meaningful rather than perfunctory, that decision-making processes be transparent, and that mechanisms exist through which disadvantaged stakeholders can exercise genuine influence over outcomes[58]. In creative industry contexts, this often requires explicit provisions ensuring that artists and cultural communities maintain substantive voice in policy decisions affecting their sectors. The institutional challenge lies in operationalizing these commitments in ways that actually shift power dynamics rather than simply creating new forums for discussion without corresponding shifts in resource allocation and decision authority.

## Collective Organization Models: Cooperatives and Artistic Collectives

Cooperative and collective organizational models represent institutional structures through which creative workers can aggregate their economic power, reduce individual financial risk, and maintain greater control over their creative output and commercial relationships. Art cooperatives and collectives, organized on principles of democratic membership and shared governance, enable artists to pool resources for shared studio space, equipment access, and marketing, while maintaining artistic autonomy and collective decision-making authority[37]. These organizations institutionalize principles of shared prosperity rather than zero-sum competition, creating institutional contexts in which cultural expression and economic sustainability can be pursued simultaneously without requiring subordination of creative integrity to commercial imperatives.

The institutional advantages of cooperative models extend to enhanced visibility and market presence—collective exhibitions and performances typically attract larger audiences than individual presentations, providing increased market access for individual members[37]. Furthermore, cooperative structures create accountability mechanisms through which individual members maintain influence over organizational decisions affecting their interests, establishing democratic institutional processes that contrast with hierarchical commercial enterprises in which artists typically exercise limited influence over decisions affecting their work and compensation[37]. Community engagement represents another significant institutional benefit of cooperative structures, as collectives frequently undertake projects involving public participation or addressing local issues, strengthening community ties and establishing creative organizations as important cultural institutions within their communities[37].

However, cooperative models also face significant institutional challenges, particularly regarding financial sustainability and internal governance. Many collectives struggle with securing stable funding, managing internal conflicts arising from differing artistic visions and commitment levels, and balancing the creative freedom that attracted members to the collective structure with the operational efficiency necessary for financial viability[37]. The success of cooperative institutions in balancing economic and cultural objectives depends critically on effective governance structures, clear agreements regarding decision-making processes and conflict resolution, and access to stable funding sources that reduce pressure for short-term commercial returns at the expense of cultural integrity.

## Government Support Programs and Creative Economy Legislation

Direct government support for creative industries through grants, subsidies, workforce development programs, and legislative frameworks represents an important category of institutional guarantee through which states attempt to ensure that creative sectors can sustain themselves economically while maintaining cultural authenticity. The United States has implemented comprehensive federal legislation addressing creative economy support, including the Creative Economy Revitalization Act, which authorizes 300 million dollars in grants to pay local creative workers through public creative projects[21]. The Promoting Local Arts and Creative Economy Workforce Act aims to bolster local creative economies and workers through amendments to existing legislation, new creative economy grants, and directed funding for creative businesses and underserved populations[21]. The Comprehensive Resources for Entrepreneurs in the Arts to Transform the Economy Act expands Small Business Administration and Economic Development Administration loan programs to serve creative entrepreneurs, institutionalizing recognition that creative businesses require comparable access to capital and technical assistance as other sectors[21].

These legislative frameworks institutionalize government responsibility for supporting creative sector sustainability, recognizing that creative industries generate significant economic value and employment but frequently face barriers in accessing traditional sources of financing due to the intangible nature of creative assets and perceived difficulty in assessing credit risk in creative ventures. By establishing dedicated funding mechanisms and expanding access to capital for creative businesses, these programs institutionalize recognition that creative industries warrant specific support rather than being expected to compete with other sectors for undifferentiated business support resources.

The European Union's Creative Europe Programme provides an important international example of government support for cultural and creative industries. Operating from 2014 through 2020 with a budget of 1.47 billion euros, and continuing through 2027, the programme supports cultural and creative organizations throughout EU member states and non-EU countries, institutionalizing a commitment that creative and cultural sectors warrant public investment and support[1]. Research examining European crowdfunding adoption for creative and cultural industries found that the presence of a central ministry with cultural competence positively influences the adoption of reward-based crowdfunding, suggesting that institutional recognition of cultural sectors through dedicated governmental structures facilitates the emergence and adoption of alternative financing mechanisms[1]. However, research also identified a nonlinear relationship between European grants and crowdfunding demand, with the finding that when EU grants exceed a critical value, they actually have a restraining effect on crowdfunding adoption, suggesting that institutional support mechanisms can potentially crowd out alternative financing if not calibrated carefully[1].

## Artist and Cultural Worker Rights Frameworks

Institutional frameworks explicitly establishing rights and protections for artists and cultural workers represent an important category of guarantee that formalizes recognition of creative labor's value and establishes minimum standards for working conditions and compensation. The Artist and Cultural Workers Bill of Rights establishes comprehensive protections including recognition of artists as cultural workers, rights to flexible work arrangements, fair wages, transparent compensation practices, intellectual property protections, and access to benefits typically afforded to other workers[28]. By institutionalizing these protections through formal legal frameworks, such bills create accountability mechanisms through which governments can enforce compliance and cultural organizations must acknowledge their obligations to artistic workers.

Federal employment protections for arts and humanities workers further institutionalize recognition of creative labor's value. The prevailing minimum compensation rules applying to organizations receiving funding from the National Endowment for the Arts or the National Endowment for the Humanities establish that professional performers and supporting personnel must receive compensation reflecting prevailing rates in their profession and geographic location[26]. These rules institutionalize the principle that creative labor cannot be treated as below-market, volunteer work when organizations receive public funding, establishing legal guarantees that public investment in arts and culture results in fair compensation for creative workers rather than subsidizing organizational overhead while workers remain underpaid[26].

Creative workforce development programs institutionalize government commitment to building human capital in creative industries through training and employment support. California's Creative Workforce Development Act of 2021, inspired by New Deal-era programs, provides employment and job training for artists and creative sector workers, explicitly prioritizing support for people from disadvantaged communities who might not otherwise have opportunities to pursue creative careers[44]. By institutionalizing this commitment through dedicated legislation and funding, the framework recognizes that creative talent exists across all social strata but that access to career pathways in creative industries remains constrained by existing economic inequalities and systematic barriers.

## Benefit Corporations and Social Enterprise Models

Benefit corporation legal structures represent an institutional innovation establishing that for-profit enterprises can legally pursue social and environmental objectives alongside profit maximization, with directors and officers receiving legal protection to prioritize public benefit even when doing so reduces shareholder returns[15][18]. Approximately thirty states and Washington D.C. have enacted benefit corporation legislation, enabling organizations to formally commit to pursuing social objectives as core components of their corporate purpose[15]. For creative enterprises, benefit corporation status enables organizations to legally establish that cultural preservation, artist support, and community development objectives constitute core purposes rather than secondary considerations subordinate to shareholder profit maximization[18].

The institutional advantages of benefit corporation status include clarity regarding corporate purpose, legal protection for directors acting in accordance with public benefit objectives, and third-party accountability mechanisms through which organizations report on their performance regarding stated social objectives[18]. The Massachusetts benefit corporation statute requires benefit corporations to appoint a "benefit director" responsible for drafting opinions regarding whether the organization acted in accordance with its public benefit purpose and what impact the corporation's status is having on its business, creating institutional accountability mechanisms through which stakeholders can assess whether stated commitments to cultural and social objectives are being realized in practice[18]. By institutionalizing transparency requirements and third-party assessment obligations, these frameworks attempt to create institutional guarantees that organizational commitment to social objectives remains substantive rather than merely performative.

## Collective Licensing and Rights Management Systems

Institutional systems for collective licensing of creative works represent important mechanisms through which individual creators can access the benefits of wide distribution while maintaining control over use terms and compensation. Collective management organizations enable authors, performers, producers, and other right holders to authorize professional CMOs to manage their rights, simplifying rights administration and facilitating efficient licensing while ensuring that rights holders receive appropriate compensation[23]. The WIPO Connect system represents an important institutional innovation enabling CMOs to manage operations locally while connecting to regional and international networks for works and right holder data exchanges, creating institutional infrastructure through which collective rights management can operate across borders without requiring individual creators to navigate international licensing arrangements[23].

These institutional systems become particularly important in creative contexts where individual creators cannot reasonably manage licensing directly—for example, radio stations cannot contact every composer and performer whose music might be played to negotiate individual licenses; instead, they obtain licenses through collective management organizations representing creators' interests[23]. By institutionalizing this collective licensing arrangement, CMO systems enable efficient markets for creative works while ensuring that creators receive compensation even when their work reaches audiences through complex distribution channels. The effectiveness of these systems in protecting creators' interests depends critically on the democratic governance of CMOs, transparency regarding royalty distribution methodologies, and accountability mechanisms through which member creators can assess whether CMOs are adequately representing their interests and distributing revenues fairly.

## Financing Mechanisms Including Crowdfunding and Alternative Finance

Institutional frameworks structuring alternative financing mechanisms, particularly reward-based crowdfunding, have emerged as important complements to traditional financial sources in creative industries. Research on crowdfunding adoption for cultural and creative industries reveals how national policies and cultural dimensions affect creative enterprises' financing strategies[1]. Crowdfunding in creative industries typically operates on a reward-based model where funders receive nonmonetary rewards or in-kind products based on their investment level, creating direct relationships between creators and supporters that can strengthen cultural resonance while generating production capital[1]. The reward-based crowdfunding market reached 159 million euros in 2017, with 35 percent of volume flowing to businesses in creative industries including arts, music, and design[1].

The institutional framework through which crowdfunding functions demonstrates how alternative financing can both complement and substitute for traditional sources, with research identifying that government grants can either encourage or discourage crowdfunding adoption depending on their magnitude and structure[1]. When government support reaches certain thresholds, it apparently reduces demand for crowdfunding by providing sufficient capital through traditional channels, whereas more moderate levels of government support may actually encourage crowdfunding adoption by establishing institutional recognition that creative industries warrant investment, thereby signaling market validity to potential crowdfunding supporters[1]. This demonstrates how institutional guarantees from government can interact with market-based mechanisms in complex ways, sometimes supporting and sometimes substituting for alternative financing arrangements.

The institutionalization of fintech solutions and digital payment platforms has facilitated crowdfunding and other alternative financing mechanisms, creating new techniques for assessing credit risk and managing information asymmetries that previously disadvantaged micro-firms and small-to-medium enterprises in creative industries[1]. By creating institutional infrastructure through which distributed funders can collectively finance creative projects while creators maintain greater autonomy than traditional venture capital or bank financing provides, crowdfunding institutionalizes a democratization of creative finance that can support cultural expression and artistic autonomy alongside economic sustainability.

## Transparency and Supply Chain Accountability Mechanisms

Institutional frameworks establishing supply chain transparency represent an increasingly important category of guarantee through which creative industry value chains can be monitored for compliance with established standards regarding fair compensation, cultural respect, and environmental sustainability. Supply chain transparency requires businesses to maintain visibility across the full range of vendors and partners involved in production, from sourcing of raw materials through manufacturing, distribution, and customer delivery[27]. For creative industries involving artisanal production, particularly in developing countries where cultural heritage products are created, supply chain transparency becomes critical for ensuring that economic benefits flow to original creators and communities rather than accumulating at later stages of value chain activity.

Key principles of supply chain transparency include knowing suppliers and understanding their operational practices, mapping complete value chains including all upstream and downstream relationships, collecting and sharing relevant data regarding production conditions and social impacts, establishing clear standards for supply chain practices, conducting regular verification and auditing, communicating transparently regarding supply chain challenges and progress, and leveraging technology for traceability[27]. For cultural industries, these mechanisms institutionalize recognition that ethical production practices matter as much as product quality, and that consumers and downstream businesses have obligations to understand how their cultural products were created and whether original creators and communities received fair compensation and had their cultural heritage respected.

Digital technology platforms including blockchain and Internet of Things devices enable unprecedented supply chain transparency, creating institutional infrastructure through which every step in creative value chains can theoretically be tracked and verified[27]. These technologies can document cultural authenticity by establishing provenance for traditional cultural products, verifying that products claiming cultural heritage origins actually come from the communities claiming cultural connections, and enabling direct traceability that prevents misrepresentation and cultural appropriation in commercial contexts. The institutional guarantee that blockchain and similar technologies provide rests on their immutability and transparency—once information is recorded in these systems, it cannot be retroactively altered, creating institutional confidence in the accuracy of supply chain information.

## Regulation of Digital Platforms and Streaming Compensation

Institutional frameworks regulating digital music streaming platforms represent an important emerging category of guarantee through which creative industries are attempting to ensure fair compensation as creative consumption increasingly occurs through digital channels. The economics of music streaming reveal systemic inequities in how compensation flows through value chains, with artists on average earning less than one-tenth of a cent per stream on platforms like Spotify[31][34]. These inequities arise from several institutional factors, including the dominance of major record labels in negotiating with streaming platforms, the "pro-rata" royalty distribution model that pools revenue from all subscribers and distributes it proportional to total streams (thereby favoring major artists with enormous audiences), and the opacity of compensation mechanisms through which intermediaries extract significant portions of revenue[34].

Institutional reforms attempted in several jurisdictions seek to address these inequities. France's 2022 agreement establishing minimum streaming royalty rates represents an important institutional innovation institutionalizing that artists will receive compensation proportionate to their streams, creating legal guarantees that streaming platform revenues must be distributed according to established minimum standards[34]. This framework institutionalizes recognition that digital streaming fundamentally altered music economics but that existing institutional arrangements failed to adapt compensation mechanisms to ensure fair returns to creators. Similarly, legislative proposals in the United States, including Representative Rashida Tlaib's resolution establishing that it is the "duty of the federal government to establish a new royalty program" to pay artists on streaming platforms, institutionalize recognition that government intervention may be necessary to ensure that private platform control over creative distribution does not result in systematic underpayment of creators[34].

Alternative payment models, including user-centric streaming approaches in which subscribers' fees go directly to artists they listen to rather than being pooled and distributed proportionally, represent institutional innovations attempting to address systemic inequities built into current streaming economics[34]. While user-centric models have received limited adoption, the institutional framework underlying their development institutionalizes recognition that streaming economics need not operate according to current pro-rata models, and that alternative institutional arrangements could distribute compensation more fairly to independent artists and niche creators who suffer most under current arrangements[34].

## International Agreements and Multilateral Frameworks

International institutional frameworks including UNESCO conventions and World Intellectual Property Organization agreements provide important guarantees through which creative industries and cultural heritage are protected across national boundaries. The UNESCO Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions, adopted in 2005, established a milestone recognition of the dual nature—both cultural and economic—of contemporary creative expressions, providing a framework through which member states can design policies protecting cultural diversity while supporting creative industries[19]. The convention affirms states' sovereign right to adopt policies protecting and promoting cultural diversity, establishing international recognition that cultural preservation is a legitimate governmental objective that need not subordinate to pure market mechanisms[59]. By institutionalizing this principle at the international level, the convention provides protection for national-level policies that might otherwise be challenged as barriers to free trade, creating international institutional guarantees that states can legally prioritize cultural objectives even when doing so constrains international commerce in cultural products.

The WIPO Copyright Treaty and related agreements provide international institutional frameworks through which copyright protection extends across borders, creating mechanisms through which creators can obtain protection in multiple jurisdictions without separately registering copyrights in each country[36]. By institutionalizing reciprocal copyright protection through international agreements, these frameworks ensure that creators' economic interests receive protection as their works circulate internationally, creating institutional guarantees that cultural products created in one jurisdiction receive protection in other jurisdictions adhering to these international frameworks.

## Benefit Sharing and Cultural Preservation Integration

Institutional frameworks explicitly linking benefit-sharing to cultural preservation represent important recent developments recognizing that economic benefits must be strategically directed toward activities that maintain cultural continuity rather than simply distributed to individuals without regard to cultural consequences. Research on benefit-sharing frameworks for traditional knowledge and cultural heritage demonstrates that non-monetary benefits—including joint research agreements, institutional recognition, and educational support for cultural transmission—can be as important as financial benefits for cultural preservation[39]. When pharmaceutical or agricultural companies partner with indigenous communities for traditional knowledge, institutional arrangements providing joint research and development support validate traditional knowledge systems by placing them on equal footing with Western scientific methods, creating institutional recognition of knowledge sovereignty alongside financial compensation[39].

These frameworks institutionalize recognition that cultural preservation requires active investment in the cultural institutions and practices that maintain traditions across generations. By directing benefit-sharing arrangements toward capacity-building, language revitalization programs, and institutional support for cultural knowledge-holders, these frameworks institutionalize links between economic value extraction and cultural sustainability, creating institutional guarantees that communities receiving benefits from commercial exploitation of their cultural heritage can reinvest revenues in the cultural practices and institutions necessary for cultural continuity[39].

## Challenges and Persistent Institutional Gaps

Despite the proliferation of institutional frameworks designed to balance economic benefits with cultural heritage protection, significant gaps and challenges remain. Information asymmetries continue to disadvantage artists and cultural communities in negotiations with commercial enterprises, with creative workers frequently lacking the bargaining power to demand terms reflecting the cultural and economic value of their contributions[31]. The systematic underpayment of independent and emerging artists in music streaming platforms demonstrates how institutional arrangements ostensibly designed to compensate creators can effectively extract value from creative work without proportionate compensation[31][34].

Digital copyright enforcement presents ongoing institutional challenges, as traditional intellectual property frameworks designed for analog environments struggle to address digital reproduction and distribution[4]. The scope and application of intellectual property rights in digital environments remain unclear, with digital reproduction, multiple sharing, and new business models like streaming representing values in the digital domain but challenges for copyright enforcement[4]. This institutional uncertainty can facilitate unauthorized appropriation of cultural expressions while creating compliance difficulties for legitimate commercial enterprises attempting to respect cultural intellectual property rights.

Power imbalances between creative workers and commercial enterprises persist despite institutional frameworks designed to address them. Antitrust laws preventing independent artists from collectively bargaining with streaming platforms institutionalize their disadvantage, enabling platforms to maintain asymmetric negotiating power while individual artists lack collective bargaining capacity[31]. These systemic institutional arrangements effectively guarantee that individual artists will be unable to negotiate fair compensation, even where institutional frameworks ostensibly protect their rights.

## Conclusion: Toward Integrated Institutional Frameworks

Ensuring that creative industry value chains balance economic benefits with cultural heritage preservation requires integrated institutional frameworks operating across multiple levels—international agreements, national legislation, sectoral standards, organizational governance structures, and individual contract terms. No single institutional mechanism can adequately address the complexity of balancing economic sustainability with cultural stewardship; rather, effective institutional guarantees emerge from combinations of intellectual property protection, collective management systems, benefit corporations and cooperative structures, government support programs, artist rights frameworks, certification systems, and governance models incorporating multiple stakeholder perspectives.

The most effective institutional frameworks appear to be those that institutionalize ongoing dialogue and negotiation among stakeholders with different interests rather than attempting to establish fixed solutions to the creative-commercial tension. Institutional mechanisms through which artists and cultural communities maintain meaningful voice in decisions affecting their work—whether through cooperative governance, benefit corporation status, or multi-stakeholder policy processes—create accountability structures encouraging ongoing attention to cultural values alongside economic imperatives. Transparency mechanisms enabling stakeholders to understand how value flows through creative supply chains become increasingly critical as creative industries grow more complex and globalized.

Moving forward, institutional strengthening in creative industries requires particular attention to ensuring that digitalization does not further concentrate power among platform operators while dispersing economic benefits away from creators, that developing country creative sectors can access institutional support enabling them to capture increased value rather than remaining trapped in extractive relationships, and that cultural communities can exercise meaningful control over how their heritage is commercialized and represented globally. By institutionalizing principles of transparency, democratic participation, fair compensation, and cultural respect as foundational to creative industry governance, societies can work toward value chains that authentically balance creative expression's cultural significance with its economic importance, ensuring that creativity remains a source of both cultural vitality and shared prosperity across multiple stakeholders.
