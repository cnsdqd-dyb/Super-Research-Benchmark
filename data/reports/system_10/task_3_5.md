# Balancing Fear Activation and Treatment Dropout in Exposure-Based CBT for PTSD: A Comprehensive Clinical and Theoretical Analysis

## Executive Summary

The tension between adequate fear activation and treatment dropout represents one of the most significant clinical challenges in delivering exposure-based cognitive behavioral therapy for posttraumatic stress disorder. While emotional processing theory establishes that fear structure activation is essential for therapeutic change[1][10], exposure therapy paradoxically carries one of the highest dropout rates among evidence-based PTSD treatments, with rates ranging from 24% to 40% depending on the specific protocol and patient population[2][6]. This apparent contradiction stems from clinicians' understandable concerns that distress during exposure will drive clients away from treatment, yet research increasingly demonstrates that fear activation and emotional engagement actually predict better outcomes and, counterintuitively, may protect against dropout when properly managed. This report synthesizes current evidence regarding the mechanisms underlying fear activation, the temporal patterns and predictors of dropout, and clinical strategies that successfully navigate this critical balance. The evidence indicates that successful treatment implementation requires sophisticated assessment of engagement levels, strategic titration of therapist directiveness, careful session scheduling and structuring, attention to the therapeutic relationship, and honest communication about treatment expectations to optimize both the conditions necessary for change and the likelihood that clients will remain engaged through the full course of therapy.

## Emotional Processing Theory and the Necessity of Fear Activation

### The Theoretical Foundation for Fear Activation in Exposure Therapy

Emotional processing theory (EPT), which forms the theoretical foundation for prolonged exposure therapy, proposes that pathological fear and other emotional structures develop and persist after trauma, and that these maladaptive emotional structures must be modified for recovery to occur[10]. According to EPT and the bioinformational model of emotion upon which it is based, fear is represented in memory as a cognitive structure containing three core elements: representations of feared stimuli, corresponding fear responses and physiological reactions, and meanings or beliefs associated with both the stimuli and responses[10]. In the context of posttraumatic stress disorder, traumatic memories are structured emotional representations that encode the stimuli present during the trauma, the individual's emotional and physiological responses such as fear, guilt, shame, or freezing, and the meanings attributed to those experiences[10]. Critically, in PTSD, the trauma memory contains an excessive number of stimuli erroneously linked to danger, and individuals develop maladaptive beliefs such as "the world is entirely dangerous" or "I am incompetent," which serve to maintain PTSD symptoms[10].

The central mechanism by which exposure therapy effects change, according to EPT, is the activation of this pathological fear structure combined with the introduction of new information incompatible with the existing fear structure[10][1]. When the fear structure is activated through exposure to feared stimuli—whether through imaginal revisiting of trauma memories or in vivo confrontation with avoided situations—the individual experiences both subjective distress and physiological arousal. This activation is theoretically necessary because it allows for the integration of disconfirming information that would otherwise not be accessible to the existing fear structure[10]. Specifically, as individuals approach and remain in contact with feared stimuli without experiencing the anticipated negative consequences, they accumulate evidence that contradicts their maladaptive threat beliefs, which gradually modifies the fear structure through a process of new learning[10][11].

### Empirical Evidence for Fear Activation as a Predictor of Treatment Outcome

The relationship between fear activation and treatment outcome has received substantial empirical investigation, with mixed but generally supportive findings. In emotional processing theory, Foa and Kozak predicted that successful treatment would be characterized by initial fear activation, measured either through subjective distress ratings or physiological indicators such as heart rate, followed by within-session habituation (a decrease in distress over the course of an exposure session) and between-session habituation (progressive decreases in peak distress from one session to the next)[1][11]. Among these proposed mechanisms, between-session habituation of fear responding to trauma-related stimuli has shown the most consistent association with treatment outcome, although several studies have also found support for the importance of initial fear activation[39].

Research examining written exposure therapy for PTSD found that initial activation of physiological fear response during the first exposure session was significantly associated with treatment gains[7]. Specifically, individuals who showed greater increases in physiological arousal during the first exposure session also showed the largest subsequent treatment response, suggesting that fear activation may serve as a marker of engagement with the trauma material necessary for therapeutic change[7]. This finding aligns with earlier research by Foa and colleagues demonstrating that initial fear activation was associated with positive response to PTSD treatment[1][7]. However, the relationship between fear activation and outcome is not uniformly linear; some research suggests that moderate rather than extreme levels of arousal may be optimal, and that the capacity to tolerate and engage with distress is more important than the absolute level of distress experienced[11][33].

### The Distinction Between Underengagement and Overengagement

Contrary to common clinical expectations, one of the most significant barriers to effective exposure therapy is not client overengagement but rather underengagement with trauma-related material[1][34]. Underengagement occurs when clients provide what has been described as a "rote or sanitized version" of the traumatic event that fails to evoke strong emotions, which is problematic because fear activation must occur for exposure to be effective[1]. From a clinical perspective, underengagement represents a form of covert avoidance in which clients appear to be participating in exposure while actually maintaining emotional distance from the trauma material. For instance, a client might describe a traumatic assault in a detached, factual manner without accessing the emotional responses that were present during the event or that the memory currently triggers[1]. Such underengagement prevents the activation necessary for new learning to occur.

In contrast, overengagement occurs when a client becomes so immersed in the trauma memory that they lose contact with the present therapeutic relationship and begin to relive rather than revisit the event[34]. When a client is overengaged, they may appear to be "out of control" and not present with the therapist in the room, as though they are actually back experiencing the trauma rather than discussing it from a position of safety[34]. While overengagement represents full emotional activation of the trauma material, it can paradoxically undermine the therapeutic process because the client's cognitive resources are consumed by the reliving experience rather than available for learning and meaning-making[34]. Additionally, extreme overengagement may increase the likelihood that a client experiences such intolerable distress that they terminate treatment[1][34].

The optimal level of engagement has been characterized as a state in which the client is "connected to the memory on an emotional level but is also aware that he or she is safely grounded in the present," experiencing significant yet tolerable distress[34]. This optimal engagement state allows for fear activation sufficient to promote new learning while maintaining the client's capacity to engage with the therapist and retain a sense of safety and control. Clinical skill in recognizing these distinctions and titrating the client's level of engagement represents one of the most important competencies for effective exposure therapy delivery[34].

## Dropout in Exposure-Based PTSD Treatment: Prevalence, Timing, and Patterns

### Dropout Rates Across Exposure-Based Treatments

Treatment dropout represents a significant challenge across evidence-based PTSD interventions, with particularly high rates in exposure-based treatments. A meta-analysis examining dropout from specific PTSD treatment approaches in military populations found that the weighted dropout rate for all PTSD interventions was 25.6%, but substantial variation existed across treatments[2]. Treatments linked with higher dropout included weekly formats of cognitive processing therapy (40.1%) and prolonged exposure therapy (34.7%), while lower dropout was associated with present-centered therapy (16.1%), mindfulness-based stress reduction (17.4%), and group-based exposure therapy (6.9%)[2]. Notably, intensive outpatient programs yielded the lowest dropout rates overall, even when they included CPT (8.5%) or PE (5.5%)[2]. In civilian populations, meta-analyses have reported average dropout rates of 24% for prolonged exposure and 29% for cognitive processing therapy[23]. In real-world clinical settings outside of research protocols, dropout rates are often even higher, with some studies reporting dropout rates approaching 50% in certain clinic settings[3].

When examining military populations specifically, research using individual participant data meta-analysis found that the overall dropout rate from cognitive-behavioral therapy with a trauma focus (CBT-TF) was higher than previously reported in some studies, with military personnel and veterans significantly more likely to drop out compared with civilians, with a relative risk of 1.73[5]. Younger age emerged as one of the most consistent predictors of dropout, with veterans aged 18-29 showing significantly higher dropout risk compared to those aged 60-69[5]. Among 2,606 veterans treated through the Veterans Health Administration PE Training Program, 30% completed fewer than 8 sessions, which is considered a minimum therapeutic dose[6]. These patterns highlight that while exposure therapy remains highly effective for those who complete treatment, engagement and retention represent substantial clinical challenges.

### Temporal Patterns of Dropout: When Clients Leave Treatment

Understanding when dropout occurs during the course of treatment provides critical information for targeting interventions. The greatest risk for dropout frequently occurs before treatment even begins, with pre-treatment attrition ranging from 16% to 50% across studies[56]. Among those who initiate treatment, the majority who drop out do so during early sessions, particularly before the halfway point of the intervention protocol[5]. Specifically, in prolonged exposure therapy, the point of highest risk for dropout during active treatment corresponds to the beginning of imaginal exposures[55]. This pattern is particularly notable because it suggests that dropout is often triggered not simply by overall treatment demands, but by the specific moment when clients must directly confront their traumatic memories through imaginal exposure, the core element designed to activate the fear structure[55].

In cognitive processing therapy, the single "riskiest" point for dropout was found to occur between sessions two and three, with the majority of discontinuations (55.7%) occurring between sessions two and five[55]. Among a sample of CPT participants, the mean number of treatment sessions completed for those who dropped out was 4.91 sessions compared with 13.66 sessions for treatment completers[5]. This timing suggests that dropout often occurs during the phase when clients are beginning to engage with their trauma narratives, which is when emotional activation begins to intensify. For those who drop out, the decision often comes before substantial therapeutic progress has occurred, suggesting that early intervention to address retention concerns is critical[5].

### Predictors of Dropout: Clinical Characteristics and In-Session Factors

Research has identified multiple factors associated with increased dropout risk, though notably, demographic and baseline clinical characteristics often prove less predictive than factors related to engagement during early treatment sessions. Among demographic variables, younger age has emerged as one of the most consistent and robust predictors of dropout[5][6]. Among baseline clinical characteristics, higher levels of pretreatment avoidance have been associated with increased dropout in some studies[23], though the relationship between baseline symptom severity and dropout has proven inconsistent across investigations. Some research suggests that more catastrophic cognitions, presence of anger, current partner conflict, and impairment in social functioning predict higher dropout[32].

More recent research examining in-session processes has revealed more specific and actionable predictors of dropout. In cognitive processing therapy, observational coding of therapy sessions revealed that more physiological distress and cognitive-emotional processing predicted lower dropout risk, whereas more avoidance predicted higher dropout[32]. Specifically, patients showing high levels of physiological distress and cognitive-emotional processing were approximately 83-87% less likely to drop out[32][54]. This counterintuitive finding—that more physiological distress predicts lower dropout—appears to reflect that physiological distress indicates genuine emotional engagement with the trauma material, which paradoxically increases commitment to treatment, perhaps because clients recognize they are actually engaging with their trauma rather than avoiding it[32].

In contrast, avoidance behaviors during early treatment sessions—such as declining to provide trauma narratives, providing minimal or emotionally disconnected accounts, or demonstrating prolonged pauses and emotional suppression—predicted higher dropout[32]. Similarly, overgeneralization and exaggerated threat beliefs predicted higher dropout, while ruminative processing of trauma content in certain contexts predicted lower dropout[32][23]. These patterns suggest that clients who engage emotionally and cognitively with their trauma material, even when that engagement generates physiological distress, are more likely to continue in treatment, whereas those who maintain emotional distance or avoid engaging with trauma-related content are more likely to terminate prematurely[32].

## The Paradox: Fear Activation as Both Treatment Necessity and Dropout Risk

### The Clinician's Concern About Distress and Dropout

One of the most significant barriers to the implementation of exposure therapy exists not in the clients themselves, but in the beliefs and concerns of clinicians[1][31]. Among therapists new to exposure therapy, perhaps the greatest concern is that asking clients to revisit trauma memories and confront feared situations will result in such severe distress that clients will experience "overengagement," manifest as extreme anxiety reactions, dissociation, or panic, leading to treatment discontinuation[1]. This concern is so common that it has been identified as one of the principal barriers to clinician adoption and utilization of exposure therapy across diverse settings[47].

However, empirical evidence suggests that this clinician concern, while understandable, may reflect an inaccurate understanding of both typical client responses and the relationship between distress and dropout. Research indicates that clients more commonly demonstrate underengagement rather than overengagement when initially approaching trauma-related memories[1]. Furthermore, contrary to the intuitive expectation that high distress would drive dropout, evidence suggests that appropriate fear activation during early sessions may actually predict treatment completion rather than dropout[35]. This counterintuitive finding appears to reflect that when clients experience manageable but genuine emotional engagement with trauma material, they recognize the treatment is "working" and become more committed to continuing, whereas clients who experience minimal distress or who successfully avoid activating their fear structure may question whether the treatment is beneficial[1][35].

### The Temporal Dynamics of Distress and Learning

Understanding the temporal dynamics between distress experience and learning outcomes illuminates why fear activation and completion are not necessarily antagonistic. Research examining within-session and between-session habituation patterns found that lower activation and less habituation during the first exposure session were associated with increased treatment discontinuation[35]. This pattern suggests that clients who do not sufficiently activate their fear systems during initial exposures—that is, who maintain their avoidance patterns even within the therapeutic context—are subsequently more likely to drop out, possibly because they perceive the treatment as ineffective or unnecessary[35]. In contrast, clients who experience activation sufficient to trigger the learning processes underlying fear extinction appear more likely to continue, recognizing progress and developing confidence in the therapeutic approach.

The distinction between initial activation in early sessions and the pattern of change across sessions further clarifies this relationship. While initial activation in early exposures predicts better outcomes and lower dropout, the same relationships do not necessarily hold for later sessions[35]. This suggests that the "therapeutic work" of exposure involves different processes at different treatment stages: early sessions require sufficient activation to engage the fear system and initiate learning, while later sessions involve consolidating that learning and managing any residual distress. Clinicians must therefore calibrate their approach to ensure adequate activation early in treatment while remaining sensitive to the timing and nature of distress as treatment progresses[35].

## Clinical Strategies for Optimizing the Balance Between Fear Activation and Retention

### Session Frequency and Treatment Structure as Retention Factors

Emerging evidence suggests that relatively modifiable aspects of treatment structure, particularly session frequency, significantly influence dropout rates and may represent an accessible lever for improving retention without sacrificing the conditions necessary for fear activation. A meta-analysis examining the relationship between prolonged exposure frequency and dropout across 35 randomized controlled trials with 1,508 adults found that prescribing PE sessions at least twice weekly was associated with a significantly lower dropout rate compared with weekly sessions (21% versus 34%)[3]. This effect remained significant even after controlling for numerous study-level and participant-level characteristics including demographics, clinical characteristics, and study quality[3].

The mechanism by which twice-weekly sessions reduce dropout likely involves multiple factors. More frequent sessions may reduce the period of time between exposures during which clients are vulnerable to rehearsing avoidance and developing reasons to discontinue, while also maintaining therapeutic momentum and the client's engagement with the treatment rationale[3]. Additionally, more frequent sessions may reduce the likelihood that practical barriers such as transportation, work schedules, or competing life demands interfere with treatment continuation[3]. Importantly, the finding that twice-weekly sessions reduce dropout without compromising—and potentially enhancing—treatment outcomes appears to result from more intensive treatment facilitating more rapid cognitive and emotional processing of trauma material[3].

Beyond frequency, alternative treatment structures have shown promise for reducing dropout while maintaining efficacy. Massed exposure therapy, in which clients receive multiple sessions over a concentrated time period (for example, 10 sessions over two weeks), has demonstrated both low dropout rates and strong treatment outcomes[3][60]. Additionally, written exposure therapy, a briefer protocol involving five sessions focused on written rather than imaginal exposure to trauma memories, has demonstrated noninferiority to prolonged exposure while showing substantially lower dropout rates (6.4% compared to approximately 34-40% in standard PE and CPT protocols)[58]. These findings suggest that clinicians should consider how treatment structure and scheduling might be individualized to reduce practical barriers and maintain engagement, while ensuring that whatever structure is adopted includes sufficient frequency and intensity of contact to support the fear activation necessary for therapeutic change[3].

### Therapist Directiveness and Engagement Management

One of the most important clinical skills for balancing fear activation and retention involves calibrating the therapist's level of directiveness and control during exposure sessions. Contrary to some conceptualizations of exposure therapy as requiring high therapist directiveness, current understanding emphasizes a more nuanced and flexible approach in which the therapist dynamically adjusts directiveness based on the client's current level of engagement[1][34][49]. The general principle is that less therapist directiveness is often more therapeutically powerful because it allows clients to focus on aspects of the trauma memory most salient to their own experience and to arrive at new understandings and beliefs through their own cognitive processing rather than through therapist-provided interpretations[1][34][49].

During imaginal exposure specifically, the therapist's role involves active but subtle monitoring and titration of the client's engagement level[34]. When clients demonstrate underengagement—providing emotionally flat or abbreviated descriptions of trauma without accessing strong emotions—the therapist should use techniques to increase engagement, such as shifting from present to past tense during recounting (which increases immersion in the memory), encouraging the use of more sensory details, or asking probing questions designed to direct focus toward the most emotionally evocative or threatening aspects of the memory (termed "hot spots")[1]. However, this probing should be calibrated to the client's readiness; new therapists frequently err toward excessive directiveness, which can evoke feelings of being controlled or not heard, potentially impairing confidence in the exposure process and undermining treatment commitment[1][34].

When clients demonstrate overengagement, becoming so immersed in reliving the trauma that they lose present awareness, the therapist should employ techniques to reduce engagement and maintain safety, such as encouraging the client to open their eyes, briefly stepping out of the imaginal focus, or reminding the client of their current location and safety[34]. Importantly, rather than stopping the imaginal exposure entirely when overengagement occurs, experienced therapists first apply a range of strategies to help the client reduce engagement to an optimal level and successfully complete the exposure[1].

### Processing and Cognitive Work Following Imaginal Exposure

The processing phase following imaginal exposure represents another critical opportunity to balance emotional activation with cognitive consolidation and meaning-making, and this phase also requires careful attention to therapist directiveness[1][34][49]. After each imaginal exposure, the therapist and client engage in "processing," discussing how the exposure went, what it was like for the client, and what unhelpful thoughts or beliefs may have arisen[1]. During this processing phase, the therapist highlights meaningful work the client did during exposure, helps to address key themes emerging from the exposure, and asks open-ended questions to guide examination and shifting of beliefs thought to be central to maintaining the client's PTSD[1].

A common error among less experienced therapists is to do too much of the interpretive work during processing, essentially telling clients what they should think or feel rather than allowing clients to arrive at conclusions on their own[1][34][49]. Client-directed, "organic" shifts in beliefs that emerge from the client's own reflection following exposure typically produce more durable cognitive change than those directly suggested or provided by the therapist[1]. Additionally, when therapists do too much of the meaning-making work, they may inadvertently position themselves as experts and the client as passive recipients, which can undermine the collaborative nature of therapy and the client's sense of agency in their recovery[49]. Instead, effective processing involves the therapist asking guided questions that direct the client's attention toward disconfirming evidence and new learning, allowing the client to articulate their own updated beliefs and understandings.

### The Therapeutic Alliance and Collaborative Treatment Planning

The quality of the therapeutic alliance—the collaborative relationship between therapist and client characterized by mutual understanding, trust, and agreement on treatment goals and methods—emerges as a critical factor influencing both engagement with the challenging work of exposure and continuation in treatment[12]. Research examining the relationship between therapeutic alliance and dropout in cognitive processing therapy found that mean ratings of alliance significantly predicted treatment dropout, with clients reporting weaker overall alliances more likely to discontinue treatment[9]. Importantly, initial alliance (at the first session) did not predict dropout, nor did late alliance (at the final session), suggesting that the overall quality of the relationship across treatment sessions, rather than any single time point, predicts engagement[9].

This finding underscores that dropout risk is not fixed at the beginning of treatment but rather remains dynamic and responsive to the quality of the therapeutic relationship as it develops and fluctuates through various phases of treatment[9]. During earlier sessions, therapists work to establish safety and trust while introducing the treatment rationale. During middle phases when exposure work intensifies, the alliance may be tested as clients encounter the distress of confronting their trauma. During later sessions, alliance may shift again as clients reflect on progress and consolidate their learning[9]. Maintaining a strong alliance throughout these fluctuations appears essential for retention.

Collaborative treatment planning represents a concrete manifestation of the alliance that specifically addresses the balance between fear activation and engagement[49][50]. Research on patient engagement in PTSD treatment emphasizes that shared decision-making, in which providers and patients collaboratively review treatment options and decide on a treatment plan, results in greater treatment initiation and retention than clinician-driven treatment planning[50]. This collaborative approach extends to decisions about session frequency, the specific traumas to target, the pace of exposure progression, and the techniques to be employed[40]. When clients feel respected as experts on their own experience and participate in determining how treatment will proceed, they are more likely to remain committed to the process, even when that process requires them to experience manageable distress[1][34][49][50].

### Pre-Treatment Preparation and Expectation Setting

The investment in thorough pre-treatment preparation and explicit discussion of what to expect during exposure therapy significantly influences both dropout and the quality of engagement[1][44]. Psychoeducation at the beginning of treatment should include detailed information about PTSD, the theoretical rationale for exposure therapy, the specific components of the protocol, what clients can expect to experience during sessions, the temporary nature of any distress that occurs, and the ways in which symptoms typically improve across the course of treatment[1][40][49].

Importantly, this psychoeducation should address clients' beliefs about treatment, as research demonstrates that client beliefs about treatment mechanisms, effectiveness, and the necessity of engaging with trauma material represent some of the strongest predictors of whether individuals will choose and remain engaged in exposure therapy[44]. Clients who believe that revisiting trauma is necessary for recovery, that exposure therapy addresses the root causes of PTSD rather than just symptom management, and that the therapist's role is to guide them through a process of recovery in which they themselves do the crucial work of confronting their trauma, demonstrate stronger commitment to the treatment[44]. In contrast, clients who hold beliefs that exposure therapy will only make things worse, that avoiding trauma reminders is the best strategy, or that the therapist should direct the process without their input are at higher risk for dropout[44].

Clinicians should therefore use the initial sessions to understand clients' preexisting treatment beliefs and incorporate these beliefs into the treatment rationale, working to align the rationale with clients' own values and goals[44]. For instance, if a client values independence, the therapist might frame exposure as developing the client's own mastery over their trauma rather than relying on the therapist or on avoidance strategies. If a client values family, exposure might be framed as enabling them to reengage with valued relationships and social roles. This individualized approach to treatment rationale increases the likelihood that clients will persevere through the challenging early phases of treatment[1][44].

### Addressing Comorbidities and Emotion Regulation Skills

For clients with significant comorbidities, low distress tolerance, or limited emotion regulation capacity, the balance between fear activation and retention may require supplementary interventions. Some research suggests that for complex cases or those with significant emotion dysregulation, emotion regulation skills training can be integrated with exposure to reduce overwhelm and increase completion[22]. This approach differs from pure exposure by temporarily prioritizing the development of coping strategies and distress tolerance alongside exposure, potentially allowing clients to engage more fully with fear activation once they have confidence in their capacity to manage the resulting distress[22][33].

Research on distress tolerance reveals that individuals' perceived or actual ability to tolerate negative emotional states significantly influences their capacity to engage with exposure and their risk of dropout[33]. For those with particularly low distress tolerance, preliminary interventions targeting distress tolerance—through psychoeducation about tolerance versus elimination of distress, and through graduated practice tolerating manageable distress—may facilitate subsequent engagement with exposure[33]. This suggests a sequential or integrated approach in which clinicians assess distress tolerance early and, when it appears to be a significant limitation, invest initial sessions in building tolerance while also beginning to engage with exposure material at a pace that remains challenging but manageable[33].

## The Emerging Understanding of Rapid Improvement and Treatment Discontinuation

### The Good-Enough Level Model and Early Termination

Recent research examining whether treatment dropout necessarily represents treatment failure has challenged some traditional assumptions about the relationship between session completion and outcome. The dose-response model of psychotherapy proposes a linear relationship whereby each additional therapy session provides incremental benefit, such that more sessions should consistently produce better outcomes. In contrast, the good-enough level model suggests that some clients improve rapidly and may reasonably discontinue when they have achieved a satisfactory level of symptom reduction, even if this occurs before the complete protocol[43].

Research examining dropout patterns in both prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy found evidence supporting the dose-response model, with patients who completed more sessions demonstrating greater likelihood of achieving reliable clinically significant improvement[55][43]. However, notably, 13-37% of participants who discontinued before completing the full protocol had achieved clinically significant improvement, suggesting that for some subset of clients, early termination does not represent a treatment failure[43][55]. When examining the timing of highest dropout risk in PE, the peak corresponded to the beginning of imaginal exposures, which represents the point at which the core therapeutic mechanism of fear activation through vivid reliving of trauma begins[55]. This suggests that some dropout may reflect clients perceiving they have achieved adequate gains from early psychoeducation and behavioral activation before imaginal exposure intensifies, rather than withdrawal in response to overwhelming distress[43].

Understanding these patterns has important implications for balancing fear activation and retention. It suggests that clinicians should not assume all dropout represents treatment failure, but rather should seek to understand each client's decision to discontinue[43]. For some clients, early termination following initial improvement in avoidance and physiological hyperarousal may indeed represent a reasonable treatment endpoint, particularly if reassessment reveals that they have achieved adequate functional improvement[43]. However, clinicians should also be able to distinguish between clients who are appropriately satisfied with adequate improvement and those who are withdrawing in response to avoidance or overwhelm. Research indicates that clients who interpreted symptom worsening as part of treatment continued, while those who viewed worsening as a sign that treatment was not working discontinued[32]. This suggests that explicit discussion of the normality of some symptom fluctuation and reframing of temporary worsening as part of the therapeutic process may facilitate continued engagement[32][43].

## Emerging Adaptations and Innovations for Balancing Engagement and Retention

### Peer Support and Social Factors in Maintaining Engagement

One novel approach to addressing dropout involves leveraging peer support during the in vivo exposure homework component of PE. Research indicates that approximately half of veterans who had dropped out of PE reported they would be more likely to complete treatment if a peer who had completed treatment successfully were available to help with in vivo exposure homework[41]. This finding has prompted pilot research testing structured peer support protocols in which veterans who have completed PE are paired with veterans currently in treatment at high risk for dropout, with the peer providing encouragement and support during in vivo exposure exercises[41]. Early data suggest that over 50% of those who had dropped out agreed to return to treatment when peer support was offered[41], suggesting that social support and reduced isolation during the challenging homework components of treatment can meaningfully enhance retention[41].

This approach aligns with broader research on patient engagement indicating that supportive relationships create a sense of safety and trust, which can reduce avoidance and foster engagement with therapy[22]. From the perspective of balancing fear activation and retention, peer support may enhance clients' confidence that they can tolerate the distress associated with in vivo exposure while maintaining engagement and forward progress. Additionally, peers who have successfully completed treatment can model that the distress experienced during exposure is tolerable and temporary, and can provide concrete evidence that completing treatment leads to meaningful symptom improvement[41].

### Intensive Outpatient Formats and Treatment Accessibility

The substantially lower dropout rates observed in intensive outpatient programs (IOPs) that included CPT (8.5%) or PE (5.5%), despite these programs containing the same core therapeutic elements as standard weekly outpatient treatment, point to the potential value of more intensive treatment formats[2]. While the mechanisms underlying the lower dropout in IOPs likely involve multiple factors—including the structured daily schedule, frequent therapist contact, intensive clinical monitoring, and potentially stronger therapeutic relationships developed through more frequent contact—these findings suggest that for some clients, particularly those at very high risk for dropout, intensive formats may offer advantages[2].

Notably, massed exposure therapy, in which 10 sessions of PE are compressed into a two-week period, has demonstrated low dropout rates and strong treatment outcomes[60], suggesting that clients may experience reduced dropout when treatment is provided intensively rather than across multiple months. This pattern seems particularly pronounced for clients with practical barriers to treatment engagement, such as those with complex work or family obligations, those with limited transportation, or those in geographic regions with limited mental health access[3]. For such clients, intensive formats may reduce the cumulative practical burden of treatment while maintaining the frequency of exposure necessary for fear activation and learning[3][60].

### Alternative Exposure Formats and Written Exposure Therapy

Written exposure therapy represents an important innovation relevant to the balance between fear activation and retention. WET involves five sessions of focused written exposure to trauma memories, with repeated writing about the same trauma and processing of the written narratives[37][58]. A randomized clinical trial directly comparing written exposure therapy to prolonged exposure found that despite the substantially lower treatment intensity (five 30-60 minute sessions versus 8-15 90-minute sessions), WET demonstrated noninferiority to PE on PTSD symptom outcomes[58]. Critically, treatment retention was significantly better for WET participants compared to PE[58]. These findings suggest that for some clients, particularly those struggling with the sustained emotional intensity required for imaginal exposure across 90-minute sessions, a brief written format may engage the necessary mechanisms for change while reducing the overwhelm that might otherwise drive dropout[58].

The mechanisms by which written exposure achieves therapeutic change appear to involve similar processes to imaginal exposure—repeated memory activation, emotional engagement, and cognitive processing—but the written format may offer distinct advantages for some clients[37]. Writing provides a tangible product that clients can review and revise, potentially promoting cognitive processing and meaning-making. The fixed time structure of writing sessions may reduce overwhelm compared to imaginal exposure that sometimes extends through escalating emotional intensity[37]. Additionally, the briefer protocol reduces the cumulative practical demands of treatment[37][58].

## Conclusion: Toward an Integrated Clinical Understanding

The apparent tension between fear activation and treatment dropout in exposure-based CBT for PTSD resolves when understood through the lens of careful attention to engagement levels, collaborative treatment planning, and strategic treatment structuring. Evidence indicates that adequate fear activation is not itself the primary driver of dropout; rather, dropout occurs when clients either fail to engage sufficiently with their trauma material (underengagement) or when practical and contextual factors outweigh their motivation to continue treatment despite manageable distress. Remarkably, research demonstrates that physiological and emotional engagement during early treatment sessions predicts lower rather than higher dropout rates, suggesting that clients who genuinely engage with fear activation recognize this as evidence that treatment is working and become more committed to continuing[32][54].

Clinicians can optimize the balance between fear activation and retention through several concrete strategies: scheduling treatment at increased frequency when feasible to maintain momentum and reduce dropout risk; carefully titrating their level of directiveness to promote optimal engagement without overwhelming clients; building strong therapeutic alliances characterized by genuine collaboration and shared decision-making; investing in thorough pre-treatment preparation and expectation-setting that aligns treatment rationale with client values; considering intensive outpatient formats or massed exposure for high-risk clients; and remaining flexible in treatment structure by considering alternatives such as written exposure therapy that may achieve therapeutic change with lower dropout rates[3][43][58].

The central insight for clinical practice is that fear activation through appropriate engagement with trauma material, when achieved within a strong therapeutic relationship and with careful attention to the client's capacity to tolerate distress while maintaining progress, does not drive dropout—instead it facilitates the very learning and change that supports both symptom improvement and continued engagement in treatment. The challenge for clinicians involves developing the sophisticated clinical judgment to recognize optimal engagement, the flexibility to adjust approaches based on individual client needs and responses, and the communication skills to help clients understand that manageable distress during treatment represents therapeutic progress rather than treatment failure. When these elements are in place, clients can engage with the fear activation necessary for recovery while remaining committed to the treatment course, achieving the enhanced outcomes associated with treatment completion.
