# The Mechanisms of Satisfaction Evaluation: Understanding How the Gap Between Expectations and Experience Forms Satisfaction Judgments

This comprehensive report examines the cognitive and psychological mechanisms through which satisfaction evaluations are formed from the discrepancy between initial planning expectations and actual lived experience. The expectation-confirmation theory and its related frameworks provide a fundamental understanding of how individuals translate the gap between what they anticipated and what they actually encountered into satisfaction or dissatisfaction judgments. Research reveals that this process involves multiple interrelated cognitive mechanisms including expectation formation, performance perception, comparative evaluation processes known as disconfirmation, and subsequent emotional and cognitive adjustments that collectively determine satisfaction outcomes. The mechanism operates through both automatic cognitive processes and deliberate evaluative reasoning, with individual differences, emotional responses, and attributional processes all playing significant roles in shaping the final satisfaction evaluation.

## The Foundational Theory of Expectation Confirmation and Disconfirmation

The expectation confirmation theory represents one of the most influential frameworks in understanding how satisfaction emerges from the comparison between anticipated and actual outcomes[1][4][9]. Developed through a series of papers by Richard L. Oliver in 1977 and 1980, expectation confirmation theory proposes that satisfaction is fundamentally a post-consumption or post-adoption judgment that results from comparing what people expected with what they actually experienced[9]. Unlike earlier theories that treated satisfaction as a simple function of product quality or performance alone, ECT emphasizes that satisfaction depends critically on the relationship between expectations and perceived performance, demonstrating that even a relatively inferior quality product can successfully meet consumers' expectations and lead to acceptable satisfaction levels[4][7][14].

The mechanism begins with what consumers expect before they encounter a product or service. These expectations serve as a reference point or standard against which all subsequent experiences are measured[1][4]. The theory's foundation rests on the recognition that human psychological evaluation is fundamentally comparative in nature. When individuals set expectations, they establish a baseline or anchor point that becomes deeply integrated into their cognitive framework for evaluating their subsequent experience[9]. This baseline is not merely a passive number or concept; rather, it actively shapes how people perceive and interpret the experiences that follow. Once expectations are established, they function as a psychological standard that individuals continuously reference when forming their satisfaction judgments[1].

Following the expectation formation phase, individuals encounter the actual product or service and form perceptions of its actual performance[9]. This perceived performance represents the individual's subjective assessment of how well the product or service actually delivers on its attributes and promises. Importantly, perceived performance is distinct from objective performance—it reflects the individual's psychological interpretation and perception rather than purely objective measurements[4][7]. This distinction is crucial because the same objective performance can be perceived differently by different individuals depending on their cognitive frameworks, attention patterns, and interpretive biases.

The critical mechanism that transforms the gap between expectations and perceived performance into a satisfaction judgment is the process of disconfirmation or confirmation[1][4][9]. Disconfirmation refers to the cognitive evaluation that occurs when individuals compare their actual experiences with their prior expectations[2][7]. When actual performance exceeds expectations, positive disconfirmation occurs, leading to satisfaction and delight[1][2]. When actual performance falls short of expectations, negative disconfirmation results, producing dissatisfaction and disappointment[2][4]. When actual performance aligns with expectations, a state of confirmation exists, typically resulting in moderate satisfaction[2][7]. This comparison process is not a simple mathematical subtraction but rather a complex cognitive operation that involves multiple layers of evaluation and processing[4].

## Expectation Formation: The First Building Block of Satisfaction Evaluation

Expectations do not arise spontaneously or arbitrarily; they are systematically constructed through multiple information sources and psychological processes[1][4][17]. Understanding how expectations form is essential to comprehending the entire satisfaction evaluation mechanism because expectations fundamentally anchor all subsequent evaluations[9]. Consumers form expectations based on accumulated information that includes their prior direct experiences with similar products or services, word-of-mouth communication from friends and family members, explicit marketing communications and advertising messages, industry norms and competitive offerings, and their personal values and needs[1][4][17]. The process of expectation formation is ongoing and dynamic rather than static; consumers continuously update their expectations through new information exposure and experiential learning[1].

Marketing communications play a particularly influential role in expectation formation[10]. When firms make claims about their products and services through advertising, promotional messages, and marketing campaigns, these communications directly shape the expectations that consumers develop[1][10]. This has profound implications for the satisfaction evaluation mechanism because advertising that creates unrealistic or inflated expectations sets the stage for negative disconfirmation later when actual performance cannot match the exaggerated claims[10]. Conversely, advertising that sets realistic or even slightly conservative expectations may facilitate positive disconfirmation when actual performance meets or exceeds these more modest expectations[10].

The expectations that consumers form exist at multiple levels simultaneously[4][7]. Research distinguishes between "will" expectations, which represent what consumers believe will actually happen based on their assessment of probability and likelihood; "should" expectations, which reflect what consumers believe ought to happen based on norms, fairness principles, and equity considerations; and "ideal" expectations, which represent the best possible performance that consumers could imagine[4][59]. These different types of expectations may not align perfectly and can produce different satisfaction outcomes depending on which expectation type dominates in the evaluative process[4][59]. The complexity of expectation formation suggests that satisfaction evaluation is not a simple process but rather involves multiple comparative processes operating simultaneously[28].

Individual differences substantially shape the expectations that people form and how they use these expectations in satisfaction evaluation[1][4]. Personality characteristics such as perfectionism influence expectation levels, with perfectionists forming higher expectations than non-perfectionists[1]. Prior experience with similar products or services systematically shapes expectations, as individuals with extensive experience often develop more nuanced and realistic expectations based on their accumulated knowledge[4][11]. Demographic characteristics, cultural backgrounds, and personal values all influence the types of expectations individuals develop[4]. The phenomenon of disconfirmation sensitivity reflects the fact that different individuals react differently to the same magnitude of disconfirmation based on their personality traits, prior experiences, and situational factors[1]. This individual variability in expectation formation and expectation use means that the satisfaction evaluation mechanism produces different outcomes for different people even when they experience objectively identical products or services[1].

## The Perception of Actual Performance and Its Role in Satisfaction Evaluation

Satisfaction evaluation requires not only well-formed expectations but also accurate perception of actual performance. However, the process of perceiving actual performance is far more complex than simple objective measurement because human perception is actively shaped by expectations, attention patterns, and cognitive biases[4][7][9]. The relationship between objective performance and perceived performance is mediated by numerous psychological mechanisms, meaning that what consumers objectively experience and what they perceive themselves to experience may diverge substantially[4][31].

Expectations exert a powerful influence on the perception of actual performance through several psychological mechanisms[43][44]. One particularly important mechanism is the tendency for people to perceive action outcomes in ways that align with their expectations[43]. Research using signal detection paradigms demonstrates that observers are biased to report the presence of expected outcomes, indicating that expectations bias the perceptual decision-making process itself[43]. This perceptual bias occurs because expectations increase the weight that individuals give to expected sensory signals when making perceptual judgments, a process consistent with Bayesian models of perception that assume people weight expected information more heavily than unexpected information[43].

Another critical mechanism through which expectations influence perceived performance is through selective attention[8][15][48]. Attentional bias describes the human tendency to focus on certain elements of the environment while ignoring others, and expectations fundamentally shape what receives attention[48]. When individuals have formed expectations about what they will experience, they tend to pay more attention to information that is consistent with these expectations and less attention to information that contradicts them[15]. This selective attention process means that two individuals with different expectations experiencing the same objective performance may actually perceive quite different performances because they attend to and process different aspects of the experience[8].

The anchoring effect provides another mechanism through which expectations influence perceived performance[19][22]. Anchors represent reference points that influence subsequent judgments and estimates[22]. When expectations serve as anchors, they influence estimates of performance by biasing individuals toward anchor-consistent estimates[19][22]. The anchoring bias operates through both changes in response criteria (how people set their decision thresholds) and changes in sensitivity (the ability to discriminate between different levels of stimulation)[19]. This dual mechanism explains why expectations so powerfully influence performance perception—they work not just at the level of final judgment but at the more fundamental level of how people's perceptual systems process incoming information[19].

Assimilation and contrast effects further complicate the relationship between objective performance and perceived performance[20][23]. Assimilation occurs when people judge something as closer to contextual information, such that contextual anchors pull perceptions toward them[20][23]. In the context of satisfaction evaluation, assimilation to expectations means that perceived performance shifts toward alignment with expectations[20]. Contrast effects occur when people see something as farther apart from its context, producing the opposite effect where perceived performance is pushed away from expectations[20][23]. Which effect dominates—assimilation or contrast—depends on factors including the extremity and accessibility of the expectation information, the temporal sequence of exposure, and the degree of mental effort devoted to comparison[20][23].

## The Disconfirmation Process: Core Mechanism of Satisfaction Formation

The disconfirmation process represents the central mechanism through which the gap between expectations and perceived performance is converted into a satisfaction evaluation[1][2][4][7][10]. Disconfirmation is not simply a mathematical calculation of the difference between expectations and performance; rather, it involves complex cognitive processing wherein individuals explicitly or implicitly compare what they expected with what they perceive themselves to have experienced[2][4][7]. The outcome of this comparison process—positive disconfirmation, negative disconfirmation, or confirmation—directly determines satisfaction and dissatisfaction[1][2][4][7].

Positive disconfirmation occurs when perceived performance exceeds pre-consumption expectations[1][2][4]. In this circumstance, the individual receives more than anticipated, leading to satisfaction and often creating strong positive emotional responses such as delight, joy, or enthusiasm[1][2][4]. Positive disconfirmation activates cognitive processes that strengthen the individual's psychological connection to the product or service, leading to increased trust and emotional attachment to the brand[1]. When consumers experience positive disconfirmation, they often exhibit higher levels of repurchase intention and greater willingness to engage in positive word-of-mouth communication[1][4]. The psychological impact of positive disconfirmation extends beyond the immediate satisfaction with the specific product or service; it shapes future expectations and purchase decisions through a feedback loop mechanism[1][4].

Negative disconfirmation occurs when perceived performance falls short of expectations, producing dissatisfaction, disappointment, and negative emotional responses[1][2][4][7]. Research demonstrates that negative disconfirmation has asymmetric effects compared to positive disconfirmation—negative discrepancies produce stronger dissatisfaction than positive discrepancies of equivalent magnitude produce satisfaction[37][56]. This asymmetry reflects loss aversion principles from prospect theory, wherein losses loom larger psychologically than equivalent gains[37][56]. When consumers experience negative disconfirmation, they are more likely to engage in complaint behavior, negative word-of-mouth communication, and brand switching[1][4]. The dissatisfaction resulting from negative disconfirmation can be particularly damaging to customer relationships because it requires approximately twelve positive experiences to compensate for one significantly negative experience[3].

Confirmation occurs when perceived performance aligns with expectations, typically producing moderate satisfaction without strong emotional responses in either direction[1][2][4][7]. In confirmation situations, satisfaction is relatively stable and neutral; the individual experiences a sense that the product or service delivered as promised without exceeding or falling short of expectations[1][4][7]. Confirmation represents a stable state where expectations and reality align, producing neither the enthusiasm of positive disconfirmation nor the disappointment of negative disconfirmation[4].

The simplified expectation disconfirmation model proposes that satisfaction is directly determined by three constructs: expectations, disconfirmation, and satisfaction[4][7]. However, research increasingly favors the complete expectation disconfirmation model, which includes performance as a fourth variable[4][7][14]. This complete model demonstrates that expectations have a positive effect on performance and that higher product performance is more likely to exceed initial expectations, leading to positive disconfirmation[4][7][14]. The complete model performs better at explaining variations in satisfaction and highlights assimilation and asymmetric effects that the simplified model misses[4][7][14]. The distinction between these models reflects the complexity of the satisfaction evaluation mechanism—satisfaction is not determined solely by disconfirmation but involves the independent effects of both performance perception and disconfirmation operating through different psychological pathways[4][7][14].

## Cognitive and Emotional Components of Satisfaction Evaluation

The satisfaction evaluation mechanism involves both cognitive and emotional processing, representing an important departure from earlier theories that treated satisfaction as purely cognitive[1][4][17]. Research increasingly recognizes that satisfaction is not purely a cognitive judgment but rather emerges from the integration of cognitive evaluations with affective or emotional responses[1][4][17][57]. The gap between expectations and actual performance can trigger various emotional reactions ranging from delight and joy to disappointment and anger, and these emotional responses significantly influence the overall satisfaction judgment[1][17][57].

The emotional dimension of satisfaction evaluation reflects the affective component whereby individuals respond with feelings rather than purely logical assessments[17][57][60]. When individuals perceive positive disconfirmation—that is, when they receive more than they expected—they experience positive emotions that contribute to satisfaction[17][60]. Conversely, negative disconfirmation triggers negative emotions that contribute to dissatisfaction[17][60]. This emotional component explains why satisfaction cannot be fully predicted from cognitive assessments alone; the affective experience of the expectation-performance gap constitutes an essential part of the overall satisfaction evaluation[17][57][60].

Research distinguishes between affective satisfaction, which reflects an emotional response to the product or service, and cognitive satisfaction, which reflects a rational assessment of whether the product or service meets expectations[60]. When respondents are asked to define satisfaction, approximately seventy-seven percent provide affective definitions reflecting emotions such as happiness, liking, or comfort, while the remaining responses reflect cognitive evaluations[60]. This preponderance of affective definitions suggests that emotional responses play a central role in how people form satisfaction evaluations, though both affective and cognitive components contribute to the overall satisfaction judgment[60]. The integration of affective and cognitive components occurs through complex neurological processes; research on decision-making indicates that emotional responses from the body provide summaries of internal states that are essential for forming judgments, and when these emotional signals are disrupted, decision-making becomes severely impaired[60].

The activation of emotions through expectation disconfirmation operates through specific cognitive mechanisms. Expectancy violations theory, adapted from communication research, proposes that violations of expectancies cause arousal and cognitive appraisals of the violation that then determine emotional responses[16]. When individuals' expectations are violated, they experience increased arousal and attention, and their cognitive appraisals of the violation determine whether the emotional response is positive or negative[16]. If individuals interpret an expectation violation positively and the source of the violation is rewarding, they experience positive emotions; if they interpret it negatively, they experience negative emotions[16]. This theoretical framework explains how the objective fact of disconfirmation is converted into emotional responses that become part of the satisfaction evaluation[16].

## Cognitive Biases and Mental Processes in Satisfaction Evaluation

The formation of satisfaction evaluations involves multiple cognitive biases and mental processes that can distort the relationship between objective performance and satisfaction judgments. Understanding these cognitive mechanisms is essential for comprehending how satisfaction evaluations are formed because they systematically alter how people process the gap between expectations and experience[8][15][18][43][44][45].

Cognitive dissonance theory provides one framework for understanding how cognitive biases influence satisfaction evaluation[15][18]. Cognitive dissonance describes the psychological discomfort experienced when holding conflicting beliefs, attitudes, or values simultaneously[18]. When expectations are disconfirmed—particularly when negative disconfirmation occurs—individuals experience cognitive dissonance between their belief about what would happen and the reality of what actually happened[15][18]. To resolve this dissonance, individuals employ various strategies including changing their beliefs about the product or service, dismissing contradictory information, or selectively attending to information that supports their preexisting expectations[18]. This cognitive dissonance resolution process can lead to rationalization and post-hoc justification of satisfaction judgments[18].

Post-hoc rationalization represents another critical mechanism through which satisfaction evaluations are formed[44][47]. After making a decision or having an experience, individuals often construct logical reasons to justify their decisions and experiences, even if these reasons were not the actual basis for the original decision[44][47]. In the context of satisfaction evaluation, individuals may rationalize negative disconfirmation by constructing post-hoc reasons why the product's failure to meet expectations was not actually problematic[44][47]. Conversely, individuals may rationalize positive disconfirmation by constructing explanations for why they should have expected the superior performance[44][47]. This rationalization process means that satisfaction evaluations are not merely direct reflections of the expectation-performance gap but are actively reconstructed and reinterpreted after the experience[44][47].

The halo effect describes the tendency for positive impressions in one area to influence impressions in other areas, and it influences satisfaction evaluation by causing overall judgments to be colored by salient positive or negative characteristics[50][53]. When a product or service has a particularly positive or negative salient feature, this feature can influence the overall satisfaction judgment even if this feature should not logically determine overall satisfaction[50][53]. In performance evaluation contexts, supervisors often commit halo errors whereby a single positive characteristic like enthusiasm causes the entire evaluation to be colored positively, producing inflated satisfaction judgments[50][53]. The reverse halo effect or horn effect produces the opposite pattern, wherein negative characteristics disproportionately influence overall judgments[50][53].

Perceptual confirmation bias represents another mechanism through which expectations influence satisfaction evaluation[8][16]. The likelihood that an expectancy about a product or service will bias the evaluation to be expectation-consistent means that individuals may perceive their experiences as confirming their expectations even when objective evidence suggests otherwise[8][16]. This confirmation bias can allow some individuals to maintain satisfaction despite objectively experiencing expectation violations because they selectively interpret their experiences as confirming their expectations[8]. Conversely, individuals might emphasize expectation violations despite also having positive experiences, if they attend selectively to disconfirming evidence[8].

## Individual Differences in Satisfaction Evaluation Mechanisms

The satisfaction evaluation mechanism operates differently across individuals due to stable personality characteristics, prior experiences, and situational factors that influence how people form expectations, perceive performance, and process disconfirmation[1][4][8][39]. Individual differences shape every stage of the satisfaction evaluation process, meaning that different people may arrive at different satisfaction conclusions even when experiencing objectively identical expectation-performance gaps[1][4].

Personality characteristics influence satisfaction evaluation mechanisms in multiple ways[1][4][39]. Perfectionism represents one important personality dimension that affects expectations and satisfaction—perfectionists form higher expectations than non-perfectionists, which increases the likelihood that actual performance will fall short of expectations and produce negative disconfirmation[1]. Neuroticism, attachment anxiety, and attachment avoidance represent personality dimensions that influence how individuals process expectation violations[39]. Research examining relationship satisfaction demonstrates that individuals high in neuroticism or attachment insecurity tend to expect lower future satisfaction and may interpret ambiguous expectation violations more negatively[39]. Self-esteem and self-efficacy influence how individuals attribute expectation violations and whether they maintain hope that future performance will improve[39].

Prior experience systematically shapes how individuals form expectations and process disconfirmation[4][7][11]. Individuals with extensive prior experience with similar products or services develop more refined expectations based on accumulated knowledge, making them less likely to experience extreme positive or negative disconfirmation because their expectations tend to align closely with actual performance[4][7][11]. Conversely, individuals with limited prior experience may form expectations based on limited or stereotyped information, making them more vulnerable to substantial disconfirmation[4][11]. The cumulative experience of repeated satisfaction or dissatisfaction shapes individuals' reference points and baseline expectations for future encounters with similar products or services[4][7].

Situational factors and context influence the satisfaction evaluation mechanism substantially[4][8][38]. The perceived importance of the product or service affects how carefully individuals process expectation-performance gaps; for low-involvement products, individuals may expend minimal cognitive effort on evaluation, while for high-involvement products, detailed cognitive processing occurs[4]. The number of alternatives available influences satisfaction; when alternative products or services are readily available, individuals may have higher expectations and be less satisfied with negative disconfirmation[4]. The visibility of performance and whether others are aware of the expectation-performance gap influence emotional responses and satisfaction attributions[31]. Time pressure and cognitive resources available during evaluation influence how much cognitive processing occurs and whether sophisticated or heuristic evaluation strategies are employed[38].

## Temporal Dynamics and Adaptation in Satisfaction Evaluation

The satisfaction evaluation mechanism operates across time, with satisfaction judgments potentially changing as individuals' time horizons shift and their reference points adapt[33][35][36]. The hedonic treadmill or hedonic adaptation phenomenon describes how individuals tend to return to baseline levels of satisfaction despite experiencing positive or negative events[33][36]. This temporal process means that satisfaction evaluations formed immediately after an experience may differ substantially from satisfaction evaluations formed weeks, months, or years later as adaptation occurs[33][35][36].

Hedonic adaptation operates through multiple mechanisms including cognitive changes such as shifting values, goals, and interpretation of situations[33]. After a satisfying experience or purchase, individuals adapt to the new circumstance and revise their expectations and reference points upward, reducing the intensity of positive affect associated with the initially satisfying experience[33][36]. Similarly, after an initially dissatisfying experience, individuals may adapt by adjusting their expectations downward or reinterpreting the experience in less negative terms[33][36]. The adaptation level theory suggests that satisfaction is influenced by individuals' adaptation level, which serves as a reference point for comparison, and this adaptation level shifts in response to experience[25][33]. The process of adaptation is not inevitable and uniform; individuals differ substantially in their adaptation rates and degrees, with some individuals experiencing substantial changes to their satisfaction set points while others maintain relatively stable satisfaction levels despite changed circumstances[33][36].

The peak-end rule provides another temporal mechanism that influences satisfaction evaluation[35]. This rule suggests that individuals' overall assessment of an experience is disproportionately influenced by the peak or most intense moment of the experience and the most recent moment of the experience (the end), rather than by averaging across all moments[35]. This temporal asymmetry in satisfaction evaluation means that the same objective experience may produce different satisfaction judgments depending on when the peak occurs and how the experience ends[35]. Research examining retrospective evaluation of sequential events demonstrates that people do not simply average their moment-to-moment satisfaction levels but rather weight certain moments more heavily in their retrospective evaluation[35].

Retrospective perceptions of how satisfaction has changed also involve complex cognitive processes[32]. When individuals assess how their satisfaction has changed over time, they engage in evaluation strategies that may not perfectly correspond to measured changes in satisfaction[32]. Individuals report focusing more on their present state compared to their past state, considering their life circumstances more than their feelings, and sometimes relying on gut instinct rather than careful consideration[32]. These retrospective evaluation strategies mean that individuals' perceptions of satisfaction change may diverge from objectively measured changes, suggesting that the satisfaction evaluation mechanism includes a retrospective component wherein individuals actively construct narratives about how their satisfaction has changed[32].

## Extensions and Refinements of the Expectation-Confirmation Model

The basic expectation confirmation framework has undergone multiple revisions and extensions that have enhanced its applicability and explanatory power[1][4][7][14][25][28]. One significant extension involves integrating expectation confirmation theory with fairness and equity principles, recognizing that consumers' perceptions of fairness in exchange processes can moderate the relationship between disconfirmation and satisfaction[1]. Even when negative disconfirmation occurs, if consumers attribute the failure to meet expectations to uncontrollable external factors (such as weather or natural disasters), they may experience lower dissatisfaction than when they attribute failures to controllable factors (such as poor company service)[1][17]. This attributional dimension of satisfaction evaluation recognizes that people do not respond to disconfirmation mechanically but rather interpret and evaluate disconfirmation in light of causal attributions[1].

Another important extension involves recognizing that the satisfaction evaluation mechanism is dimension-specific, meaning that expectations, performance, and disconfirmation operate differently across different attributes and dimensions of products or services[4][7][11]. Rather than satisfaction being a unidimensional construct determined uniformly by expectation-performance gaps, satisfaction involves multiple specific satisfaction dimensions, each of which may be determined differently by disconfirmation on that specific attribute[4][7][11]. This dimensional specificity explains why satisfaction research sometimes produces conflicting findings—different attributes may show different patterns of expectation-performance relationships and satisfaction determination[4][11].

The distinction between subjective confirmation (the difference between subjective expectations and subjective perceived performance) and objective confirmation (the difference between objective expectations and objective performance) represents another refinement that recognizes the importance of subjective psychological processes in satisfaction evaluation[4]. Most satisfaction research has focused on subjective confirmation because satisfaction is ultimately a subjective psychological state, but the divergence between subjective and objective confirmation highlights how perceptual and cognitive processes distort the relationship between objective reality and subjective satisfaction[4].

The information elaboration model (IEM) provides a sophisticated framework for understanding how context and cognitive processes influence satisfaction evaluation[20]. According to the IEM, information used in forming a representation of the target (the product or service being evaluated) results in assimilation effects, while information used in forming a representation of the comparison standard (the expectations) results in contrast effects[20]. This model explains how the same information can produce different effects on satisfaction depending on whether it is interpreted as part of the target or part of the comparison standard, demonstrating that satisfaction evaluation involves complex metacognitive processes[20].

## The Role of Attribution in Satisfaction Evaluation

Attribution theory provides a complementary framework for understanding how satisfaction evaluations are formed from expectation-performance gaps[1][8][16][17]. Attribution refers to the process whereby individuals determine the causes of events and outcomes, and these causal attributions substantially influence satisfaction evaluations[1][8][17]. When negative disconfirmation occurs—when actual performance falls short of expectations—individuals' satisfaction with the experience depends partly on how they attribute this failure[1][8][17]. If individuals attribute the failure to internal, stable, and controllable factors (such as poor company performance or negligent service), they experience greater dissatisfaction[1][17]. If individuals attribute the failure to external, temporary, or uncontrollable factors (such as weather, traffic, or circumstances beyond anyone's control), they experience lower levels of dissatisfaction[1][8][17].

Similarly, positive disconfirmation is interpreted differently depending on causal attributions[8]. When individuals positively disconfirm expectations (experience better performance than expected), they may attribute this success to their own good judgment (internal attribution) or to the company's exceptional quality and care (stable attribution of the provider's ability), leading to strong positive emotions and commitment[8]. Alternatively, they might attribute the positive disconfirmation to luck or temporary circumstances, which produces less enduring positive affect[8]. The attributional process thus represents another mechanism through which satisfaction evaluations are actively constructed rather than simply determined by objective disconfirmation[1][8][17].

Benign postnatal attributions represent one specific form of attribution wherein individuals interpret expectation violations charitably as resulting from benign causes rather than hostile causes[8]. Research on relationship satisfaction demonstrates that benign attributions for expectation violations moderate the relationship between disconfirmation and satisfaction—when individuals attribute expectation violations to benign causes, the negative impact on satisfaction is reduced[8]. This attribution mechanism suggests that satisfaction evaluation is not determined solely by objective disconfirmation but rather by how individuals cognitively and emotionally process and interpret the disconfirmation[8].

## Information Processing and Reference Points in Satisfaction Evaluation

Reference point theory complements expectation confirmation theory by suggesting that satisfaction evaluations depend on how individuals establish and use reference points for evaluating outcomes[21][24]. Expectations serve as reference points that influence how people evaluate outcomes, with outcomes evaluated positively or negatively depending on their relationship to the reference point[21][24]. Research using real-effort experiments demonstrates that expectations serve as reference points that influence behavior and satisfaction—when expected payment levels are high, individuals work longer and expend more effort, suggesting that expectations establish a reference point against which actual outcomes are evaluated[21][24].

The anchoring effect, which describes how initial reference points influence subsequent judgments, operates prominently in satisfaction evaluation[19][22]. When expectations serve as anchors, they influence judgments of performance and satisfaction by serving as starting points for adjustments[22]. Individuals often make insufficient adjustments from the anchor, meaning that their performance judgments remain biased toward the expectation anchor even when actual performance provides clear evidence of the correct performance level[22]. This anchoring bias means that expectations exert a continuous influence on satisfaction evaluation throughout the entire evaluation process, not just at the initial comparison stage[19][22].

The information gap theory provides a framework for understanding how awareness of expectation-performance gaps influences satisfaction and emotional responses[52]. When individuals become aware of a gap between what they expected and what they actually experienced (an information gap), this awareness attracts attention and evokes emotions[52]. The curiosity gap created by uncertainty about whether expectations were met influences satisfaction-related emotions, and people may selectively seek information about whether expectations were met or may actively avoid such information depending on anticipated emotional consequences[52].

Training employees to understand satisfaction evaluation mechanisms improves their ability to manage customer expectations and enhance perceived performance[3][6][26]. The retrospective evaluation of satisfaction involves complex processes including the peak-end rule and selective attention to certain dimensions of experience, meaning that satisfaction evaluations may change when evaluated retrospectively versus prospectively[32][35].

Understanding that satisfaction evaluation depends on managing expectations, enhancing actual performance, and supporting positive cognitive and emotional processing of the expectation-performance gap provides organizations with evidence-based strategies for improving customer satisfaction[3][6][26][27][30]. The consistent operation of expectation-confirmation mechanisms across diverse domains from consumer purchases to public services to employment to personal relationships suggests that this mechanism represents a fundamental feature of human psychological evaluation deeply rooted in cognitive and emotional processing systems[5][37][39][40].
