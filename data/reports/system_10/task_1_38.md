# Early Alzheimer's Diagnosis: Balancing Treatment Benefits Against False Positive Harms Through Amyloid Biomarkers and Cognitive Testing

Recent advances in blood-based biomarkers and imaging technologies have revolutionized the potential for detecting Alzheimer's disease at increasingly early stages, even decades before cognitive symptoms manifest. Blood tests measuring phosphorylated tau variants, particularly p-tau217, can now identify individuals with amyloid pathology in the brain with approximately 88 to 92 percent accuracy, offering physicians a minimally invasive alternative to costly positron emission tomography scans or lumbar punctures[1][3]. Simultaneously, newly approved anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies like lecanemab demonstrate the first disease-modifying effects that can slow cognitive decline in early-stage disease, promising patients and families genuine hope for disease modification[14][17]. However, this diagnostic revolution exists alongside a critical challenge: the widespread adoption of sensitive biomarker testing in cognitively unimpaired populations has created unprecedented opportunities for misdiagnosis, with amyloid positivity increasingly detected in cognitively normal individuals who may never develop dementia in their remaining lifetimes. The tension between enabling early intervention for those who will truly benefit and avoiding unnecessary treatment and psychological harm in those who will not develop clinical disease represents one of modern medicine's most pressing diagnostic dilemmas. This report examines how the field is attempting to navigate this balance through integrating biomarker evidence, cognitive assessments, treatment efficacy data, and an emerging understanding of who truly needs early intervention.

## The Extended Preclinical Phase and Natural History of Amyloid Pathology

To understand the balance between early diagnosis benefits and false positive harms, one must first appreciate the extraordinarily long preclinical phase of Alzheimer's disease and the surprising disconnect between amyloid burden and cognitive function. The National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association have defined preclinical Alzheimer's disease as the stage in which individuals display biomarker evidence of amyloid pathology and tau accumulation without any detectable cognitive impairment or functional decline[9]. Brain amyloid accumulation typically begins 15 to 20 years before the clinical onset of cognitive symptoms, and cerebrospinal fluid changes indicating amyloid pathology can be detected up to 20 years before symptom onset in some individuals[42]. The prevalence of amyloid positivity increases substantially with advancing age, estimated at approximately 10.4 percent in individuals aged 50 years, rising by 3 to 5 percent every five years of life, reaching an estimated 43.8 percent by age 90 years[42]. This remarkably high prevalence of asymptomatic amyloid positivity creates a fundamental challenge: for the majority of amyloid-positive individuals, accumulation of this pathological protein does not translate into cognitive decline or dementia during their remaining lifetime.

Research examining the relationship between amyloid burden and cognition in cognitively normal elderly populations reveals a striking paradox. In a seminal positron emission tomography study of 43 cognitively unimpaired older adults, investigators found that 21 percent were amyloid-positive by Pittsburgh Compound B imaging, yet these individuals showed no significant cognitive impairment compared to their amyloid-negative counterparts[11][32]. In fact, the amyloid-positive group performed slightly better on delayed word recall, suggesting that cognitive reserve or other compensatory mechanisms may protect some individuals from the neurotoxic effects of accumulated amyloid[32]. This observation that amyloid deposition can be detected at an asymptomatic stage with no associated cognitive impairment suggests that an elderly person with significant amyloid burden can remain cognitively intact[11]. The concept of cognitive reserve has been proposed to explain this apparent protection, whereby individuals with higher educational attainment, engagement in cognitively stimulating activities, greater physical activity, and strong social connections demonstrate reduced susceptibility to amyloid-related cognitive impairment[56]. These findings fundamentally challenge the assumption that amyloid positivity automatically predicts future dementia and highlight the critical importance of considering individual variation in disease trajectories.

While amyloid-positive cognitively normal individuals do show elevated risk of future cognitive decline compared to amyloid-negative individuals, the absolute progression rates remain modest, particularly over the relatively short observation periods typical in clinical practice. In a large population-based study examining amyloid-positive participants without cognitive impairment over a mean follow-up period of 3.7 to 3.8 years, 14.5 percent progressed to amnestic mild cognitive impairment and only 3.2 percent developed Alzheimer's dementia[26]. In comparison, amyloid-negative individuals showed progression rates of 4.2 percent to mild cognitive impairment and 0.3 percent to dementia over the same timeframe[26]. Although these represent approximately 3.4-fold and 10-fold increased risk ratios respectively, the absolute risk remains low enough that the majority of amyloid-positive individuals remain cognitively unimpaired during clinically relevant timeframes. Progression rates per 1000 person-years were 42.0 for amyloid-positive individuals without cognitive impairment versus 12.4 for amyloid-negative individuals without cognitive impairment, again indicating that while risk is elevated, most individuals remain stable[26]. This natural history reveals a critical point: biomarker positivity identifies increased risk but does not determine clinical destiny, particularly in younger, cognitively normal individuals.

## Blood-Based Biomarkers: Transformative Diagnostic Tools With Imperfect Predictive Power

The development of blood-based biomarkers represents one of the most significant advances in Alzheimer's disease detection, offering physicians the potential to identify individuals at risk for future cognitive decline without requiring invasive procedures or expensive imaging. The most promising blood biomarkers measure phosphorylated tau variants, particularly p-tau217 and p-tau181, as well as the ratio of amyloid-beta 42 to amyloid-beta 40[1]. These biomarkers reflect underlying pathological changes in the brain and correlate with the presence of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles as detected by positron emission tomography imaging or cerebrospinal fluid analysis. A large clinical study of 1,213 patients examined the performance of the PrecivityAD2 test (APS2), which combines plasma p-tau217 ratio and amyloid-beta ratio measurements[1]. This study reported that the APS2 test significantly outperformed both primary care clinicians and Alzheimer's disease specialists using traditional clinical diagnostic methods in accurately detecting Alzheimer's pathology[1]. When clinicians used standard diagnostic approaches without biomarker information, they achieved 61 percent accuracy in primary care settings and 73 percent accuracy in specialty memory clinics, whereas the APS2 blood test achieved 88 to 92 percent accuracy across all participants[1][3].

The FDA cleared the first blood-based diagnostic device for Alzheimer's disease in 2024, the Lumipulse G pTau217/β-Amyloid 1-42 Plasma Ratio, based on clinical evidence that this test could reliably predict the presence or absence of amyloid pathology in patients with cognitive symptoms[27]. In the regulatory approval study of 499 cognitively impaired individuals, 91.7 percent of those with positive test results had confirmed amyloid plaques on PET scan or cerebrospinal fluid testing, while 97.3 percent of those with negative results had negative confirmatory testing[27]. These impressive accuracy metrics for detecting brain pathology in symptomatic populations have led some clinicians and researchers to propose using blood biomarkers more broadly in asymptomatic populations for earlier detection. Indeed, research has demonstrated that plasma p-tau217 can predict with 79 to 86 percent accuracy whether cognitively unimpaired individuals will test positive for amyloid-beta pathology on amyloid PET scans, and this predictive accuracy improves to 90 percent or above when combined with additional biomarker testing[1].

However, a critical distinction exists between detecting brain pathology and predicting clinical disease. The high accuracy of blood biomarkers at detecting amyloid plaques and tau tangles does not translate into equivalently high accuracy at predicting which individuals will develop cognitive impairment. This distinction between pathological accuracy and clinical predictive accuracy represents a fundamental challenge in early Alzheimer's diagnosis. Research examining plasma p-tau217 as a predictor of cognitive decline in cognitively unimpaired individuals shows that while elevated levels are associated with increased risk, the relationship is probabilistic rather than deterministic. Among individuals with elevated p-tau217, high p-tau217 levels were associated with a significantly faster cognitive decline, with a hazard ratio of 3.16, meaning roughly three times greater risk of decline compared to low p-tau217 individuals[13]. However, examining the absolute numbers, this still means that many individuals with high p-tau217 do not experience significant cognitive decline over the observation period. Moreover, when p-tau217 is combined with other biomarkers like neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein, the interactions become complex, with elevated GFAP showing the greatest additional hazard when combined with high p-tau217[13]. This multifactorial nature of disease progression means that blood biomarkers must be interpreted within the context of multiple other biological and clinical factors rather than as standalone predictive tests.

## Cognitive Testing: From High Sensitivity for Dementia to Limited Accuracy for Mild Impairment

Cognitive testing has long served as a cornerstone of Alzheimer's disease diagnosis, with numerous brief and comprehensive neuropsychological batteries available for clinical use. A systematic review examining the accuracy of brief cognitive tests for distinguishing Alzheimer's dementia from normal cognition or mild cognitive impairment found that many brief tests demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity for detecting Alzheimer's dementia when compared to cognitively normal individuals[21]. Standalone cognitive tests demonstrating high performance included the Mini-Mental State Examination with median sensitivity of 0.88 and specificity of 0.94 across seven studies, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment with sensitivity of 0.94 and specificity of 0.94 across two studies, and the Brief Alzheimer Screen with sensitivity of 0.92 and specificity of 0.97[21]. Memory tests such as list delayed recall achieved sensitivity of 0.89 and specificity of 0.94, while language tests including category fluency demonstrated sensitivity of 0.92 and specificity of 0.89[21]. These metrics suggest that cognitive tests perform reliably well at their traditional role of distinguishing moderate to severe dementia from normal cognition.

However, the accuracy of cognitive testing deteriorates substantially when clinicians attempt to identify milder forms of disease, precisely the population that represents the target for early intervention and disease modification. The same systematic review found that accuracy was significantly lower when distinguishing mild Alzheimer's dementia from normal cognition and particularly poor when attempting to differentiate Alzheimer's dementia from mild cognitive impairment[21]. This limitation reflects the fundamental biological reality that cognitive changes in early disease are subtle and may overlap substantially with normal aging or with other conditions affecting cognition. The Mini-Mental State Examination, despite its widespread use, demonstrates particular difficulty in identifying early cognitive impairment, with studies showing it does not reliably detect mild cognitive impairment when using the standard 24-point cutoff[21][24]. Brief cognitive assessments based on original norms that are not culturally adapted or nationally standardized show further degraded accuracy compared to comprehensive neuropsychological assessment using multiple tests in each of five cognitive domains recommended by the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association[22].

The research literature reveals that false negative diagnostic errors in mild cognitive impairment are relatively common, with conventional diagnostic criteria leading to significant misclassification errors in both directions. In one analysis of 520 participants, investigators identified 7.1 percent of those conventionally classified as cognitively normal who actually met criteria for mild cognitive impairment based on more sensitive actuarial neuropsychological criteria[5]. These "false negative" cases, despite being diagnosed as cognitively normal using standard criteria, demonstrated worse neuropsychological performance, had cerebrospinal fluid biomarker evidence of Alzheimer's pathology, and showed 20 percent rate of progression to dementia, higher than true negatives but lower than true positives[5]. Conversely, the same research group had previously documented that one-third of conventionally diagnosed mild cognitive impairment participants actually performed within normal limits on more extensive cognitive testing, representing "false positive" diagnostic errors[5]. These individuals had normal cerebrospinal fluid biomarker levels and low progression rates, suggesting they represented overdiagnosis of mild cognitive impairment[5]. This high rate of misclassification using conventional criteria emphasizes that cognitive testing alone, without objective biomarker data, provides limited diagnostic accuracy in early disease stages.

The challenge of false memory in mild cognitive impairment and early Alzheimer's disease adds additional complexity to cognitive diagnosis. Research examining false positive responses in recognition memory tests found that amyloid-positive participants with mild cognitive impairment made significantly more false positive responses in recognition tests, with false positive responses in recognition tests predicting Alzheimer's disease pathology with increased risk of 1.31 to 1.36 times in verbal and visual recognition respectively[2]. While increased false memory shows potential value for further study, investigators concluded that these measures have only low to moderate discriminatory accuracy and would be insufficient for direct clinical application[2]. These findings illustrate that cognitive testing, despite decades of refinement, remains imperfect for early disease detection and particularly struggles with subtle impairments characteristic of preclinical and prodromal Alzheimer's disease.

## Approved Anti-Amyloid Treatments: Demonstrating Modest but Genuine Clinical Benefits

The approval of lecanemab and donanemab, monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid pathology, represented a watershed moment in Alzheimer's disease treatment, as these represent the first disease-modifying therapies demonstrating the ability to slow cognitive decline in early disease stages. Lecanemab, a humanized monoclonal antibody directed against aggregated amyloid-beta, received FDA approval in 2023 based on the Clarity AD trial, a phase 3 study of 1,795 participants with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease with confirmed amyloid pathology[14]. In the 18-month primary analysis, lecanemab-treated participants showed a mean change from baseline of 1.21 on the Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes scale compared to 1.66 for placebo, yielding a difference of 0.45 points representing a 27 percent slowing of cognitive decline[14][17]. While this may seem modest, it represents the first demonstration that removing amyloid from the brain can slow the rate of cognitive decline in living patients with early Alzheimer's disease.

Extended follow-up data from the Clarity AD trial's open-label extension study provides additional perspective on lecanemab's long-term effects. Over three years of continuous treatment across the core study and open-label extension, lecanemab demonstrated a reduction in cognitive decline of 0.95 points compared to the expected decline in historical cohorts, with this benefit growing to 1.75 points by four years[17][55]. Among patients who started treatment in the earliest stages of disease with low tau burden on positron emission tomography, 56 percent showed improvement from baseline on the Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes scale after four years, and 69 percent showed improvement or no decline[17][55]. On the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale and the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study-Activities of Daily Living Scale for Mild Cognitive Impairment, approximately 50-60 percent of early-stage patients showed improvement or no decline after four years of treatment[17][55]. These results suggest that initiating treatment early in disease may provide sustained benefits over extended periods, preserving cognitive and functional abilities that would otherwise be expected to decline.

However, critical questions persist regarding the clinical meaningfulness of these treatment benefits in the context of individual patient lives. The minimally clinically important difference for the Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes is generally considered to be 1 to 2 points, meaning that the 0.45-point benefit at 18 months falls substantially below what most experts consider clinically meaningful improvement[58]. While longer-term data showing 1.75 points of benefit reduction over four years approaches the lower bound of clinically meaningful change, this still represents slowing of decline rather than improvement, and the vast majority of treated patients continued to show overall cognitive decline despite treatment[17]. A meta-analysis examining the clinical meaningfulness of monoclonal antibody treatments concluded that despite statistical significance and reduction of amyloid burden, the cognitive improvements fall far below the minimally clinically important difference for most outcome scales[58]. This distinction between statistical significance and clinical meaningfulness has important implications for understanding whether the treatment benefits justify the risks for individual patients, particularly those identified through biomarker screening rather than clinical symptoms.

## Safety Concerns: Amyloid-Related Imaging Abnormalities as a Significant Harm

The primary safety concern associated with anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies is the development of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, abnormalities on brain magnetic resonance imaging that can manifest as either vasogenic edema (ARIA-E) or microhemorrhages (ARIA-H). In the Clarity AD trial with lecanemab, amyloid-related imaging abnormalities were observed in approximately 12 percent with ARIA-E and 17 percent with ARIA-H among treated participants[14][20]. While most amyloid-related imaging abnormalities are asymptomatic and resolve without clinical sequelae, some patients develop symptomatic manifestations including headache, confusion, nausea, dizziness, seizures, and in rare cases death. The symptomatic rate for ARIA-E occurs in approximately 1 to 2 percent of treated patients. Several risk factors have been identified for developing amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, including carrying the APOE ε4 allele, advanced age, prior history of cerebral microhemorrhages, history of prior strokes, and antithrombotic use[20]. In some clinical settings, these exclusion criteria based on amyloid-related imaging abnormalities risk have excluded as many as 90 percent of potential Alzheimer's disease patients from receiving anti-amyloid treatments[20].

A critical consideration is that the risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities increases substantially when anti-amyloid treatments are used in asymptomatic amyloid-positive individuals rather than those with established cognitive symptoms. Clinical trials deliberately targeting preclinical Alzheimer's disease, such as the A4 trial in cognitively normal amyloid-positive individuals, have faced challenges in recruiting and retaining participants partly because of concerns about developing imaging abnormalities without clear clinical benefit[15]. The fact that amyloid-related imaging abnormalities can cause serious neurological complications including stroke in rare cases, as documented in case reports of lecanemab-treated patients[23], creates an asymmetry in the risk-benefit calculus: asymptomatic individuals with biomarker evidence of disease who may never develop clinical symptoms are being exposed to treatment-related harms. This represents a fundamental ethical problem in diagnosing and treating asymptomatic individuals using biomarkers alone.

## The False Positive Problem: Prevalence, Consequences, and Clinical Implications

The widespread adoption of sensitive blood biomarker testing in populations without cognitive symptoms has created an unprecedented opportunity for false positive diagnoses, where individuals are incorrectly identified as having or destined to develop Alzheimer's disease when in fact they will remain cognitively intact throughout their lives. A false positive in Alzheimer's diagnosis occurs when biomarker testing suggests the presence of amyloid pathology in an individual who either does not actually have Alzheimer's disease pathology or who has amyloid pathology but will not progress to clinical disease during their remaining lifespan. The distinction is critical because current blood biomarker tests measure the presence of pathological proteins but cannot predict with certainty whether an individual will develop clinical symptoms.

The consequences of false positive Alzheimer's diagnoses are substantial and multifaceted. When individuals are incorrectly told they have Alzheimer's disease or are at imminent risk of developing dementia, they may experience significant psychological distress, anxiety, and depression[28][36][40]. These individuals may make major life decisions prematurely, including quitting work, selling assets, entering long-term care facilities, or restricting their activities based on the assumption of inevitable cognitive decline[43]. One analysis estimated that misdiagnosed patients generate $400,000 or more in unnecessary healthcare costs[43]. More troublingly, false positive diagnoses may lead clinicians to prescribe anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody treatments to individuals who do not have clinically manifest disease and who may be harmed rather than helped by treatment. Given that amyloid-related imaging abnormalities can cause brain edema and hemorrhage in approximately 10-20 percent of treated individuals and can rarely result in stroke, false positive patients receive exposure to these serious treatment-related harms without the offsetting benefit of slowed cognitive decline because they were not destined to develop clinical cognitive decline anyway.

False positive misdiagnosis may also result in other treatable causes of cognitive impairment being overlooked. Treatable conditions including vitamin B12 deficiency, medication interactions or adverse effects, sleep apnea, thyroid dysfunction, depression, and normal pressure hydrocephalus can all produce cognitive impairment that mimics early Alzheimer's disease[36]. When biomarkers alone drive diagnosis without comprehensive clinical evaluation, these reversible causes may be missed, leading to inappropriate treatment and further cognitive decline[36]. Additionally, other irreversible but distinct neurodegenerative conditions including Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal degeneration, and progressive supranuclear palsy can be associated with elevated amyloid and tau biomarkers, leading to misattribution of cognitive symptoms to Alzheimer's disease when they result from entirely different neuropathological processes[43].

The prevalence of false positive diagnoses appears to be substantial in clinical practice. In one study examining the conventional diagnostic criteria for mild cognitive impairment, investigators found that 34.2 percent of participants who met clinical criteria for mild cognitive impairment actually performed within normal limits on more comprehensive neuropsychological testing and had normal cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers[5]. These false positive mild cognitive impairment cases had low rates of progression to dementia (11 percent) compared to true positive cases (40 percent), suggesting that the conventional diagnostic criteria substantially overdiagnose mild cognitive impairment[5]. As blood biomarker testing becomes more widespread and sensitive, the risk of false positive diagnoses seems likely to increase, particularly as testing is extended to asymptomatic populations where the pretest probability of Alzheimer's disease is lower. In younger, cognitively normal individuals with positive amyloid biomarkers, the positive predictive value of biomarkers for future dementia is substantially lower than in older symptomatic populations, yet the psychological impact of a positive biomarker result may be similar or even greater due to the extended remaining lifespan at risk.

## The Disclosure Problem: Ethical and Psychological Dimensions of Preclinical Diagnosis

Beyond the medical consequences of false positive diagnoses lie important ethical and psychological considerations surrounding the disclosure of biomarker results to cognitively unimpaired individuals. Research examining how individuals respond to learning they have elevated Alzheimer's disease biomarkers reveals complex reactions mixing hope for early intervention with significant distress about future illness. Qualitative studies of participants in Alzheimer's disease prevention trials who were informed of their amyloid or APOE status described wrestling with difficult decisions about whether and how to share this information with family members, friends, and others[28]. Participants compared disclosure of biomarker status to "coming out of the closet," suggesting the profound identity implications of being labeled as someone at risk for dementia[28]. Many chose not to disclose biomarker results outside close relationships because of concerns about stigma and discrimination in employment, insurance, housing, and financial control[28].

The psychological impact of receiving a preclinical Alzheimer's disease diagnosis appears to extend beyond individual distress to affect care partner relationships and family dynamics. Some participants described preparing family members for the possibility that they might become a caregiver, either to help the diagnosed individual adjust to the future risk or to prepare the care partner's own expectations[28]. Others withheld information from family members to protect them from unnecessary worry about an uncertain future. Clinical practice guidelines currently recommend against offering biomarker testing or APOE genetic testing to cognitively unimpaired asymptomatic individuals because the clinical relevance of results is not yet well established[28]. However, as clinical trials increasingly test disease-modifying treatments in asymptomatic populations and such treatments potentially become approved for this indication, the recommendation against testing may evolve, creating ethical obligations for clinicians to carefully consider how and whether to disclose results.

The concept of diagnostic uncertainty adds another layer of complexity to disclosure. Unlike many laboratory tests that provide binary results, biomarker tests for Alzheimer's disease typically produce probabilistic risk information rather than definitive diagnoses. A positive p-tau217 test or amyloid PET scan indicates increased risk of future cognitive decline but does not indicate when, if ever, that decline will manifest. This probabilistic nature of biomarker results creates challenges for informed consent because patients may misinterpret results as indicating definite future disease rather than elevated risk. Research on communication of genetic risk information demonstrates that individuals frequently misunderstand probabilistic risk information and may overestimate the certainty of future disease[40]. Similar challenges appear likely with biomarker disclosure, as the brain-specific nature of Alzheimer's disease biomarkers may feel more threatening and real than genetic information, potentially amplifying the tendency toward catastrophic misinterpretation[7].

## Clinical Implementation: Balancing Early Detection With Diagnostic Precision

Navigating the balance between benefits and harms of early Alzheimer's diagnosis requires thoughtful clinical implementation strategies that maintain diagnostic precision while enabling early detection and treatment for those who will genuinely benefit. Recent clinical practice guidelines from the Alzheimer's Association recommend that blood-based biomarker tests with 90 percent or greater sensitivity and 75 percent or greater specificity can be used as aids in diagnosis in specialty care settings to support clinical judgment[44]. However, these guidelines explicitly note that biomarker testing should not replace comprehensive clinical evaluation including cognitive assessment, medical history, physical examination, and assessment of functional abilities[1][44]. The guidelines further recommend that diagnostic confirmation should ideally include both biomarker evidence and cognitive testing results rather than relying on either modality alone[1].

Current best practice recommendations suggest a stepped diagnostic approach in which cognitive testing is performed first in individuals with reported cognitive symptoms or concerns, with biomarker testing then used to confirm or refute suspected Alzheimer's disease pathology in those demonstrating objective cognitive impairment[1]. In this approach, biomarkers serve to confirm the pathological basis for observed cognitive changes rather than to screen asymptomatic populations for future disease. This conservative approach reduces false positives by ensuring that individuals who test positive have corroborating evidence of cognitive impairment or functional decline in addition to biomarker evidence. The FDA-cleared Lumipulse G pTau217 test is indicated specifically for use in patients aged 55 years and older "exhibiting signs and symptoms of the disease," restricting its use to symptomatic populations rather than asymptomatic screening[27].

For individuals identified as amyloid-positive through biomarker screening but without cognitive symptoms, research suggests that cognitive reserve and other protective factors may significantly modify disease trajectories. High cognitive reserve, operationalized through educational attainment, engagement in cognitively stimulating activities, regular physical exercise, and strong social connections, appears to buffer against amyloid-related cognitive impairment[56]. Sleep quality also emerges as a potentially modifiable factor that may serve as a cognitive reserve factor, with research demonstrating that good quality deep sleep is associated with better cognitive performance in amyloid-positive individuals independent of amyloid burden[59]. These observations suggest that individuals identified as amyloid-positive without cognitive symptoms might benefit more from interventions targeting lifestyle factors and cognitive reserve than from immediate pharmacological treatment, reserving anti-amyloid therapy for those with established cognitive impairment or those at imminent risk of progression.

Recent research has examined the utility of combining biomarker information with behavioral and neuropsychiatric symptoms to improve prognostication in mild cognitive impairment. Mild behavioral impairment, defined as persistent neuropsychiatric symptoms that emerge later in life and are not accounted for by primary psychiatric illness, appears to significantly enhance risk stratification in mild cognitive impairment populations. Individuals with mild cognitive impairment and mild behavioral impairment showed 14.7 percent annual progression rate to dementia compared to 8.3 percent for mild cognitive impairment without behavioral symptoms, with mild behavioral impairment also reducing the likelihood of reversion to normal cognition[31]. These findings suggest that comprehensive clinical assessment incorporating behavioral and neuropsychiatric features alongside objective biomarkers and cognitive testing provides superior prognostication compared to biomarker evidence alone.

## Patient Preferences and the Role of Patient Choice in Early Detection

Understanding patient preferences regarding early detection and treatment has become increasingly important as the field recognizes that early diagnosis involves both significant potential benefits and genuine harms that may be experienced quite differently across individuals. Recent survey data reveals that Americans overwhelmingly support early Alzheimer's diagnosis and treatment. Seventy-nine percent of respondents stated they would want to know if they had Alzheimer's disease before having symptoms or before symptoms interfered with daily activities, and 91 percent said they would want to take a simple blood biomarker test if available[57]. Eighty percent said they would ask to be tested rather than wait for their physician to recommend testing. When asked about willingness to accept risk associated with anti-amyloid treatments, 58 percent said they would accept moderate to very high levels of risk with medication to slow disease progression, and nearly one-quarter indicated willingness to accept high levels of risk[57].

These preferences suggest that, from a patient autonomy perspective, many individuals would welcome the opportunity to undergo early biomarker testing and to access early treatment, even with knowledge of associated risks. The predominant ethical principle guiding medical decision-making should acknowledge this patient preference for information and choice. However, the high rates of patient preference for early testing and treatment may also reflect limited understanding of the actual prevalence of false positives, the modest magnitude of treatment benefits, and the frequency of serious treatment-related complications. Research examining informed consent in dementia research demonstrates that research participants often have limited understanding of study procedures and risks despite receiving detailed informed consent forms[28].

Additionally, physician perspectives on early detection and treatment appear more conservative than patient preferences. In surveys, many primary care physicians express concerns about labeling cognitively unimpaired individuals with amyloid positivity as having disease, worry about psychological harms of such labeling, and uncertainty about appropriate management strategies[33]. Some specialty care clinicians similarly express caution about aggressively pursuing biomarker testing and treatment in asymptomatic populations, particularly given the modest treatment benefits and unknown long-term effects of continuous anti-amyloid therapy[10]. This apparent gap between patient preferences and physician caution reflects different risk-benefit calculations and different values regarding the importance of autonomy, prevention, and precaution in medical decision-making.

## Diagnostic Criteria Evolution: From Symptom-Based to Biomarker-Anchored Diagnosis

The definition of Alzheimer's disease itself has undergone profound evolution with the recognition of the long preclinical phase and the development of biomarker evidence of disease. The traditional diagnostic criteria for Alzheimer's disease required the presence of cognitive symptoms, typically memory impairment, along with progressive decline in function. The revised National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer's Association criteria proposed in 2011 and further refined subsequently incorporated biomarker evidence, defining preclinical Alzheimer's disease as the stage with biomarker evidence of amyloid and tau pathology but without cognitive or functional impairment[9]. This represents a fundamental shift from symptom-based diagnosis toward biomarker-anchored diagnosis, expanding the disease definition to include millions of cognitively intact individuals with imaging or blood-based evidence of pathological proteins.

This expansion of disease definition from clinical syndrome to biomarker positivity creates important downstream effects on false positive rates and on the population screened. The expanded definition effectively lowers the threshold for disease diagnosis, as individuals can now be considered to have Alzheimer's disease despite being cognitively intact. While this expanded definition has important benefits for research, allowing investigators to study disease in its earliest stages and to test interventions aimed at preventing symptom onset, it simultaneously increases the proportion of people with a "diagnosis" of disease who will never experience clinical manifestations. The prevalence of preclinical Alzheimer's disease as defined by biomarker criteria vastly exceeds the prevalence of clinical Alzheimer's disease or mild cognitive impairment, potentially labeling millions of cognitively intact older adults as having disease based on biomarker evidence alone.

The field has begun grappling with whether the biomarker-anchored definition of preclinical Alzheimer's disease should guide clinical diagnosis and treatment decisions outside research settings. The Amyloid Imaging Task Force specifically recommended against clinical use of amyloid PET imaging in asymptomatic individuals, reasoning that "there is significant potential for patients and families to make inaccurate assumptions about risk and future outcomes" and that "currently the potential harms outweigh the minimal benefits"[7]. This recommendation reflects concern that expanding diagnosis to asymptomatic individuals would result in labeling and treatment of people unlikely to develop disease. However, this recommendation predates the approval of disease-modifying anti-amyloid treatments and may require revision in light of the emergence of treatments that can slow cognitive decline in early symptomatic disease. Some commentators argue that the ethical imperative to prevent disease in asymptomatic high-risk populations justifies biomarker-based diagnosis and treatment, while others maintain that without cognitive symptoms, the diagnosis lacks clinical validity regardless of biomarker evidence.

## Integrating Multiple Data Sources: Toward Multimodal Diagnostic Approaches

Recognizing the limitations of single diagnostic modalities, leading researchers and clinicians increasingly advocate for multimodal diagnostic approaches that integrate biomarker evidence, cognitive testing, neuroimaging, genetic information, and clinical history to maximize diagnostic accuracy while minimizing false positives. Blood-based biomarkers provide information about amyloid and tau pathology but cannot distinguish between individuals whose pathology will remain asymptomatic and those destined to develop clinical symptoms. Cognitive testing provides information about current cognitive status but lacks specificity in early disease and may miss preclinical changes. Magnetic resonance imaging can detect hippocampal atrophy and other structural changes but lags behind biomarker changes in disease evolution. Positron emission tomography imaging with amyloid and tau tracers provides direct visualization of pathology but involves radiation exposure and significant cost.

Research examining multimodal approaches has identified combinations of tests that improve diagnostic accuracy beyond single modalities alone. Adding amyloid PET imaging to clinical evaluation improves diagnostic accuracy from 61-73 percent with clinical evaluation alone to greater than 90 percent with combined clinical and imaging assessment[6]. Similarly, combining multiple blood biomarkers improves predictive accuracy compared to single biomarkers. Individuals with elevated p-tau217 combined with elevated neurofilament light chain, GFAP, or other neurodegeneration biomarkers show higher risk of progression than those with elevated p-tau217 alone[13]. Machine learning algorithms trained on multimodal data incorporating neuropsychological test performance, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, magnetic resonance imaging volumetry, and demographic information have demonstrated predictive accuracy approaching 80 percent for identifying individuals with mild cognitive impairment who will progress to dementia[37].

However, these multimodal approaches substantially increase the complexity, cost, and burden of diagnosis, potentially offsetting their accuracy benefits by creating barriers to access and widespread implementation. The challenge of implementing multimodal diagnosis in primary care settings, where most individuals with cognitive concerns are initially evaluated, is substantial, as primary care clinicians typically lack access to PET imaging, advanced magnetic resonance imaging protocols, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, or comprehensive neuropsychological testing. Blood biomarkers offer the promise of reducing this access barrier by providing information about brain pathology through a simple blood draw accessible in any clinical setting. Yet relying primarily on blood biomarkers without cognitive testing or neuroimaging creates risks of false positives in asymptomatic individuals. This tension between accessibility and accuracy remains unresolved and represents an important consideration in implementation planning.

## Cost, Resource Allocation, and Equity Considerations

The economic implications of early Alzheimer's diagnosis and treatment profoundly affect whether the potential benefits can be realized equitably across populations and whether resources are allocated efficiently toward interventions that will provide genuine value. Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody treatments are expensive, costing approximately $26,500 to $28,200 annually, with additional costs for required magnetic resonance imaging monitoring, infusion center fees, and management of treatment-related complications[58]. If widespread biomarker screening were to identify millions of asymptomatic amyloid-positive individuals and these individuals were treated with anti-amyloid therapies, the economic burden would be enormous and would likely crowd out other healthcare spending.

Research examining potential cost-effectiveness of early intervention has suggested substantial potential cost savings if early treatment can delay the onset of dementia symptoms. An economic model developed by Alzheimer's Research UK and the Office of Health Economics suggested that delaying the onset of dementia by two to five years would result in 184,000 fewer people with dementia and 156,000 fewer family caregivers by 2030, with highly significant decreases in care costs[60]. However, these models typically assume that early intervention is highly effective at delaying symptom onset, which current data do not support. The modest magnitude of lecanemab's clinical benefit (0.45 to 0.59 points slowing of decline on standard scales) means that the cost per quality-adjusted life-year gained likely exceeds typical healthcare cost-effectiveness thresholds, particularly in asymptomatic populations where symptom onset may never occur.

Issues of equity and access also merit substantial consideration. Blood biomarker testing and anti-amyloid treatments are currently disproportionately available to individuals with insurance coverage, access to specialty care, higher education, and other markers of socioeconomic advantage. While the simplicity of blood biomarker testing offers promise for expanding access, the requirement for interpretation by clinicians familiar with the complexities of Alzheimer's diagnosis and the need for specialized infusion center services to administer anti-amyloid treatments maintain barriers to equitable access. In many underserved communities and globally, blood biomarker testing and anti-amyloid treatment remain largely unavailable. Expanding early diagnosis without addressing these access disparities risks widening existing health inequities in dementia care.

## Future Directions: Combining Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Prevention

Looking forward, the field increasingly recognizes that maximizing the benefits of early Alzheimer's diagnosis while minimizing harms will require combining biomarker-guided identification of at-risk individuals with evidence-based non-pharmacological interventions targeting modifiable risk factors. Research demonstrates that numerous lifestyle factors can slow cognitive aging and reduce dementia risk, including cognitive engagement, physical exercise, cognitive training, Mediterranean or DASH diet adherence, cardiovascular risk factor management, cognitive reserve building through education, and strong social engagement[59][60]. These interventions do not require diagnosis or specialist care and can be implemented in primary care settings or through community programs. Furthermore, these interventions benefit not only those at risk for Alzheimer's disease but also promote overall health and quality of life.

For individuals identified as amyloid-positive without cognitive symptoms, evidence-based recommendations increasingly emphasize lifestyle modification rather than immediate pharmacological treatment. The potential synergistic effects of combining pharmacological treatment with lifestyle modification have not been well studied, but it is plausible that individuals engaging in regular cognitive engagement, exercise, and cardiovascular risk factor management while also receiving anti-amyloid treatment might experience greater benefit than either intervention alone. Future clinical trials will likely examine these combinations, potentially identifying which individuals would most benefit from intensified biomarker screening and treatment, and which might be managed more effectively through non-pharmacological prevention strategies.

Additionally, research examining the long-term effects of continuous anti-amyloid therapy is limited, and the field does not yet understand whether the cognitive benefits are sustained indefinitely, whether cumulative exposure to amyloid-related imaging abnormalities creates additional risk with extended treatment, or whether treatment duration and intensity can be modified based on individual biomarker and clinical trajectories. Recent data indicating that cognitive decline accelerates when lecanemab treatment is discontinued raises important questions about the long-term commitment required for continued benefit and the sustainability of treatment over years or decades[38]. These uncertainties argue for cautious expansion of early treatment and suggest that continued research in clinical trial settings will be important to better understand optimal treatment strategies.

## Conclusion: Toward a Balanced and Nuanced Approach to Early Alzheimer's Diagnosis

The apparent tension between the tremendous potential of early Alzheimer's diagnosis and treatment to benefit those who will develop clinical disease and the genuine risks of false positive diagnosis, unnecessary treatment, and psychological harm in those who will not develop clinical disease can be navigated through thoughtful, evidence-informed clinical practice that maintains diagnostic precision while enabling early access to treatment for appropriate candidates. Blood-based biomarkers have transformed the possibility of detecting Alzheimer's disease pathology decades before symptom onset, offering previously unavailable opportunities to identify at-risk individuals and to test preventive interventions. However, biomarker positivity without cognitive symptoms does not equate to disease requiring treatment, as many cognitively normal amyloid-positive individuals will remain cognitively intact throughout their remaining lifespan.

Early Alzheimer's diagnosis should continue to be anchored in objective cognitive testing demonstrating impairment, combined with biomarker evidence of Alzheimer's pathology to confirm the underlying etiology of that cognitive impairment. This approach maintains diagnostic specificity by requiring concordance between cognitive and biomarker evidence, reducing false positives while retaining sensitivity for detecting early clinical disease. For individuals identified as amyloid-positive without cognitive impairment, current best practice recommends counseling regarding lifestyle modifications targeting dementia risk reduction, including cognitive engagement, regular exercise, Mediterranean or DASH diet adherence, cardiovascular risk factor management, and maintenance of strong social connections, rather than immediate pharmacological treatment. These lifestyle interventions offer benefits exceeding those of any single pharmacological agent, carry minimal risks compared to anti-amyloid therapy, and improve overall health and quality of life.

The emerging evidence that cognitive reserve and other protective factors substantially modify the relationship between amyloid burden and cognitive decline suggests that not all amyloid-positive individuals require equivalent intervention intensity. Those with high cognitive reserve, excellent sleep quality, robust social engagement, and regular exercise may warrant less intensive biomarker screening and treatment than those lacking these protective factors. This more nuanced approach recognizes that Alzheimer's disease pathology represents one of multiple factors determining cognitive aging and that interventions addressing modifiable factors may be more beneficial than biomarker-driven pharmacological interventions in many individuals.

For individuals with objective cognitive impairment confirmed by neuropsychological testing, blood biomarkers combined with cognitive testing provide high diagnostic accuracy for Alzheimer's disease pathology, enabling earlier diagnosis than has been possible previously. Early diagnosis in this symptomatic population enables access to anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody treatments, which demonstrate modest but genuine effects at slowing cognitive decline, particularly when treatment is initiated early in disease and in individuals with low tau burden. While the absolute magnitude of cognitive slowing remains modest and treatment carries risks of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, the existence of a disease-modifying treatment that addresses the underlying pathology represents substantial progress in Alzheimer's care. Shared decision-making between clinicians and patients regarding the risks and benefits of early treatment should acknowledge individual variation in treatment tolerability, amyloid-related imaging abnormality risk factors, preferences regarding risk acceptance, and estimated remaining lifespan.

Implementation of early diagnosis strategies should prioritize equitable access through expanding availability of blood biomarkers in primary care settings and ensuring that all populations, regardless of socioeconomic status, geographic location, or racial or ethnic background, have access to biomarker testing and treatment. However, this expansion should proceed thoughtfully, with clear clinical practice guidelines distinguishing appropriate use of biomarkers to confirm suspected Alzheimer's disease pathology in symptomatic individuals from inappropriate screening of asymptomatic populations. Professional societies should collaborate to develop standardized pre-test and post-test counseling to ensure that individuals understand biomarker results as indicating pathological changes and risk of disease rather than definite current or future illness, reducing psychological harms of misinterpretation. Insurance policies and payment mechanisms should be structured to support evidence-based diagnostic algorithms rather than isolated biomarker testing, ensuring that expensive biomarker tests and treatments are reserved for those with appropriate clinical indications.

The balance between benefits and harms of early Alzheimer's diagnosis using biomarkers and cognitive tests ultimately cannot be resolved through scientific evidence alone, as it involves value judgments regarding the relative importance of early intervention, prevention of disease, autonomy, and avoidance of unnecessary treatment and harm. Future guidance should explicitly acknowledge these value dimensions and should support individual patients and clinicians in making decisions that align with patient values and preferences. As the field continues to refine biomarkers, develop more effective treatments, and expand understanding of disease natural history and trajectories, the balance will continue to evolve. However, the fundamental imperative remains constant: early diagnosis should enable early intervention for those destined to benefit while protecting those without clinical disease from the labeling, treatment, and harms associated with unnecessary medical intervention based on biomarker evidence alone.
