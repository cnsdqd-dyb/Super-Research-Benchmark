# How Effective Population Size Affects Genetic Load During Range Contraction Due to Climate Change

Effective population size (Ne) stands as one of the most critical determinants of how genetic load accumulates and impacts populations experiencing range contraction driven by climate change[1][4][31]. When populations contract their geographic ranges in response to climatic shifts, they simultaneously experience reductions in census size and Ne, creating a cascade of genetic consequences that fundamentally alter the composition and fitness implications of the genetic variation they carry. The relationship between Ne and genetic load during range contraction is not simply linear; rather, it involves complex interactions between genetic drift and natural selection, differential purging of highly deleterious versus mildly deleterious mutations, and the spatial dynamics of habitat fragmentation. Understanding these interactions is essential for developing effective conservation strategies for species facing rapid climate change. Contemporary research reveals that populations undergoing range contractions experience elevated genetic load despite potentially purging some of their most harmful mutations, a paradox that emerges from the differential efficacy of selection acting on mutations of varying effect sizes[2][18][29]. Moreover, the lag between demographic decline and the realization of genetic diversity loss creates a deceptive stability period during which populations may appear genetically healthy even as they accumulate deleterious mutations that will compromise their long-term viability[55]. This report synthesizes evidence from population genetics, conservation biology, and empirical studies of natural populations to elucidate how Ne modulates genetic load dynamics in the context of climate-driven range contraction, with implications for species conservation in the Anthropocene.

## Effective Population Size as a Foundational Concept in Evolutionary Genetics

Effective population size represents a crucial parameter that connects demographic processes to evolutionary outcomes, fundamentally determining the strength of genetic drift and the efficacy of natural selection within populations[1][4][31]. The concept was first formally introduced by Sewall Wright in 1931 as a means to measure the effect of genetic drift in populations, providing a theoretical framework for understanding how the random fluctuations of allele frequencies can shape genetic composition over time[6]. Unlike census population size, which simply counts the number of individuals present in a population at a given moment, effective population size accounts for the actual breeding structure of the population, including variation in reproductive success among individuals, unequal sex ratios, and temporal fluctuations in population size across generations[1][34]. In many natural populations, Ne is substantially lower than census size—sometimes by an order of magnitude or more—because not all individuals contribute equally to future generations[34]. The importance of Ne becomes starkly apparent when considering how it determines the rates at which genetic variation is lost through random drift, independent of selection[4][31]. Populations with smaller Ne experience stronger genetic drift, meaning that allele frequencies fluctuate more dramatically due to chance alone, and rare alleles are lost more readily regardless of their fitness effects[1][18].

The relationship between Ne and genetic drift can be quantified through the parameter \(2N_e\mu\), where \(\mu\) represents the mutation rate, with this expression determining the balance between new mutations entering a population and existing genetic variation being maintained[34]. When Ne is large, genetic drift acts weakly, and selection becomes the dominant force shaping allele frequencies; natural selection efficiently removes deleterious alleles and can readily fix beneficial mutations that increase fitness[18]. Conversely, when Ne is small, genetic drift becomes powerful enough to overwhelm selection, particularly for mutations with weak effects on fitness[18]. This threshold can be quantified through the inequality \(2N_e s < 1\), where \(s\) represents the selection coefficient, meaning that mutations with selection coefficients smaller than \(\frac{1}{2N_e}\) behave as if they are essentially neutral and subject primarily to drift rather than selection[18][29]. The implications are profound: a mutation that would be efficiently removed by selection in a large population may become fixed by genetic drift in a small population, even if it slightly reduces fitness[18]. This principle has cascading consequences for how genetic load evolves in populations undergoing range contraction, as declining Ne fundamentally alters which categories of mutations accumulate or are removed.

Recent advances in estimating Ne from genomic data have revealed considerable temporal and spatial variation in this critical parameter[1][34]. Linkage disequilibrium-based estimates of Ne can trace changes in effective population size through time, revealing how historical bottlenecks, expansions, and contractions have shaped the genetic composition of contemporary populations[1][34]. For example, studies of human populations using genome-wide linkage disequilibrium patterns have demonstrated that non-African populations experienced severe population bottlenecks during the out-of-Africa migration, reducing Ne to approximately 3,100 individuals, while African populations maintained substantially larger Ne values around 7,500[1]. These historical Ne changes have long-term consequences for genetic load; populations that experienced ancient bottlenecks may have purged highly deleterious mutations but accumulated slightly deleterious mutations that persist in modern genomes[18][29]. Moreover, Ne varies across the genome itself due to differences in recombination rates and selection pressures, with some genomic regions experiencing more drift and others more effectively cleared of deleterious mutations[34]. This heterogeneity in Ne across the genome creates complex patterns of genetic variation that conservation geneticists must consider when assessing population viability.

## Genetic Load: Types, Accumulation, and Expression in Small Populations

Genetic load encompasses the reduction in mean population fitness caused by the presence of deleterious mutations and represents a critical lens through which to understand the evolutionary consequences of small population size[9][12][15][29]. The concept can be subdivided into two complementary frameworks: potential genetic load, which refers to the total number of deleterious alleles present in a population regardless of whether they are currently expressed in homozygous form, and realized genetic load, which specifically quantifies the fitness reduction that manifests when these deleterious alleles are expressed as homozygotes in individual organisms[12][49]. This distinction proves essential for understanding how genetic load changes across the demographic spectrum, because deleterious alleles that exist as hidden heterozygotes in large populations do not immediately reduce fitness, but become potent fitness-reducers when inbreeding rises in small populations and forces these alleles into homozygous form[12][49]. The accumulation of genetic load can be further categorized by mutation effect size: highly deleterious mutations with large negative fitness effects, mildly deleterious mutations with small effects, and effectively neutral mutations whose fitness impacts are negligible[9][29]. These different classes of mutations respond differently to the competing forces of selection and drift, such that the composition of genetic load in small versus large populations diverges substantially over time[9][29].

In large, outbred populations, natural selection efficiently removes highly deleterious mutations because their fitness costs are large relative to the strength of drift, making them visible to selection even when rare[18][29]. Conversely, mildly deleterious mutations often escape removal by selection in large populations because their effects are weak enough that drift and other stochastic factors often overwhelm selective pressure[29]. However, in small, inbred populations, the dynamics reverse dramatically: highly deleterious mutations become more visible to selection because inbreeding forces them into homozygous form where their effects are fully expressed, allowing purifying selection to eliminate them more efficiently, a process called purging[9][26][29]. This seemingly counterintuitive pattern explains why some bottlenecked populations show evidence of having removed their most harmful mutations—the strong selective pressure from realized genetic load provides powerful selection against highly deleterious homozygotes[10][26][29]. In contrast, mildly deleterious mutations accumulate more readily in small populations because genetic drift can increase their frequency despite weak negative selection, and once they become common, selection against them remains weak and inefficient[15][26][29]. The empirical reality is that small populations typically experience a transition from a heavy load of highly deleterious mutations to a burden of mildly deleterious mutations, with overall genetic load often increasing despite purging of the most severe mutations[26][29].

The distinction between ancestral load and segregating load further clarifies genetic load dynamics[9][29]. Ancestral load refers to highly deleterious mutations that accumulated in the population before the bottleneck and are more readily purged during the intense inbreeding that follows population reduction[9][26][29]. Segregating load, by contrast, comprises mildly deleterious mutations that are present in intermediate frequencies at the time of bottleneck and are more prone to increase in frequency due to drift, effectively becoming fixed at higher rates than would occur in larger populations[29]. A comprehensive analysis of deleterious mutation dynamics across multiple small populations revealed that historical effective population size serves as a key indicator of the intensity of purifying selection against mildly deleterious alleles, such that populations with larger historical Ne successfully maintained stronger selection against these weakly harmful mutations[29]. The implications for range contraction are substantial: as populations contract and Ne declines, the efficacy of purifying selection weakens precisely when populations most need to maintain genetic variation and adaptive potential[20][25][29].

The realized genetic load in individuals depends critically on the degree of inbreeding, a parameter quantified by the inbreeding coefficient F[12][49]. When two related individuals mate, their offspring inherit the same deleterious alleles from both parents, resulting in homozygosity for these alleles at higher than expected frequencies[12]. The number of homozygous deleterious genotypes rises substantially with increasing F, meaning that inbred individuals in small populations carry more expressed genetic load than their counterparts in larger populations[12][15][29]. Even more concerning, the relationship between realized load and inbreeding is often nonlinear, such that a doubling of inbreeding coefficient can more than double the fitness reduction, particularly when many deleterious alleles segregate in the population[12][49]. Recent empirical studies measuring realized genetic load in populations of varying sizes reveal that small, long-term isolated populations consistently harbor significantly more deleterious homozygous mutations and exhibit higher frequencies of mildly deleterious variants than larger populations[15][29]. One study of multiple populations with diverse demographic histories found that the level of inbreeding proved to be the strongest predictor of realized loads of deleterious mutations, regardless of whether populations were historically large or small[29]. This finding suggests that contemporary inbreeding levels, which increase rapidly as populations contract, provide a reliable indicator of fitness risk from genetic load.

## Range Contraction Due to Climate Change: Mechanisms and Spatial Dynamics

Climate change drives species range contractions through the fundamental mechanism that warming temperatures create spatial mismatches between species' thermal tolerances and available habitat[14][17][43]. As climatic conditions shift, the geographic areas meeting species' environmental requirements shrink, forcing populations to either adapt in place, migrate to new suitable habitat, or face local extinction[14][17][43]. Range contractions differ fundamentally from range expansions in their genetic consequences because contracting populations lack the gene flow from a diverse, high-fitness core region that can rescue populations at the expanding edge[2][14]. In Mediterranean marine ecosystems, climate models project that endemic species will experience median range losses of 57 to 68 percent by 2050 under various emissions scenarios, with ranges contracting toward cooler refugia such as the northern Adriatic and northern Aegean[14]. These contractions do not occur uniformly; rather, they create fragmented ranges where suitable habitat persists as isolated patches separated by climatically unsuitable terrain[14]. The increasing distance between suitable habitat patches reduces connectivity and facilitates local extinctions, as populations become isolated and unable to exchange migrants that could rescue declining local populations[14][21]. This fragmentation effect has profound genetic consequences: metapopulation models demonstrate that mutation accumulation and extinction times are highly sensitive to habitat fragmentation, with extinction risks increasing dramatically as dispersal neighborhoods shrink and effective population sizes in isolated populations decline[21].

The speed of range contraction fundamentally modulates genetic load dynamics in ways that many conservation practitioners overlook[2]. Slow range contractions allow more time for selection to act and for populations to track favorable conditions, whereas rapid contractions driven by accelerating climate change limit the time available for selection to purge deleterious mutations and increase the likelihood that fitness-reducing variants will surf to high frequency due to genetic drift[2]. In simulation models of range shifts, populations shifting rapidly accumulated fewer mutations at the expanding front but experienced lower overall population fitness, effectively becoming trapped in a downward spiral where declining population numbers reduced the ability of selection to remove newly arising deleterious mutations[2]. Furthermore, unlike range expansions where a diverse core population can provide migrants to rescue edge populations, range shifts eliminate the species core as environmental conditions become unsuitable, removing the potential for genetic rescue through immigration[2]. Mathematical models predict that range shifts can lead to extinction because low-fitness populations can no longer grow sufficiently fast to colonize new habitat, with the shifting environmental conditions outpacing population expansion even in the absence of other threatening processes[2]. This creates what researchers term "spatial selection," where only the fastest-moving individuals contribute disproportionately to colonization of new areas, potentially altering trait distributions even if the shift occurs slowly[2][22].

The spatial structure of populations during range contraction creates what is sometimes termed "edge effects," where the leading edge of a contracting population experiences different selective pressures and demographic dynamics than the core[43]. Leading edge populations, where range expansion is occurring, experience expansion load due to gene surfing of deleterious mutations, but trailing edge populations, where extinction is occurring, are often locally adapted to unique environmental conditions and harbor adaptive variation that may be critical for species persistence[27][43]. Trailing edge populations that are better adapted to extreme climatic events relative to core populations may become increasingly important under future climates, yet these populations often experience the earliest extinctions during range contraction because habitat loss and fragmentation disproportionately affect geographic margins[43]. Theoretical work suggests that locally adapted range-edge populations at both leading and trailing edges are predicted to expand their distributions and become increasingly abundant throughout species' ranges under future climates, yet the concentration of anthropogenic habitat disturbances at range edges where many species' range margins converge may eliminate these locally-adapted populations before they can expand[43]. This pattern has been documented in multiple taxa: populations at species range edges often exhibit unique genotypes reflecting local adaptation to extreme conditions, but these populations are simultaneously most vulnerable to climate-induced extinction because their narrowly defined environmental niches will shrink under projected climate scenarios[43]. The loss of these trailing edge populations represents not merely a reduction in species range but a loss of adaptive genetic variation that may be irreplaceable.

## The Dynamics of Genetic Drift and Selection During Range Contraction

The fundamental tension between genetic drift and natural selection intensifies during range contraction, fundamentally altering which mutations accumulate and which are removed from populations[18][29]. In standard population genetics models, the efficacy of selection depends on the product of effective population size and selection coefficient, quantified as \(2N_e s\)[18][29]. When \(2N_e s \gg 1\), selection is efficient and deleterious alleles are effectively removed; when \(2N_e s \ll 1\), drift dominates and selection becomes inefficient regardless of how strongly an allele affects fitness[18][29]. During range contraction, Ne decreases, necessarily reducing the threshold above which selection becomes efficient—mutations with selection coefficients that would be clearly visible to selection in large populations become effectively neutral in small populations[18]. This fundamental shift in the strength of selection creates a window during which previously uncommon, mildly deleterious alleles can increase in frequency through drift, a process that is often irreversible on short timescales[18][29]. Research simulating populations under various levels of selection pressure and population size reveals that in smaller populations facing strong selection pressure, deleterious allele frequencies decrease more slowly than in larger populations, explaining why small populations often accumulate higher genetic loads despite experiencing apparent purging of the most severe mutations[18][29].

The efficacy of selection varies across the genome in ways that prove particularly consequential for range-contracting populations[34]. Genomic regions with lower recombination rates experience stronger genetic drift because selection acting on linked sites cannot as readily counteract drift-driven frequency changes of slightly deleterious mutations[34]. This creates genomic heterogeneity in genetic drift, such that effective population size for neutral loci in regions of low recombination is lower than in high-recombination regions, and consequently, deleterious mutations accumulate more readily in low-recombination regions[34]. During range contraction, populations face the double burden of reduced Ne globally and locally intense drift in low-recombination genomic regions, potentially creating mutational hotspots where genetic load concentrates. Furthermore, the expression of genetic load during range contraction depends critically on the degree of dominance of deleterious alleles[18][29]. Fully recessive deleterious mutations remain hidden in heterozygotes even in large populations, but as inbreeding increases due to population contraction, these hidden mutations are unmasked and exposed to selection[18][29]. The process generates complex dynamics: initially, purifying selection intensifies as recessive deleterious mutations become homozygous and visible, but over subsequent generations, if drift is strong, the exposed mutations may become fixed despite their negative effects, a phenomenon called "mutation surfing"[19][29]. This process has been documented empirically: analyses of the red flour beetle during experimental range expansions found little difference between core and edge populations in measures of genetic load using standard metrics, yet edge populations exhibited reduced fitness, suggesting that the genetic basis of fitness reduction involves complex dynamics beyond simple accumulation of identifiable deleterious mutations[19].

Purging during range contraction presents a paradox that challenges simple intuitions about how genetic load changes in small populations[26][29]. When populations contract sharply, highly deleterious mutations are exposed through increased homozygosity and are removed more efficiently than mildly deleterious mutations[10][26][29]. Empirical evidence from Alpine ibex, which experienced extreme population bottlenecks from thousands of individuals to a global population of fewer than 60, demonstrates this pattern: the bottleneck enabled purging of highly deleterious, recessive mutations, yet mildly deleterious mutations accumulated throughout, resulting in a net increase in overall genetic load despite the purging of severe mutations[26]. The implication is that range-contracting populations may simultaneously purge their most harmful mutations while accumulating mildly deleterious mutations, resulting in genetic profiles that appear favorable at first glance but harbor substantial cryptic genetic load[26][29]. This cryptic load represents a particularly insidious threat because it manifests primarily under environmental stress; individuals with high loads of mildly deleterious mutations maintain relatively normal fitness in benign conditions but experience severe fitness reductions when environmental conditions become challenging[25][53]. As climate change progresses, range-contracting populations will face precisely the kind of environmental stress—altered temperature regimes, novel precipitation patterns, increased disease pressure—that causes mildly deleterious mutations to be expressed, creating a delayed genetic crisis that may not be apparent until populations are already critically endangered.

## Empirical Evidence: Genetic Load Dynamics in Natural Range-Contracting Populations

Real-world studies of populations undergoing range contraction provide powerful evidence for the mechanisms outlined above, revealing patterns that emphasize both the severity of genetic load accumulation and the considerable lag between demographic decline and genetic erosion[13][16][55]. A comprehensive analysis of genetic diversity patterns across 628 species, assembled from 882 published studies that tracked intraspecific genetic diversity over time, found that two-thirds of the analyzed populations exhibited declining genetic diversity[16]. Critically, this decline occurred not only in populations recognized as threatened or endangered, but also in populations of more common species such as black-tailed prairie dogs and house sparrows that appeared demographically healthy[16]. The implication is sobering: genetic erosion is occurring across a far broader swath of biodiversity than is captured by conservation status classifications alone. The time lag between population size decline and genetic diversity loss, termed genetic drift debt, means that populations experiencing demographic collapse may not realize the full genetic consequences of that collapse for many generations[55]. In the regent honeyeater, a critically endangered Australian bird whose population has collapsed from thousands to fewer than 500 individuals over the past 80 years, genomic analysis revealed that despite this dramatic population reduction, genetic diversity has declined by only 9 percent, with forward-time simulations indicating that this time lag conceals ongoing genomic erosion that will accelerate in coming decades[55]. This pattern holds across multiple taxa, indicating that single-timepoint genetic assessments substantially underestimate the severity of genetic load accumulation in declining populations.

Mediterranean marine species provide particularly clear examples of how range contraction, genetic load, and climate change interact to threaten biodiversity[14][27]. Climate models project that endemic Mediterranean fishes, cephalopods, and mollusks will experience range contractions of 57 to 68 percent by 2050, with ranges fragmenting into isolated patches in the northern Mediterranean[14]. In a study of owl blenny fish along the range contraction gradient, populations at the trailing edge (where extinction is occurring) exhibited the highest levels of genomic load, despite maintaining substantial genetic diversity overall[27]. This counterintuitive pattern reflects the fact that trailing edge populations, being locally adapted to warm conditions, carry alleles that are beneficial in their current environment but will become deleterious as those environments warm further—in essence, the adaptive landscape that favored these alleles is shifting beneath the population's feet[27][43]. These trailing edge populations represent an evolutionary mismatch: they are genetically well-suited for conditions that are disappearing, yet the mutations they carry may prove maladaptive in the warmer futures they will encounter. The genomic offset—the mismatch between a population's current genetic composition and the genotypes that would be favored in future climatic conditions—increases dramatically for populations at the trailing edge, where the genetic architecture of adaptation to current conditions becomes increasingly mismatched to future environmental requirements[58].

Studies integrating population genomics with demographic data reveal that the relationship between genetic diversity and extinction risk is mediated by demographic context in ways that simple genetic metrics often obscure[25]. In a study of small-eared dogs that track year-to-year variation in population size and extinction risk alongside genomic metrics, the association between heterozygosity and extinction disappeared when demographic and environmental factors were included in models[25]. However, more nuanced analyses revealed significant context-dependent effects: low heterozygosity predicted higher extinction risk in large but not in small populations, while negative associations between heterozygosity, extinction, and mortality were detected in small populations with recent histories of decline[25]. These results indicate that the impact of genetic load on extinction risk depends critically on demographic trajectory, generation time, and current population size—factors that vary among species and determine whether genetic factors will manifest as limiting factors before other threats drive populations extinct. This finding has profound conservation implications: populations with genetic diversity patterns that appear concerning in isolation may actually pose modest extinction risk if demographic variables favor recovery, whereas populations with apparently sufficient genetic diversity may be at high risk if recent declines have created conditions favoring genetic load accumulation.

The accumulation of genetic load in fragmented landscapes demonstrates how spatial structure compounds the genetic consequences of range contraction[21][24]. Metapopulation models show that habitat fragmentation increases extinction risk through mutation accumulation, with the effect being particularly severe when dispersal between patches is limited[21][24]. As habitat becomes fragmented, isolated populations experience reduced effective sizes and lose the demographic resilience provided by immigration from intact core populations[21][24]. The consequence is that fragmented landscapes reduce the extinction genetic effective size of metapopulations below what simple calculations based on total population size would predict[21]. One study found that nearest-neighbor dispersal, typical of fragmented landscapes, produced the shortest median times to extinction due to mutation accumulation, while global dispersal that maintained connectivity substantially extended population persistence[21]. This pattern has direct relevance to climate-driven range contraction, where fragmentation is a near-inevitable consequence: as the geography of suitable climate becomes more patchy and dispersal between patches becomes more difficult due to intervening unsuitable climate, fragmentation effects compound genetic load accumulation, creating a synergistic threat to population persistence.

## Conservation Implications and Adaptive Management Strategies

The understanding that effective population size fundamentally determines genetic load accumulation has profound implications for conservation practice, suggesting that maintaining or restoring Ne should be a central conservation objective, potentially co-equal with maintaining census population size[28][32][35][52]. The classical "50/500" rule, developed in 1980, proposed that a minimum effective population size of 50 was necessary to avoid immediate inbreeding depression, while 500 was required to maintain evolutionary potential over longer timescales[32]. However, contemporary evidence suggests these thresholds may be inadequate for many species[32]. Even populations of effective size around 1,000 individuals can accumulate substantial burdens of mildly deleterious mutations, and populations smaller than 500 will likely experience substantial fitness declines over even modest timescales[26][29]. The implication is that conservation targets need to be species-specific and consider life history traits, current genetic load, and projected environmental changes, rather than applying universally[32][35]. For species with long generation times or low reproductive rates—large mammals, long-lived birds, slow-reproducing plants—the required effective population size to maintain viability may be substantially higher than for short-lived species with high reproductive rates[32][35].

Assisted migration, the deliberate human-mediated movement of climate-adapted populations, has emerged as one approach to counteracting genetic load accumulation and loss of adaptive potential in populations facing range contraction[38][41][57]. The underlying logic is that transferring individuals from populations currently adapted to future climates (either within the current range of a species or from adjacent populations) can introduce adaptive genetic variation that enables recipient populations to persist under novel climatic conditions[38][41]. Genomic approaches can identify the specific adaptive alleles present in potential source populations and select translocated individuals to maximize the introduction of genetic variation relevant to future climates[38][41]. However, assisted migration remains controversial because introducing non-local individuals risks outbreeding depression, where hybridization between adapted populations generates offspring poorly suited to either parental environment[38][57]. Recent theoretical and empirical work suggests that adaptive introgression—interspecific or intraspecific gene flow—can facilitate adaptive responses to climate change when it introduces alleles that increase fitness under new climatic conditions, particularly when source and recipient populations are related and share similar ecological requirements[57]. One compelling example involves adaptive introgression from warm-adapted populations to cold-adapted populations, where the warm-adapted alleles increase growth rates under warming conditions without substantially impairing cold tolerance in the short-term[57]. The key distinction is between maladaptive introgression, where incoming alleles reduce fitness, and adaptive introgression, where they enhance fitness—a distinction that genomic approaches are increasingly able to identify prospectively.

Habitat protection and restoration to maintain or restore connectivity emerges as perhaps the most robust conservation approach for preventing catastrophic genetic load accumulation in range-contracting populations[36][41]. By maintaining large, connected habitat patches, conservation actions prevent the severe population fragmentation that compounds genetic drift and enables rapid accumulation of deleterious mutations[21][24]. Modeling studies examining various management interventions found that increasing spatial heterogeneity and decreasing temporal variability in climate refugia benefited a wide range of species, with effects being most pronounced for species with short dispersal distances that depend on local environmental variation to track climate change[36]. This finding suggests that complex landscapes with diverse microclimatic conditions support species persistence better than homogenous landscapes, even under equivalent climatic averages[36]. In practice, this might involve protecting or restoring forest patches in agricultural landscapes, maintaining varied canopy structure to buffer interannual variation, or using wildlife-friendly farming practices that create stepping-stone habitat facilitating dispersal[36]. Maintaining corridors linking climate refugia proves particularly valuable, as these corridors allow species to disperse when necessary to track shifting suitable conditions[36]. From a genetic perspective, connectivity directly increases Ne by enabling gene flow between otherwise isolated populations, directly counteracting genetic drift[37]. Small but connected islands or habitat patches can maintain higher levels of genetic diversity than larger but isolated habitat areas, suggesting that connectivity should be prioritized in conservation planning alongside simply maximizing habitat area[37].

Genetic monitoring and adaptive management frameworks represent essential tools for assessing whether conservation actions are effectively maintaining genetic health in range-contracting populations[55]. Temporal genomic monitoring allows conservationists to detect accumulation of genetic load, changes in inbreeding levels, and loss of adaptive variation before these changes translate into demographic declines[55]. The integration of genomic essential biodiversity variables (EBVs)—including genetic diversity, effective population size, inbreeding, and adaptive variation—into conservation assessment frameworks enables more nuanced understanding of extinction risk than demographic data alone[55]. For example, a population exhibiting stable census size might simultaneously be accumulating genetic load due to invisible inbreeding, or conversely, a population experiencing demographic recovery might rapidly restore genetic diversity through influx of diverse migrants[55]. Conservation genomics using whole-genome sequencing enables identification of specific deleterious mutations enriched in small populations, allowing conservationists to potentially prioritize certain individuals for breeding or translocation based on their genetic backgrounds[49][52]. However, the accessibility and cost-effectiveness of genetic monitoring remain substantial challenges for most conservation programs, and proxy measures based on demographic data and population size remain important tools for conservation planning when direct genetic assessment is infeasible[16][35].

Climate adaptation strategies and genomic selection for drought tolerance offer promise for enabling populations to track environmental change even within current range boundaries[39][40][41]. In long-lived species such as conifers, which face climate change at rates potentially exceeding their natural migration rates, genomic selection of individuals carrying alleles associated with drought tolerance could accelerate evolutionary adaptation toward climate-appropriate genotypes[39][41]. However, the efficacy of such approaches depends critically on the heritability of climate-relevant traits and the amount of standing genetic variation available—factors that are often reduced in small populations[39][40]. Empirical studies of white spruce indicate that genomic prediction for drought response is feasible and can guide multi-trait selection that improves climate tolerance while maintaining other economically important traits[39]. The broader principle is that conservation should explicitly consider whether populations retain sufficient genetic variation for evolutionary rescue—the adaptation of genotypes toward climate-appropriate phenotypes—and if not, whether assisted gene flow, assisted migration, or genomic selection might augment adaptive potential[20][40][57].

## The Role of Environmental Context in Modulating Genetic Load Expression and Extinction Risk

A critical emerging insight is that the fitness costs of genetic load are not constant across environments but depend intimately on environmental stress and stability[25][36][53]. Inbreeding depression typically manifests most severely in stressful environments, such as during food scarcity, parasitic infection, or thermal stress, whereas inbred individuals may exhibit near-normal fitness in benign captive environments[8][25][53]. This genotype-by-environment interaction has profound implications for range-contracting populations, which will simultaneously experience reduced effective population size and increasingly stressful environments due to climate change[25][53]. Experimental studies separating the effects of different environmental stressors and inbreeding reveal that combinations of stressors impose non-additive fitness costs: a population facing both inbreeding depression and temperature stress may experience fitness reductions far exceeding the sum of the individual effects[53]. In one experiment with cowpea beetles, inbreeding depression decreased survival by only 12.7-14.0 percent in benign environments but increased survival reduction to 45.4-48.7 percent under host stress, and when both temperature and host stressors were present, survival dropped to less than 2 percent[53]. This pattern illustrates why range-contracting populations under climate change face compounding risks: genetic load that might be tolerable in current stable environments becomes catastrophic as environmental variability increases, creating a vicious cycle where environmental stress exacerbates the fitness costs of genetic load, potentially triggering demographic collapse even in populations that were previously stable.

Parental care and other traits that buffer against environmental stress may modulate the expression of genetic load in ways that affect population persistence, though the magnitude of these buffering effects remains incompletely understood[50]. In burying beetles, maternal care significantly reduced the severity of inbreeding depression in survival-related traits, with the buffering effect being strongest for traits where environmental stress from competition and starvation would otherwise exacerbate fitness costs of inbreeding[50]. This finding suggests that populations with evolved mechanisms for ameliorating environmental stress—whether through parental care, social behavior, or phenotypic plasticity—may be somewhat more resistant to fitness declines from genetic load than populations lacking such mechanisms[50]. However, these buffering effects are likely insufficient to prevent substantial fitness consequences in populations experiencing severe range contraction, particularly under the rapid environmental changes associated with climate change. Density-dependent fitness, where individuals in smaller populations suffer less density-dependent stress because populations are below carrying capacity, can also reduce the realized genetic load compared to predictions based on inbreeding coefficients alone[52]. In other words, small populations sometimes avoid the severe inbreeding depression seen in laboratory studies because reduced population density removes some sources of environmental stress, creating a paradoxical situation where small populations may appear more viable than expected[52]. However, this density-dependent buffering is likely transient—as environmental conditions deteriorate under climate change, populations will experience more stress, revealing hidden genetic load and potentially triggering rapid population decline.

## Conclusion and Future Perspectives

The relationship between effective population size, genetic load, and range contraction under climate change emerges from this synthesis as profoundly complex, defying simple models but yielding actionable insights for conservation practice. Fundamentally, declining effective population size during range contraction weakens the efficacy of natural selection, creating conditions favoring accumulation of mildly deleterious mutations while simultaneously creating selective opportunities for purging of highly deleterious mutations[18][29]. This paradoxical outcome means that range-contracting populations often experience increasing total genetic load despite purging of severe mutations, a pattern that has now been documented empirically across multiple taxa and is predicted by population genetics theory[26][29][55]. The spatial dynamics of range contraction—fragmentation, isolation of populations, loss of core populations that provide genetic rescue—compound the effects of drift-driven genetic load accumulation, such that genetic erosion in fragmented ranges proceeds faster than in connected populations of equivalent total size[21][24]. The time lag between demographic collapse and the realization of genetic load, termed genetic drift debt, means that populations appearing genetically healthy at a single timepoint may harbor substantial cryptic load that will manifest under environmental stress or in future generations, creating a deceptive stability period that lulls conservation practitioners into complacency[55]. Most concerningly, the fitness costs of genetic load are not fixed but increase dramatically under environmental stress, meaning that range-contracting populations simultaneously experiencing increasingly harsh climatic conditions will face multiplicative, not additive, fitness reductions from combined effects of genetic load and environmental change[25][53].

These findings argue urgently for conservation approaches prioritizing maintenance and restoration of effective population size through habitat protection, connectivity maintenance, and in some cases, assisted gene flow[36][38][41]. The conventional conservation focus on maximizing census population size or habitat area is necessary but may be insufficient; explicit attention to Ne, genomic monitoring, and potential interventions such as assisted migration or genomic selection for climate-adapted traits may be essential for many species to persist through the rapid climate changes projected for coming decades[39][40][41]. For species with long generation times or currently small populations, conservation actions taken immediately may represent the only opportunity to prevent mutational meltdown, the catastrophic spiral where accumulating deleterious mutations drive demographic decline which further elevates genetic load, ultimately driving extinction[51][54]. Conversely, the finding that even populations with Ne in the thousands can accumulate substantial genetic load suggests that recovery plans relying on achieving specific census size targets without attention to genetic factors may be inadequate[26][29]. Future research should integrate temporal genomic monitoring with demographic data and environmental measurements to develop predictive models of genetic load dynamics under different climate change scenarios, enabling conservation prioritization based on explicit predictions of which populations are most vulnerable to genetic erosion. As species distributions shift in response to climate change, the effective population size of those populations will become one of the most consequential parameters determining whether they can adapt, persist, and evolve in their new environments—making Ne not merely an abstract population genetics parameter but a fundamental driver of extinction risk in the Anthropocene.
