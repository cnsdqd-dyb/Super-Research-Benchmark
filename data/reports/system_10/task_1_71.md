# Uncertainty Propagation in Cumulative Effect Frameworks and Its Influence on Environmental Permit Threshold Setting

Environmental permitting decisions fundamentally depend on establishing threshold values that distinguish acceptable from unacceptable impacts. Yet these decisions are increasingly complicated by the need to account for cumulative effects and the inherent uncertainties that propagate through complex assessment methodologies. This report synthesizes current scientific understanding and regulatory practice regarding how uncertainty propagates through cumulative effect frameworks and subsequently influences the threshold determinations that underpin permit decisions. The key finding is that traditional deterministic threshold approaches inadequately capture the full range of possible outcomes when multiple stressors and their associated uncertainties interact, necessitating more sophisticated probabilistic approaches that explicitly characterize uncertainty distributions. This shift from point estimates to probability distributions fundamentally reshapes how permit thresholds are established, creating both scientific and regulatory challenges that require careful integration of technical rigor, precautionary principles, and transparent decision-making frameworks.

## Fundamentals of Uncertainty Propagation in Environmental Assessment

### The Nature and Sources of Environmental Uncertainty

Uncertainty in environmental assessment operates on multiple dimensions, each contributing to the overall challenge of establishing defensible permit thresholds. **Propagation of uncertainty** in the statistical sense refers to the effect of variables' uncertainties on the uncertainty of a function based on them[4]. When measurement variables contain uncertainties due to measurement limitations and instrumentation precision, these uncertainties propagate through mathematical functions and models, compounding at each step of an assessment[4]. This fundamental principle creates a cascading effect where initial measurement errors or data gaps become amplified as information flows through increasingly complex analytical frameworks.

Environmental uncertainty manifests in several distinct forms that operate simultaneously in permitting contexts. **Variability** refers to the inherent heterogeneity or diversity of data in an assessment, expressing itself through statistical metrics such as variance, standard deviation, and interquartile ranges that reflect the spread of a set of values[46][53]. This type of uncertainty is irreducible because it represents genuine differences in populations, exposures, or environmental conditions that cannot be eliminated through additional sampling. For example, when assessing air pollution exposure in a community, variability exists in measured pollution concentrations due to when, where, and how measurements are taken, and also within the population itself as different individuals engage in different activities that result in different exposure levels[46].

Conversely, **uncertainty** refers to a lack of data or incomplete understanding of the assessment context[46][53]. Unlike variability, uncertainty can potentially be reduced through additional information gathering, improved measurement methods, or better conceptual understanding of underlying processes. Uncertainty can be either qualitative, arising from a lack of knowledge about factors affecting exposure, or quantitative, emerging from the use of non-precise measurement methods[53]. Chemical concentrations in environmental media can be approximated using assumptions (introducing more uncertainty) or described using measured data (reducing uncertainty)[53]. This distinction proves critical in permit threshold determinations because it suggests that some sources of uncertainty are amenable to reduction through improved data collection and analysis, while others represent irreducible features of the environment being assessed.

### Mathematical Foundations of Uncertainty Propagation

The mathematical treatment of uncertainty propagation provides the technical framework for understanding how individual uncertainties combine. For linear combinations of variables, the general expression for propagating error from one set of variables to another follows established formulas[4]. When errors on input variables are uncorrelated, the variance of output variables can be calculated using matrix notation where the covariance matrix is transformed through the sensitivity coefficients of the system[4]. This approach proves tractable for linear systems, but environmental permitting frequently involves non-linear relationships between inputs and outputs.

In non-linear combinations, which characterize most environmental fate and transport models, the function must typically be linearized by first-order Taylor series expansion to propagate uncertainty[4]. Under this approach, the partial derivatives of the output function with respect to each input variable serve as the sensitivity coefficients, transforming the input covariance matrix to generate the output covariance matrix[4]. However, this linearization approach has inherent limitations when the uncertainties are large relative to the curvature of the underlying function, potentially producing inaccurate representations of the output uncertainty distribution.

More sophisticated uncertainty propagation techniques have been developed to handle nonlinear, nonmonotonic systems or cases where input parameters range over several orders of magnitude[18]. The **Monte Carlo method** has emerged as a primary tool for propagating uncertainty through complex models[18]. Monte Carlo analysis involves repeatedly sampling from probability distributions of input parameters and calculating corresponding model outputs for each realization, creating an empirical distribution of results that captures both uncertainty and variability[18]. This approach requires no assumptions about functional form and can handle discontinuities and non-linearities that confound analytical methods[4].

The challenge in applying these mathematical frameworks to permit threshold setting lies in translating probabilistic representations of uncertainty into defensible regulatory decisions. A deterministic point estimate masks the full range of possible outcomes, while a full probability distribution of results requires decision-makers to determine what probability levels justify permit denial or conditional approval. **Point estimate methods** offer a compromise approach, using carefully selected representative values from probability distributions to characterize central tendency and variability with minimal computational burden[1]. These methods prove particularly valuable for uncertainty propagation in applications where computational efficiency matters and non-linear systems predominate, as is the case in regulatory modeling[1].

### Correlation and Interaction Effects in Uncertainty Propagation

A critical but often overlooked dimension of uncertainty propagation involves correlation between variables. If uncertainties are correlated, covariance must be explicitly accounted for in uncertainty calculations[4]. Correlation can arise from two distinct sources: first, measurement errors themselves may be correlated when instruments or procedures share common sources of error; second, underlying values may be correlated across populations or spatial domains, which in turn creates correlation in the uncertainties of aggregated values[4].

The effect of correlation on uncertainty propagation produces counterintuitive results in some cases. When calculating differences between two variables, if those variables are uncorrelated, their difference exhibits greater variance than either variable individually[4]. However, an increasing positive correlation between the variables decreases the variance of the difference, converging toward zero variance for perfectly correlated variables with identical variance[4]. Conversely, negative correlation further increases the variance of the difference compared to the uncorrelated case[4]. These correlation effects have significant implications for cumulative impact assessments where permit decisions often depend on comparing predicted impacts to threshold values, and the correlation structure between different stressor pathways strongly influences the width of confidence intervals around the final decision metric.

## Cumulative Effects Assessment Frameworks and Their Complexity

### Defining Cumulative Impacts and Effects

**Cumulative impacts** are defined as the totality of exposures to combinations of chemical and nonchemical stressors and their effects on health, well-being, and quality of life outcomes[6]. These impacts include contemporary exposures to multiple stressors as well as exposures throughout a person's lifetime, and they are influenced by the distribution of stressors across both direct and indirect exposure pathways[6]. Cumulative impacts can be considered in the context of individuals, geographically defined communities, or definable population groups[6]. The spatial and temporal scales of cumulative impact assessments fundamentally affect which stressors are included and how their interactions are characterized, with broader assessments necessarily incorporating more sources of uncertainty.

A **cumulative impact assessment** is defined operationally as a process of evaluating both quantitative and qualitative data representing cumulative impacts to inform a decision[6]. This assessment requires a systematic approach to characterize the combined effects from exposures to both chemical and non-chemical stressors over time across the affected population group or community[6]. The posited elements of a comprehensive cumulative impact assessment include community role throughout the process, combined impacts across multiple chemical and non-chemical stressors from multiple sources, multiple exposure pathways across environmental media, characterization of community vulnerability and resilience, exposures during relevant past and future periods with particular attention to vulnerable lifestages, distribution of environmental burdens and benefits, individual variability and behaviors, and explicit treatment of uncertainty and variability associated with the data and information[6].

This comprehensive scope creates profound challenges for threshold setting because it requires decision-makers to integrate information across multiple domains—toxicology, epidemiology, environmental fate and transport, exposure science, and social determinants of health—each bringing its own constellation of uncertainties. Traditional regulatory thresholds, which typically address single pollutants or stressors in isolation, cannot adequately capture the emergent properties of multiple simultaneous exposures where interactions and synergisms may amplify risks beyond the sum of individual effects.

### Stressor Interactions and Nonlinear Response Functions

A central complication in cumulative effects assessment involves understanding how multiple stressors interact when present simultaneously. **Synergy** is conventionally defined as a combined effect of multiple stressors that exceeds the sum of individual stressor effects[29]. Synergies are caused by amplifying feedbacks and can provoke unpredictable ecological surprises that accelerate biodiversity loss and impair ecosystem functioning[29]. Understanding synergistic interactions requires knowledge of the underlying biological and physical mechanisms by which stressors affect systems, and this mechanistic understanding often remains incomplete, introducing substantial uncertainty into cumulative effects predictions.

The mathematical treatment of interaction effects requires selecting appropriate null models or baseline assumptions against which interactions are evaluated[29][26]. Different null models can yield dramatically different conclusions about whether interactions are synergistic or antagonistic[29]. For metrics such as growth that accumulate linearly, an additive null model may be appropriate, but for metrics such as mortality representing probabilistic events, a multiplicative (risk) model better reflects the underlying biology[29]. When individual stressors have opposing effects on a biological response, defining the boundary between synergy and antagonism becomes particularly subtle, and the choice of null model can determine whether combined effects are classified as harmful or beneficial[29].

The complexity becomes even more pronounced when addressing stress modulation and adaptation. Environmental stressors can interact through mechanisms including immune system modulation, enzyme induction, and metabolic trade-offs[26][48]. For example, the stress addition model demonstrates that pesticide mixtures, environmental stress, and fitness costs of pesticide adaptation in organisms can act synergistically, with strong synergistic potential indicating critical importance of correctly predicting multiple stresses for risk assessment[26]. Traditional models such as concentration addition and effect addition substantially underestimate combined effects when synergistic interactions occur, creating systematic bias toward underestimating cumulative impacts[26].

### Integration of Chemical and Nonchemical Stressors

Contemporary cumulative effects frameworks increasingly recognize that environmental burdens include nonchemical stressors that interact with chemical exposures to modify risk[20][25]. **Physical stressors** such as heat, noise, radiation, and proximity to transportation corridors represent direct environmental hazards that can modify both exposure to and biological response to chemical contaminants[48]. **Psychosocial stressors** including poverty, educational disparities, linguistic isolation, and health disparities create vulnerabilities that amplify responses to environmental hazards[20][25]. **Socioeconomic factors** such as employment status, income, and energy cost burdens constrain adaptive capacity and resilience[25].

The challenge in integrating nonchemical stressors into threshold-setting frameworks lies in the scarcity of quantitative dose-response relationships between these factors and health endpoints[20]. Social scientists and public health experts note that scientific evidence for general weighting of social and demographic factors remains very limited and does not support assignment of valid weights to all components of composite vulnerability indices[20]. This gap between the growing recognition that nonchemical stressors matter and the limited quantitative methods for characterizing their contributions creates a fundamental uncertainty in cumulative effects assessments that cannot be resolved through traditional statistical approaches alone.

## Threshold Setting in Environmental Permitting

### Traditional Approaches to Threshold Determination

Environmental permitting historically employs threshold values that define the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable impacts. For air quality, **National Ambient Air Quality Standards** establish maximum concentrations above which air quality is deemed harmful to public health[47]. For water quality, **water quality standards** specify numeric criteria and narrative descriptions of desired conditions[3]. For hazardous waste, **cleanup levels** are derived from risk-based models that translate concentrations to health risks[43]. These thresholds typically employ a deterministic approach in which a single point estimate of the critical parameter is compared to a fixed threshold, with impacts deemed acceptable if predicted values fall below the threshold and unacceptable if they exceed it.

The establishment of numeric thresholds requires making specific assumptions about which percentile or confidence level is appropriate for protection[43]. For example, compliance monitoring assumes concentrations do not exceed a criterion unless evidence proves otherwise, and thus uses lower confidence limits that, if exceeded, indicate confidence that actual concentrations exceed the standard[43]. Remedial action sites assume concentrations exceed a criterion initially, and thus use upper confidence limits such that if concentrations fall below the criterion, confidence exists that the site has been remediated appropriately[43]. These one-sided versus two-sided confidence interval decisions prove critical to permit threshold applications because they determine the asymmetry of acceptable versus unacceptable risk.

The confidence level selected for threshold application typically reflects both regulatory requirements and practical considerations[43]. Federal or state environmental law sometimes prescribes specific confidence levels (e.g., 95% confidence), while in other contexts decision-makers have discretion to select confidence levels based on project-specific needs[43]. However, when confidence intervals rely on assumptions about data distribution that may not hold—particularly when data are skewed, contain nondetects, or represent complex nonlinear relationships—the actual protection provided by a nominally 95% confidence level may differ substantially from the intended level.

### Cumulative Impact Analysis in Permitting Decisions

Recent regulatory developments increasingly require cumulative impact analysis as an explicit component of permit decisions[3][6]. Minnesota's 2023 cumulative impacts law requires the state pollution control agency to conduct cumulative impacts analyses for air permit decisions involving construction or expansion of new facilities or reissuance of existing permits in environmental justice areas[3][25]. These analyses must comprehensively examine past and current pollution burdens, document who impacts will affect, gather stakeholder and community views, stress uncertainties that cannot be addressed, and provide an assessment of overall impact[25].

The Minnesota regulatory approach specifies that cumulative impacts analyses should employ quantitative, semiquantitative, or qualitative methods to measure impact[3][25]. Quantitative methods include impact thresholds defined as values below which impact is acceptable and above which adverse effects may occur[25]. Semiquantitative methods employ scales based on low, medium, high impact rankings[25]. Qualitative methods describe impacts narratively, particularly for non-chemical stressors or unquantifiable indicators that supplement quantified chemical risks[25]. The flexibility in methodology acknowledges that different assessment contexts require different levels of analytical sophistication, but this flexibility also introduces discretion in how thresholds are ultimately determined and applied.

When cumulative impacts analyses identify unacceptable impacts in environmental justice areas, permitting regulations increasingly require community benefit agreements between facilities and affected residents[25]. These agreements represent an alternative to traditional numeric threshold approaches, instead negotiating project-specific mitigation measures and community benefits. This shift from purely technical threshold determination to stakeholder-negotiated outcomes reflects recognition that cumulative impacts assessments involve both technical uncertainty and value judgments about acceptable risk distribution.

### Permitting Principles and Threshold Predictability

A comprehensive perspective on environmental permitting reform emphasizes fundamental principles that underpin effective threshold setting[2]. **Accountability** requires that schedules and deadlines for permit decisions be maintained, providing stakeholders reasonable assurance of when decisions will be made[2]. **Transparency** demands that all parties understand permitting requirements, which agency has responsibility for each action, when actions will occur, and what happens next[2]. **Predictability** calls for clearly articulated requirements necessary to reach a decision, specifying what constitutes a "complete" application and the level of detail necessary to support decisions[2]. **Adaptability** acknowledges that permitting guidelines should evolve as technology, critical needs, and national priorities change, while maintaining baselines of environmental protection[2].

These principles highlight a critical tension in cumulative effects permitting: increasing scientific sophistication in characterizing cumulative impacts and their uncertainties necessarily reduces predictability because more complex analyses reveal greater variability and uncertainty in outcomes. A deterministic threshold approach provides surface-level predictability through its apparent simplicity, but this simplicity masks fundamental uncertainties that emerge only when one examines underlying assumptions and data limitations. More rigorous probabilistic approaches sacrifice this surface predictability to gain scientific accuracy and transparency regarding what is actually known and unknown.

## How Uncertainty Propagation Affects Permit Threshold Setting

### Expanding Uncertainty Through Sequential Model Steps

Cumulative effects assessments necessarily involve sequential modeling steps, each introducing additional sources of uncertainty that propagate forward to final threshold comparisons. A typical air quality permitting application requires first predicting emissions from a proposed facility, then predicting atmospheric transport and chemical transformation of those emissions, then characterizing background air quality in the assessment region, and finally summing facility impacts with background to derive a design concentration that is compared to air quality standards. At each step, uncertainties in model inputs, model structure, and parameter values accumulate.

**Model accuracy and uncertainty** in permit demonstrations proves particularly significant because even sophisticated regulatory models contain inherent limitations[11]. The EPA's regulatory air quality model, AERMOD, represents state-of-practice atmospheric dispersion modeling, yet this model was developed under specified meteorological conditions and may not accurately represent all real-world scenarios, especially under extreme conditions or complex terrain[11]. The uncertainty in model predictions themselves—separate from uncertainties in input parameters—contributes meaningfully to overall uncertainty in permit decisions. Furthermore, background air quality estimation introduces additional uncertainty because monitoring networks have limited spatial coverage, and interpolating concentrations to unmonitored locations requires assumptions about spatial correlation and temporal representativeness[15][38].

A Monte Carlo approach to combining these sequential uncertainties demonstrates the magnitude of propagation effects. When facility-predicted concentrations and background concentrations are independently uncertain, simply summing their 99th percentiles (as traditional methods often do) implicitly assumes that maximum concentrations from each source occur simultaneously, which is highly unlikely[15]. This approach is mathematically equivalent to choosing the 99.99th percentile of the combined distribution, yielding unduly conservative design concentrations that may unnecessarily burden sources with restrictive permit conditions[15]. A more accurate approach respects the seasonality of each distribution and uses Monte Carlo methods to randomly pair facility and background values from similar seasonal periods, more realistically characterizing the combined uncertainty[15].

### Confidence Intervals and Threshold Exceedance Probability

The relationship between uncertainty representation and threshold setting becomes explicit when one considers confidence intervals. A **confidence interval** is a statistical interval designed to bound the true value of a population parameter such as the mean or an upper percentile[43]. For a 95% confidence interval of the mean chemical concentration, if a group of wells was sampled 100 times, 95 of those times the measured mean would fall within the calculated interval if the distributional model fits the data[43]. However, the actual protection provided by a 95% confidence limit depends critically on distributional assumptions. When data are non-normal, skewed, or contain censored values, standard parametric confidence interval approaches may fail to provide the intended coverage.

The choice between one-sided and two-sided confidence limits proves central to permit threshold application. A **lower confidence limit** provides protection against underestimation of impacts, ensuring with selected confidence that actual values exceed a criterion if the lower bound exceeds it[43]. An **upper confidence limit** provides assurance that remediation has succeeded in reducing concentrations, allowing permits or cleanups to be considered complete when the upper bound falls below a standard[43]. However, these asymmetric confidence limits create different implicit risk allocations. A 95% lower confidence limit allocates 5% false negative risk to permitting (allowing a facility when impacts actually exceed standards), while a 95% upper confidence limit allocates 5% false positive risk to remediation (declaring success when residual impacts may persist). Different stressors and decisions may justify different allocations.

The cumulative nature of multiple stressors compounds this challenge. When a cumulative assessment integrates risks from multiple pathways and stressors, does one apply confidence limits to each individual component and then combine them, or does one apply confidence limits to the combined result? The former approach leads to undercounting of overall uncertainty (the propagation problem), while the latter requires having sufficient data to construct confidence intervals for the combined metric, which is often infeasible. The **generalized propagation formula** using Jacobian matrices provides one approach to this problem but requires calculating partial derivatives of the combined metric with respect to each input parameter, which becomes intractable when individual components themselves involve complex models[4].

### Nonlinear Dose-Response Functions and Risk Characterization

Permit threshold setting fundamentally depends on dose-response relationships that characterize how impacts change with exposure concentration. For many stressors, these relationships are nonlinear, with effects accelerating at higher dose ranges or exhibiting threshold phenomena where impacts remain negligible until some critical dose is exceeded. Uncertainty in characterizing dose-response relationships introduces substantial uncertainty into threshold determination because the same exposure concentration can correspond to vastly different risk levels depending on which portion of the dose-response curve applies.

**Benchmark dose modeling** represents a modern approach to characterizing dose-response uncertainty that has been adopted by many regulatory agencies[34][55]. Rather than selecting a single critical effect level from animal studies, benchmark dose approaches identify the dose corresponding to a predetermined level of effect (e.g., 10% increase in incidence) and develop confidence bounds around this dose, explicitly characterizing uncertainty in dose-response parameter estimation[34]. This approach better captures the distribution of plausible dose-response relationships than traditional approaches, but it requires selecting both the effect level of interest and the confidence level for bounds, both involving value judgments beyond pure science.

**Model uncertainty** in dose-response relationships presents a particular challenge because different mathematical models often fit experimental data equally well within the range of observed doses, yet extrapolate to very different predictions at low doses relevant to regulatory decisions[34]. Dose-response models combining linear, sublinear, and superlinear dose-response patterns can yield infectious dose estimates differing by orders of magnitude at the same excess risk level[34]. Model averaging methods have been proposed to account for this model uncertainty by weighting multiple plausible models rather than selecting a single "best" model[34]. However, model averaging itself introduces uncertainty about the appropriate weighting of different models and requires additional assumptions about the probability distribution of the true model within the set of candidate models.

### Interactions Between Stressors and Synergistic Effects

Perhaps the most significant way that uncertainty propagation affects permit thresholds involves interactions between multiple stressors. Traditional permitting often assumes additivity of effects, implicitly treating the combined risk from multiple stressors as the sum of individual risks[39][45]. However, both theoretical understanding and empirical evidence demonstrate that synergistic and antagonistic interactions are common, violating additivity assumptions. When synergistic interactions occur, additive models systematically underestimate combined risks, creating a bias toward underprotection[26][39].

The quantification of interaction effects requires understanding mechanisms by which different stressors modify each other's effects[48]. For pesticide-temperature stress combinations in aquatic organisms, pesticide adaptation reduces organisms' general stress capacity, creating a trade-off that increases vulnerability to combined stress[26]. Immune system challenges modify responses to chemical stressors through multiple mechanisms including enzyme induction and metabolic redirection[48]. Heat stress modifies both exposure to and biological response of chemical contaminants through changes in distribution coefficients, metabolic rates, and physiological tolerance[48]. In each case, the interaction effect depends on characteristics of the stressors, the organism or ecosystem, and environmental conditions—creating complex conditional relationships that cannot be reduced to simple multiplicative or additive formulas.

The **stress addition model** offers a theoretically grounded approach to predicting interaction types by calculating the general stress imposed by each individual stressor and then summing these general stresses[26]. Under this model, organisms maintain a finite stress capacity, and when multiple stressors drive total stress above this capacity, responses escalate rapidly, yielding synergistic effects[26]. The stress addition model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy compared to traditional concentration addition and effect addition models for pesticide mixtures combined with temperature stress[26], suggesting that physiologically based models of stress accumulation better characterize cumulative effects than conventional toxicological models.

Nevertheless, substantial uncertainty remains regarding which interaction model applies in specific permitting scenarios, how general stress capacity varies across populations and conditions, and how to estimate parameters for quantitative application. The presence of this uncertainty means that permit thresholds established under assumption of additivity contain hidden safety factors or hidden risks depending on whether actual interactions are synergistic (more protective than assumed) or antagonistic (less protective than assumed). Neither scenario is desirable—excessive conservatism can impose unnecessary costs on facilities and economic sectors, while insufficient conservatism fails to protect public health and environmental quality. Explicit characterization and communication of interaction uncertainty proves essential to making this implicit risk allocation transparent.

## The EPA Framework for Cumulative Risk Assessment and Uncertainty

### Evolution of EPA Cumulative Risk Assessment Guidance

The EPA's approach to cumulative risk assessment has evolved substantially over several decades, with increasing recognition of both the scientific complexity and the importance of characterizing uncertainty. The **Framework for Cumulative Risk Assessment** initially developed in 2003 identified basic elements of cumulative risk assessment including planning and scoping, hazard identification, exposure assessment, dose-response assessment, and risk characterization[30]. The framework acknowledged that cumulative risk extends beyond single chemical assessments mandated by legislation such as the Food Quality Protection Act, which explicitly directed EPA to assess cumulative effects of pesticides with common mechanisms of toxicity considering aggregate dietary and nonoccupational exposure pathways[30].

More recent guidance developments have emphasized the integration of nonchemical stressors and environmental justice considerations into cumulative assessment frameworks[13]. In November 2024, EPA released a draft Interim Framework for Advancing Consideration of Cumulative Impacts that set out principles for EPA to consider cumulative impacts in agency decisions, with examples of EPA actions that have engaged these principles[13]. In January 2025, EPA finalized the Cumulative Risk Assessment Guidelines developed from extensive consultation with scientific advisory bodies and stakeholder input[13]. Simultaneously, the National Environmental Justice Advisory Council published recommendations urging EPA to use cumulative impact assessments to reduce disproportionate exposures in overburdened communities and to apply the precautionary principle in permitting decisions[36][49].

These recent guidance documents reflect a fundamental shift from deterministic to probabilistic characterization of cumulative risk. Traditional EPA risk assessments often relied on point estimates for key parameters with safety factors applied to account for uncertainty[55]. This approach was computationally simple and yielded readily interpretable results—either a facility exceeded acceptable risk thresholds or it did not. However, this apparent simplicity masked the fundamental uncertainties that actually exist, and provided limited transparency regarding which assumptions were most consequential for permit decisions. The newer guidance increasingly emphasizes probabilistic approaches that explicitly characterize distributions of possible outcomes[27][35].

### Probabilistic Risk Assessment Approaches for Vulnerable Populations

**Probabilistic risk assessment** enhances each phase of risk assessment—hazard identification, dose-response assessment, exposure assessment, and risk characterization—to produce more scientifically robust assessment of risk for susceptible populations[27]. Probabilistic approaches have several advantages over deterministic methods when addressing cumulative impacts in vulnerable communities. First, they leverage existing data to quantify the range of potential risk estimates rather than obscuring variability behind point estimates[27]. Second, they quantify both variability (inherent heterogeneity that cannot be eliminated) and uncertainty (gaps in knowledge that might be reducible) separately, allowing decision-makers to understand which sources of imprecision are epistemically fixable and which are ontologically fundamental[27]. Third, they foster communication and improve decision-making by revealing the distribution of possible outcomes and allowing stakeholders to understand cumulative probability distributions rather than single values.

The application of probabilistic risk assessment to vulnerable populations reveals how risk characterization necessarily becomes more complex when one explicitly accounts for heterogeneity within populations[27]. A chosen regulatory risk level may correspond to different probabilities of adverse effect across different subpopulations depending on how sensitivity and susceptibility vary[27]. For example, children, pregnant women, elderly individuals, and those with pre-existing respiratory or cardiac conditions typically have heightened susceptibility to air pollutants, yet a single National Ambient Air Quality Standard supposedly protects all these groups equally. Probabilistic approaches that characterize how risk varies across population subgroups make this heterogeneity explicit, allowing permitting authorities and advocates to determine whether a single threshold adequately protects the most susceptible groups.

**Bayesian network models** represent an increasingly applied probabilistic framework for integrating diverse information types and explicitly managing uncertainty in cumulative risk assessments[32][35]. Bayesian networks are directed acyclic graphs where causal relationships and associated uncertainty are quantified in conditional probability tables[35]. These models inherently incorporate uncertainty and can integrate expert elicitation with empirical data, allowing systematic combination of multiple information sources even when data gaps exist[32]. Bayesian approaches prove particularly valuable for cumulative assessments because they support bidirectional reasoning, both predictive (from causes to effects) and diagnostic (from observations to potential causes)[32], allowing permitting authorities to explore how different management scenarios would change cumulative risk distributions.

### Handling Uncertainty in Cumulative Assessment Decision-Making

The integration of uncertainty characterization into cumulative assessment decision-making creates both scientific and practical challenges. One fundamental question involves how to translate probabilistic characterizations of cumulative risk into defensible permit decisions. If a cumulative risk assessment produces a probability distribution showing that impacts exceed protective thresholds in 30% of plausible scenarios, what decision is justified? Setting a threshold below which the probability of exceedance is acceptably low (e.g., 5% or 10%) represents a policy choice beyond science, yet such choices are unavoidable when making concrete permit decisions.

The **precautionary principle** has been advanced as one framework for navigating these technical uncertainties[28][36][52]. The precautionary principle suggests that when activities raise threats of harm to the environment or human health, precautionary measures should be taken even if cause-and-effect relationships are not fully established scientifically[28]. In the context of cumulative permitting, this principle would suggest that when uncertainty is substantial and cumulative impacts appear potentially significant, permitting decisions should bias toward denial or stringent conditions rather than approval based on uncertain risk characterizations. Environmental justice advocates argue that agencies should use the precautionary principle to help navigate technical uncertainties and subjective decision-making points in conventional environmental assessment[28][52], particularly in communities historically bearing disproportionate pollution burdens.

However, the precautionary principle itself involves value judgments and uncertainties about how much precaution is appropriate. Excessive precaution can prevent beneficial industrial activities and economic development, while insufficient precaution allows harmful exposures to continue. Some scholars and regulators propose alternative frameworks that maintain precaution while reducing unnecessary regulatory burden. One such approach specifies that cumulative impact assessments should be biased toward action by providing basis for preventing new pollution sources and decreasing existing pollution burden[36][49], reflecting the principle that no group should bear disproportionate cumulative impacts. This framing maintains action bias while focusing on reversibility—favoring interventions that reduce rather than allow environmental burdens to continue or increase.

## Practical Implementation Challenges and Current Practice

### Data Gaps and Confidence in Cumulative Assessments

Implementation of cumulative effects frameworks that rigorously propagate and characterize uncertainty requires substantially more data than traditional single-stressor permitting. Quantifying uncertainty bounds around cumulative risk estimates demands knowledge of parameter distributions, correlation structures, and model structure uncertainty—information that often does not exist in readily accessible form. Minnesota's cumulative impacts law explicitly requires that assessments "stress the uncertainties that cannot be addressed through the analysis, including information shortcomings or gaps"[3][25], recognizing that perfect information is unachievable and that decision-making must proceed despite persistent data gaps.

Different categories of stressors pose distinct data challenges. Chemical stressors have relatively well-developed quantitative dose-response models based on toxicology studies, though uncertainties remain regarding low-dose extrapolation and interspecies variation. Nonchemical stressors such as heat, noise, and psychosocial stress have more limited dose-response quantification, particularly for chronic exposures and interactions with chemical stressors[20][48]. Socioeconomic factors including poverty, education, and employment status have even less direct dose-response characterization, instead being incorporated through vulnerability modification factors or as contextual factors in exposure pathways[20]. The sparse quantitative data on nonchemical and socioeconomic stressors makes probability distribution specification difficult, introducing substantial uncertainty into cumulative assessments that include these factors.

The challenge becomes especially acute when cumulative assessments must address both contemporary and historical exposures. A community's cumulative burden reflects not only current pollution sources but also past industrial activity that may have ceased, legacy contamination from previous facilities, and historical land use patterns that created spatially segregated neighborhoods concentrating poor and minority populations in proximity to industrial sources[36][49]. Quantifying historical exposures requires reconstructing past environmental conditions from limited records, introducing substantial uncertainty. Yet failing to account for historical cumulative impacts perpetuates injustice by allowing contemporary permitting decisions to proceed as though previous harms did not occur[36][49].

### Limitations in Existing Screening Tools and Indices

Regulatory agencies and EPA have developed cumulative impact screening tools intended to help identify communities with elevated cumulative burdens and to facilitate cumulative impact assessments[13]. Tools such as **EJScreen** integrate multiple types of stressors and demographic vulnerability factors into composite indices. However, expert panels consulted on cumulative impact assessment development identified significant limitations in current screening tool approaches. Expert consensus holds that scientific evidence for general weighting of social and demographic factors remains very limited and does not support assignment of valid weights to all components of composite vulnerability indices[20]. The aggregation approaches employed in most screening tools lack rigorous scientific justification for the specific weighting schemes used[20].

Moreover, screening tools developed for spatial prioritization (identifying which areas face highest cumulative burdens) may not be appropriate for regulatory decision-making about specific permits. A screening tool might identify a community as facing significant cumulative impacts, justifying enhanced monitoring or management attention. However, using the same screening tool to deny permits or impose stringent conditions on individual facilities requires threshold determinations and legal authorities that extend beyond the tools' original design purposes[13]. The uncertainty propagation effects in these tools remain largely uncharacterized—what happens to overall cumulative impact scores when one varies assumptions about indicator weighting or missing data imputation?

### Integration of Community Knowledge and Lived Experience

Recent guidance emphasizes that cumulative impact assessments should integrate community participation and incorporate residents' lived experiences alongside technical analyses[36][49]. The National Environmental Justice Advisory Council recommends that EPA "validate lived experience and incorporate it into assessments and decision-making"[36]. This recommendation reflects recognition that communities directly experiencing cumulative impacts often have deep knowledge of exposure sources, pathways, and health effects that may not be captured in formal environmental monitoring or epidemiological studies. However, integrating qualitative community knowledge with quantitative technical models creates substantial methodological challenges for uncertainty characterization.

Community-identified stressors sometimes differ from regulatory models' focus. While technical assessments might emphasize PM2.5 or NO2 concentrations as the primary air quality concerns, community members often highlight dust, odors, and noise from nearby facilities as primary concerns affecting quality of life[36]. These physicochemically measurable impacts may not appear in regulatory dose-response models yet substantially affect how cumulative impacts are experienced and remembered. The participatory process through which communities identify and prioritize concerns introduces different sources of uncertainty than technical models—uncertainty about whose voices are heard, how community input is weighted against technical input, and how conflicts between community and technical assessments are resolved.

The integration of community knowledge into permitting decision-making requires developing methods for combining qualitative and quantitative information while maintaining transparency about the value judgments involved. One approach uses **qualitative impact descriptions** to supplement quantified chemical risks, allowing narrative descriptions of non-chemical stressors or unquantifiable impacts to inform overall cumulative assessments[25]. However, how decision-makers weight a narrative description of odor and dust impacts against a quantified carcinogenic risk from formaldehyde exposure remains uncertain and requires explicit deliberation about what evidence types count and how to compare incommensurable risks.

## Recent Developments in Uncertainty Quantification for Environmental Decisions

### Modeling Overshoots and Tipping Thresholds Under Uncertainty

Some of the most sophisticated recent work on uncertainty propagation in environmental assessment addresses tipping thresholds and system bifurcations where small changes in forcing can produce large discontinuous changes in ecosystem state[14]. This work proves relevant to cumulative effects in environmental systems approaching critical transitions where accumulated stressors may trigger irreversible shifts. When systems have potential tipping points, understanding not only where thresholds might exist but also the uncertainty bounds around threshold location becomes critical for permitting decisions.

Research on Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) demonstrates how uncertainties in system parameters (such as the strength of freshwater forcing required to trigger tipping) combine with uncertainties in external forcing trajectories (such as climate change scenarios) to create uncertainty in the "mitigation window"—the period during which a system remains reversible if forcing is reversed[14]. Different parameterizations of the same system model can yield dramatically different conclusions about how far and for how long a system can overshoot a tipping threshold and still return to its original state[14]. This finding suggests that when permit thresholds apply to systems near critical transitions, uncertainty in the underlying system characterization can reverse conclusions about permitting decisions[14].

The implications extend beyond climate systems to any cumulative effects assessment addressing ecosystem stability. If a watershed is approaching capacity for sediment loads, nutrient loading, or chemical contaminants, and uncertainty exists about the location of the critical transition, what permit threshold is appropriate? Conservative thresholds that assume systems are nearer to tipping points protect ecosystem services and resilience but may prevent economically beneficial activities. Optimistic thresholds that assume systems have greater capacity allow development but risk crossing tipping points with unforeseeable consequences. The explicit characterization of uncertainty distributions around critical thresholds makes this trade-off visible for stakeholder deliberation rather than hidden in implicit safety factors.

### Integration of Uncertainty into Permitting Guidance

EPA guidance documents increasingly provide explicit direction on how permitting authorities should handle uncertainty when setting conditions. **Guidance on developing background concentrations for cumulative impact analyses** in air quality permitting recommends a stepwise framework for characterizing representative background concentration that acknowledges uncertainty at each step[38]. The framework requires defining the scope of cumulative impact analysis, identifying relevant and available data, determining representativeness of ambient monitoring data, and determining nearby sources to be explicitly modeled[38]. At each step, decision-makers make judgments about what information to rely on and how to weight data from different sources, creating opportunities for both transparency and hidden assumptions about uncertainty handling.

The EPA's **Quality Assurance Handbook for Air Pollution Measurement** addresses uncertainty in monitoring networks that provide baseline data for permits[37]. The handbook specifies data quality objectives and monitoring strategies designed to ensure that collected data adequately represent the range of concentrations likely to occur, with documented uncertainty bounds[37]. However, even with rigorous quality assurance, monitoring networks have inherent spatial and temporal limitations—no fixed network can capture all conditions that occur across a region, introducing irreducible uncertainty into background concentration estimation.

Guidance on **conducting Monte Carlo analyses in regulatory contexts** emphasizes the importance of sensitivity analysis to identify which input parameters contribute most meaningfully to overall output uncertainty[18][31]. If a cumulative assessment has many input parameters but output uncertainty is driven primarily by a small subset of these parameters, permitting decisions might be rendered more certain through focused efforts to reduce uncertainty in key parameters[18]. Conversely, if output uncertainty is distributed across many parameters, no single data collection effort will substantially reduce overall uncertainty, potentially pointing toward decision-making frameworks that explicitly accept irreducible uncertainty rather than seeking to eliminate it.

## Synthesis: The Fundamental Disconnect Between Cumulative Complexity and Permitting Decisiveness

### The Tension Between Scientific Rigor and Regulatory Decisiveness

A central tension emerges when one recognizes that more rigorous characterization of cumulative effects necessarily reveals greater uncertainty, yet permitting decisions require choosing specific threshold values and making binary approve/deny determinations. Traditional deterministic thresholds provided an illusion of certainty—the threshold was established, the model was run, and results either exceeded or failed to exceed the threshold, seemingly yielding clear decisions. Probabilistic approaches that characterize full distributions of possible outcomes reveal that outcomes often fall in uncertain zones where impacts might or might not exceed thresholds depending on which realizations of uncertain parameters actually occur.

This fundamental tension cannot be fully resolved through technical improvements alone. No additional data or modeling sophistication will yield perfect knowledge of cumulative effects because the future evolution of systems always contains irreducible uncertainty, and the interactions between multiple stressors involve complex nonlinear dynamics that resist prediction. Yet regulatory systems require making definitive permit decisions based on best available science. The solution lies not in seeking perfect certainty but in explicitly characterizing what is known and unknown, propagating uncertainties throughout assessments, and making transparent value judgments about what levels and distributions of residual uncertainty justify specific permit decisions.

The EPA's 2024 guidance and ongoing development of cumulative impact assessment frameworks represent movement toward this transparent uncertainty characterization approach[13][36][49]. Rather than treating uncertainty as a problem to be hidden behind conservative point estimates and safety factors, modern guidance encourages explicit probabilistic characterization that reveals to decision-makers and stakeholders the actual state of knowledge and its implications for different regulatory choices.

### Implications for Threshold Setting Across Cumulative Assessment Phases

The preceding analysis demonstrates that uncertainty propagation affects permit threshold setting at multiple stages of cumulative assessment. At the **hazard identification stage**, uncertainty exists regarding which stressors should be included in cumulative assessment. Should ozone and particulate matter air pollution be assessed together despite their different mechanisms of action? Should chemical stressors be assessed with psychosocial stressors despite limited quantitative dose-response relationships for the latter? These inclusion decisions themselves affect the scope of cumulative assessment and thus the apparent magnitude of cumulative impacts.

At the **exposure assessment stage**, uncertainty in characterizing who is exposed to which stressors through which pathways propagates through to final cumulative impact estimates. When exposure assessment must account for life stages, duration of residence, occupation, behavior patterns, and protective equipment use, the dimensionality of the exposure characterization expands dramatically, and cumulative probability distributions of exposure become increasingly uncertain.

At the **dose-response assessment stage**, uncertainty in characterizing how risk changes with dose for individual stressors combines with uncertainty about interaction effects to create highly uncertain cumulative risk characterizations. The choice between additive and multiplicative risk models can change apparent cumulative risk by orders of magnitude, yet this choice involves assumptions about biological mechanisms that may not be well characterized for the stressor combinations of interest.

At the **risk characterization and threshold determination stage**, uncertainty about acceptable probability levels of impact exceedance introduces value judgments into what are presented as technical determinations. Should permits be set such that cumulative impacts exceed thresholds in 5% of plausible scenarios? 10%? 1%? The choice reflects policy preferences about acceptable risk that go beyond what science can determine.

### Framework for Transparent Threshold-Setting Under Uncertainty

A defensible approach to permit threshold setting in cumulative effects assessments should incorporate the following elements. First, **explicit uncertainty characterization** throughout the assessment chain, documenting both uncertainty (reducible through additional information) and variability (irreducible features of systems and populations). Second, **probabilistic propagation** of uncertainties from input parameters through model structures to final cumulative impact estimates, avoiding masking of uncertainties behind point estimates. Third, **sensitivity analysis** identifying which parameters contribute most meaningfully to output uncertainty, guiding data collection efforts toward parameters that would most effectively reduce key uncertainties if characterized more precisely.

Fourth, **transparent documentation** of assumptions, including distributional assumptions, interaction models, and aggregation approaches, with clear communication of how sensitive conclusions are to alternative assumptions. Fifth, **explicit value-based threshold selection** that acknowledges whether permitting decisions accept 5%, 10%, or other probability levels of exceeding impact thresholds, with explanation of the rationale for these choices. Sixth, **community engagement** in threshold determination that validates whether technical risk characterizations align with community understanding of cumulative exposures and concerns.

Seventh, **adaptive management approaches** that establish permit conditions as provisional rather than final, with monitoring to detect when actual impacts approach predicted levels and procedures for adjusting permits if observed impacts exceed anticipated levels. Such adaptive approaches acknowledge that uncertainty in cumulative assessments cannot be entirely eliminated through pre-decision analysis and require post-decision monitoring to ensure protective outcomes.

## Conclusion: Advancing Cumulative Effects Assessment and Permitting Practice

Environmental permitting in the face of cumulative effects and their associated uncertainties requires moving beyond traditional deterministic threshold approaches toward frameworks that explicitly characterize and propagate uncertainty while maintaining regulatory decisiveness. The technical tools now exist to conduct probabilistic cumulative assessments that rigorously characterize how uncertainties combine across multiple stressors and exposure pathways. Point estimate methods, Monte Carlo approaches, Bayesian networks, and other advanced techniques allow sophisticated uncertainty quantification without prohibitive computational burden. The challenge lies not in technical capability but in organizational and institutional readiness to adopt more complex decision-making frameworks and to transparently communicate the implications of uncertainty for permitting outcomes.

Recent EPA guidance developments and state regulatory innovations, particularly Minnesota's cumulative impacts law and frameworks for environmental justice, signal growing recognition that traditional permitting approaches inadequately protect communities facing multiple simultaneous stressors[13][25][36][49]. However, translating this recognition into changed permitting practice requires overcoming several barriers. Permitting authorities require training in probabilistic risk assessment concepts and access to computational tools for conducting cumulative assessments. Legal frameworks must evolve to accommodate threshold determinations based on probability distributions rather than point estimates. Communities must have genuine opportunities to participate in defining cumulative impacts and threshold values rather than rubber-stamping technical determinations made behind closed analytical doors.

The research and guidance synthesis presented in this report demonstrates that uncertainty propagation in cumulative effect frameworks fundamentally affects what permit thresholds are defensible, how stringent they must be to provide intended protection, and how confident decision-makers and stakeholders can be that approved permits will actually result in acceptable cumulative impacts. Failure to explicitly characterize and propagate these uncertainties creates risk that permits based on incomplete understanding will allow unintended cumulative harms, particularly to vulnerable communities disproportionately exposed to multiple stressors. By contrast, explicit uncertainty characterization enables transparent deliberation about acceptable risk and ensures that permit conditions reflect genuine scientific understanding rather than hidden conservative assumptions disguised as objective technical determinations.

The transition to rigorous cumulative effects assessment with transparent uncertainty characterization will not occur automatically but requires sustained commitment to improving environmental decision-making. EPA should continue developing and refining guidance on cumulative impact assessment methodologies, with particular attention to how nonchemical and socioeconomic stressors can be incorporated without relying on unjustified weighting schemes. States should adopt cumulative impact permitting frameworks comparable to Minnesota's, requiring explicit consideration of combined exposures and cumulative burdens in environmental justice areas. Permitting authorities should invest in training on probabilistic methods and build relationships with communities to integrate local knowledge with technical analysis. Communities should demand transparency regarding uncertainties and have meaningful opportunities to influence threshold determinations rather than merely commenting on completed technical assessments.

Through these sustained efforts to advance cumulative effects assessment practice, environmental permitting can evolve from a system that treats stressors in isolation and masks uncertainties behind point estimates toward a system that honestly characterizes what is known and unknown about combined environmental exposures and makes transparent decisions about acceptable risk distributions. This evolution will not eliminate the challenging value judgments inherent in permitting decisions, but it will ensure that these judgments are made transparently with full understanding of cumulative scientific uncertainties and their implications for environmental justice and public health protection.
