# The Influence of Local Chemical Ordering on Dislocation-Twin Boundary Interactions During Plastic Deformation in High-Entropy Alloys

High-entropy alloys represent a paradigm shift in materials science, exhibiting exceptional mechanical properties that arise fundamentally from their multicomponent nature and the complex interplay of their constituent elements at the atomic scale. During plastic deformation, the behavior of these materials is critically governed by the motion of dislocations and the formation of deformation twins, both of which are profoundly influenced by the local arrangement of atoms within their crystalline matrices. This comprehensive examination reveals that local chemical ordering in high-entropy alloys fundamentally transforms the energy landscape that dislocations encounter, thereby altering both the propensity for twin formation and the mechanisms through which dislocations interact with twin boundaries. The discovery that chemical ordering is not random but exhibits short-range and long-range ordering phenomena has revolutionized our understanding of deformation mechanisms in these materials, showing that yield strengths can increase by a factor of four while simultaneously maintaining or improving ductility, a remarkable achievement that contradicts the traditional strength-ductility trade-off observed in conventional alloys.[2][43]

## The Nature and Origins of Local Chemical Ordering in High-Entropy Alloys

### Fundamentals of Chemical Ordering in Multicomponent Systems

High-entropy alloys were originally conceived as random solid solutions in which atoms of different elements occupy lattice sites in a statistically random fashion, with the high configurational entropy of mixing providing thermodynamic stability at elevated temperatures.[2] However, substantial experimental and computational evidence has emerged demonstrating that this conception significantly underestimates the complexity of atomic arrangements within these materials. Rather than forming ideally random arrangements, high-entropy alloys exhibit local chemical ordering phenomena characterized by non-random distributions of elemental constituents at various length scales.[11][31] This ordering manifests in two principal forms: short-range ordering, in which atomic arrangements deviate from randomness over distances on the order of a few angstroms involving nearest and next-nearest neighbor shells, and long-range ordering, in which extended nanometer-scale domains develop with preferential arrangements of specific elements.[2] The thermodynamic driving force for such ordering arises fundamentally from the binary enthalpies of mixing between the constituent elements. When certain pairs of elements exhibit large negative enthalpies of mixing, they preferentially associate with one another rather than randomly mixing, creating local compositional fluctuations that propagate throughout the material.[2][11]

The presence of local chemical ordering can be understood through the lens of statistical mechanics and thermodynamic modeling based on binary interaction parameters. A thermodynamic framework utilizing binary enthalpies of mixing between element pairs has proven remarkably successful in predicting which high-entropy alloy systems will exhibit ordering and the nature of that ordering.[2] The framework demonstrates that by selecting alloying elements with appropriately chosen negative enthalpies of mixing relative to the base composition, one can introduce controlled chemical ordering into nominally random solid solutions. For instance, in the equiatomic CoFeNi alloy system, additions of aluminum with its large negative enthalpy of mixing with cobalt, iron, and nickel can systematically introduce short-range ordering without precipitating secondary phases, thereby creating an evolution from random solid solution behavior through increasingly ordered states.[2][43] This discovery opened an entirely new design paradigm for high-entropy alloys, shifting from purely compositional considerations to explicitly controlling ordering phenomena to tune mechanical properties.

### Detection and Characterization of Local Ordering States

The confirmation of local chemical ordering in high-entropy alloys required sophisticated experimental and computational methodologies. Experimental techniques including X-ray diffraction with detailed pair distribution function analysis, atom probe tomography, and transmission electron microscopy have provided direct evidence of short-range and long-range ordering in systems such as CoCrNi, FeMnCoCrNi (the canonical Cantor alloy), CrCoNi medium-entropy alloys, and refractory systems like MoNbTaW.[2][11] X-ray diffraction measurements revealed diffuse scattering patterns characteristic of short-range order, with careful analysis of superlattice peaks confirming the existence of long-range ordered domains in annealed samples.[2] Advanced computational approaches utilizing Monte Carlo simulations parameterized by accurate density functional theory calculations have elucidated the energetic preferences driving ordering, demonstrating how local atomic arrangements evolve with thermal history and composition.[2][7] Molecular dynamics simulations have provided atomic-scale visualizations of dislocation core structures and their interactions with locally ordered regions, revealing how the atomic arrangements modify the energy landscapes that dislocations must traverse.[7][31]

Studies of Warren-Cowley parameters, which quantify the propensity for unlike atom pairing relative to random arrangements, have demonstrated measurable deviations from randomness in numerous high-entropy alloys even when no distinct superlattice reflections are evident.[11][19] In the CrCoNi alloy, for example, the nearest-neighbor shell exhibits Warren-Cowley parameters indicating that Cr atoms preferentially avoid nearest-neighbor Cr pairs, clustering instead with Co and Ni atoms.[11] This short-range ordering tendency emerges from the fundamental electronic structure differences between the elements, with elements possessing different magnetic properties or electronic configurations showing enhanced ordering propensity.[2][11] The extent of ordering can be controlled through thermal processing, with lower-temperature annealing promoting stronger short-range ordering while higher-temperature treatments establish equilibrium with reduced ordering.[2][43]

## Dislocation Behavior Modifications Induced by Local Chemical Ordering

### Energy Landscape and Dislocation Core Structure

The presence of local chemical ordering fundamentally transforms the energy landscape that mobile dislocations encounter during plastic deformation. In idealized random solid solutions, dislocations experience an approximately uniform resistance arising from the statistical distribution of random solute atoms, described within classical solid-solution strengthening frameworks.[1][14] In contrast, the ordered regions create spatially heterogeneous variations in local atomic environments that dramatically alter dislocation energetics. Computational investigations employing density functional theory have directly calculated dislocation core structures across hundreds of unique local environments within ordered refractory high-entropy alloys, revealing that the distribution of dislocation core energies becomes significantly narrower as chemical short-range ordering develops.[13][25] Remarkably, while the averaged dislocation core energy remains relatively unchanged regardless of ordering state, the variance of core energies across different local environments decreases substantially with increasing short-range ordering.[13][25] This narrowing of the energy distribution occurs because short-range ordering creates more uniform local atomic configurations, reducing the extremes of hard and soft atomic environments that dislocations encounter.[13]

The dislocation core structures themselves remain fundamentally compact in body-centered cubic high-entropy alloys even in the presence of significant short-range ordering, as demonstrated through extensive molecular dynamics simulations.[13] However, the local stress and strain fields surrounding these cores show pronounced spatial variations depending on the arrangement of neighboring atoms within the ordered regions.[4][14] Atoms that are larger than the average exert tensile stresses on the surrounding lattice while smaller atoms create compressive stress zones, and these residual stress fields accumulate and interact to create an irregular energy landscape.[14][17] When local ordering occurs, these stress fields become more organized spatially, with ordered regions exhibiting more coherent stress distributions compared to random arrangements where stresses fluctuate unpredictably.[13][25]

### Dislocation Mobility and Cross-slip Behavior

The mobility of dislocations in high-entropy alloys with local chemical ordering exhibits striking differences compared to purely random solid solutions, particularly for screw dislocations that can undergo cross-slip between different slip planes.[7][8] In refractory high-entropy alloys such as MoNbTaW, molecular dynamics simulations based on machine-learning interatomic potentials reveal that screw dislocation mobility displays complex temperature and stress dependencies that are dramatically influenced by the presence or absence of short-range ordering.[7] Specifically, when dislocations glide through regions of short-range order, they must create diffuse antiphase boundaries along their glide plane, which represents a local disruption of the favorable nearest-neighbor preferences established by the chemical ordering.[7][10] This creation of antiphase boundary structures requires additional energy and generates an extra resistance to dislocation motion, acting as a Peierls-type barrier that impedes glide.[7]

The mechanism of dislocation pinning by ordered regions can be understood through the concept of hard atomic motifs—specific local atomic configurations with exceptionally high resistance to dislocation passage.[31][32] In medium-entropy alloys such as NiCoCr with chemically ordered regions, molecular dynamics simulations demonstrate that dislocations do not move smoothly but rather execute jerky, intermittent motion characterized by periods of pinning followed by rapid bursts of movement as the applied stress overcomes the pinning threshold.[31] The dislocation line becomes highly wavy as it navigates the spatially varying energy landscape, with segments becoming trapped at sites of high local fault energy while other segments advance more freely.[31] This wavy dislocation configuration emerges as a direct consequence of the spatial fluctuations in ordering-induced stress fields and barrier heights, creating a landscape with peaks and valleys that the dislocation line must conform to.[31][32]

Cross-slip of screw dislocations, a critical mechanism for dislocation escape from entanglement and forest hardening, is significantly promoted by local chemical ordering.[7][8] The intrinsic and fluctuating lattice distortion created by ordered regions with their associated stress fields facilitates frequent cross-slip events along the dislocation line, forming numerous kinks and jogs that segment the dislocation into smaller, more easily movable components.[7][32] In some extreme cases, such as oxygen-doped refractory high-entropy alloys where interstitial oxygen atoms preferentially associate with certain metals to form strong ordered complexes, double cross-slip becomes prevalent, creating wavy dislocation slip patterns instead of the coplanar arrays typical of conventional metals.[32] This fundamental change in dislocation morphology and movement mode has profound implications for strain hardening behavior and the overall mechanical response of the material.

## Twin Boundary Energy Modifications and Deformation Twinning Propensity

### Stacking Fault Energy and Its Local Variations

The propensity for deformation twinning in face-centered cubic high-entropy alloys depends critically on the stacking fault energy, the energy cost of creating planar stacking faults on close-packed planes that represent the first step in twin nucleation.[5][11][52] Direct measurements and first-principles calculations have demonstrated that stacking fault energy varies substantially with local chemical composition and ordering state, with values ranging from highly negative to substantially positive depending on system composition.[5][11] The transformative discovery that local chemical ordering modifies stacking fault energy through changes in local atomic arrangements has revolutionized the design of high-entropy alloys with controlled deformation twinning propensity.[11] When atoms within a region exhibit preferential nearest-neighbor preferences through short-range ordering, the energy cost of disrupting those preferred arrangements by creating a stacking fault increases.[11] Conversely, long-range ordering structures with ordered domains can create heterogeneous distributions of stacking fault energy, with certain planes exhibiting significantly lower stacking fault energy than others.[8][31][43]

The relationship between chemical ordering state and stacking fault energy has been quantitatively characterized through extensive first-principles calculations incorporating density functional theory.[11][2] Results demonstrate a continuous evolution of stacking fault energy with increasing ordering: in the CrCoNi alloy system, stacking fault energy values can vary from approximately negative values at high disorder to positive values exceeding sixty millijoules per square meter at high ordering states.[11] This variation arises because stacking faults represent planar defects that disrupt the local chemical order, requiring the creation and maintenance of unfavorable nearest-neighbor environments on the fault plane and immediately adjacent planes.[11] The energy penalty for this disruption directly reflects the strength of the local ordering tendency of the system, with strongly ordered systems exhibiting higher stacking fault energies than weakly ordered or random systems.[11]

### Twin Nucleation Barriers and Formation Mechanisms

The nucleation of deformation twins in face-centered cubic structures proceeds through the progressive passage of partial dislocations on successive close-packed planes, creating stacking faults that accumulate to form a planar twin.[37][50] The energy barrier for nucleating the first layer of a potential twin involves overcoming the unstable stacking fault energy and creating new twin boundary area, both of which are profoundly affected by local chemical ordering.[37][50] In systems with pronounced local chemical ordering, the distribution of nucleation barriers becomes dramatically broadened compared to homogeneous reference systems, with some regions exhibiting significantly lower barriers while others present substantially higher barriers to twin nucleation.[37][42] This heterogeneity in nucleation barriers arises from spatial fluctuations in local stacking fault energies: regions where atoms are arranged such that stacking faults carry lower energy penalties become nucleation sites where twinning initiates readily, while regions with higher local stacking fault energies present substantial barriers to twinning.[37][42]

The discovery that local chemical ordering promotes nanoscale deformation twin formation through broadening and heterogenizing nucleation barriers represents a fundamental insight into high-entropy alloy plasticity.[37] Rather than suppressing twinning through a reduction in average stacking fault energy, local ordering creates a distribution of barrier heights such that a substantial fraction of potential nucleation sites present barriers lower than the critical nucleation threshold at applied stresses well below those required for twin nucleation in homogeneous systems.[37] This mechanism explains the remarkable observation that face-centered cubic high-entropy alloys such as CrCoNi medium-entropy alloy form far higher densities of nanoscale deformation twins than conventional face-centered cubic metals with nominally similar average stacking fault energies.[37][42] The nanoscale dimensions of these twins, with thicknesses on the order of ten to fifty nanometers, arise directly from the spatial length scale of ordering-induced heterogeneity in nucleation barriers: as stresses increase, new twins nucleate at nearby sites with slightly higher barriers rather than existing twins thickening through continuous migration of twin boundaries, creating dispersed nanoscale twin arrays.[37]

## Interaction Mechanisms Between Dislocations and Twin Boundaries

### Transmission and Absorption Processes Modified by Ordering

When dislocations encounter twin boundaries during plastic deformation, their behavior depends critically on both the character of the dislocation (edge, screw, or mixed) and the local atomic structure of the twin boundary itself.[57] In conventional face-centered cubic metals, well-established mechanisms predict that dislocations either transmit through twin boundaries to continue gliding in the adjacent twin region or become absorbed and incorporated into the twin boundary structure through specific reaction sequences.[47][57] However, the presence of local chemical ordering fundamentally modifies these interaction mechanisms by altering the energetics of both transmission and absorption processes. The diffuse antiphase boundaries that form when dislocations glide through chemically ordered regions create additional energy barriers beyond those present in purely structural considerations.[32][35] When a dislocation approaches a twin boundary in an ordered region, the interplay between dislocation-induced disruption of local order and the energetics of twin boundary formation creates a complex landscape that can favor either absorption or transmission depending on subtle details of local ordering strength and spatial extent.[57]

Molecular dynamics simulations examining dislocation-twin boundary interactions in face-centered cubic metals reveal that even subtle variations in twin boundary atomic structure, on the order of one to two percent changes in nearest-neighbor distances between metastable and minimum-energy configurations, can completely reverse the outcome of dislocation interaction, switching from transmission to absorption mechanisms.[57] Such sensitivity to atomic-scale structure demonstrates that local chemical ordering, which creates larger variations in interatomic spacings through preferential association of certain element pairs, must profoundly affect transmission probabilities and absorption efficiencies. In high-entropy alloys with pronounced local ordering, the partial dislocations that comprise extended dislocation cores interact differently with twin boundaries than in random alloys because the chemical environment on one side versus the other side of the twin boundary differs, sometimes dramatically, depending on local ordering configurations.[31][32] This compositional asymmetry can break the symmetry that would otherwise make transmission and absorption equally probable, instead preferentially favoring one mechanism over another depending on which side of the twin boundary exhibits favorable ordering configurations.

### Back-Stresses and Dislocation Pinning at Boundaries

The creation of antiphase boundaries and diffuse antiphase boundary structures by dislocation motion through chemically ordered regions generates substantial back-stresses that impede further dislocation motion and strengthen the material through a mechanism distinct from traditional grain boundary strengthening.[35][43] When dislocations pass through twin boundaries in the presence of local ordering, the destruction and reformation of favorable nearest-neighbor configurations through the complex reaction processes at the twin boundary create particularly strong back-stresses that can pin dislocations or significantly impede their transmission.[35] In medium-entropy alloys such as CrCoNi where local ordering is pronounced, the passage of the leading partial dislocation creates a stacking fault while the trailing partial must follow to restore the lattice, but in the presence of ordering, this trailing partial motion is severely hindered by the necessity to restore favorable nearest-neighbor configurations while simultaneously passing through a twin boundary region.[43] The resulting dislocation configurations show pronounced pinning at twin boundaries, with dislocations accumulating in high-density arrays on one side of the boundary while transmission becomes inhibited.[43]

The interaction between dislocation pile-ups and twin boundaries in ordered high-entropy alloys exhibits fundamentally different stress concentration behavior compared to random solid solutions. In random alloys, pile-ups generate stress concentrations that eventually force dislocation transmission or source activation on the opposite side of the boundary according to relatively straightforward stress balance considerations.[47] However, in ordered systems where antiphase boundary formation accompanies dislocation motion, additional contributions to stress arise from the energetic cost of disrupting chemical order, creating more complex and potentially more severe stress concentrations that can facilitate crack nucleation if sufficient ordering strength exists.[35][43] These stress concentrations drive transitions in deformation mechanisms, potentially activating competing deformation modes such as dislocation emission from the boundary, dislocation source activation, or in some circumstances, transformation-induced plasticity if the alloy exhibits metastable phases.

## Deformation Mechanisms and Mechanical Properties Influenced by Local Ordering

### The Hierarchy of Deformation Mechanisms

During plastic deformation of high-entropy alloys containing local chemical ordering, multiple deformation mechanisms typically operate sequentially or in parallel, with the operative mechanisms depending on applied stress, temperature, strain history, and the strength of local ordering.[5][8][35][43] The remarkable achievement of simultaneously increasing yield strength by factors exceeding four while improving ductility stems from sophisticated interplay of these deformation mechanisms controlled by local ordering.[2][43] At low strains, when stresses are insufficient to overcome high barriers to twin nucleation or full dislocation glide, planar slip from partial dislocations nucleated from grain boundaries represents the primary deformation mode, generating stacking faults within the material.[43] As stresses increase slightly above this threshold, the spatially heterogeneous nucleation barriers created by local ordering allow widespread twin nucleation to occur throughout the material, generating high densities of nanometer-scale deformation twins that provide substantial strengthening through their geometrical effects and the creation of new interfaces.[37][43]

With further straining into moderate deformation regimes, the initial nanoscale twins begin to interact with one another and with mobile dislocations, leading to complex phenomena including twin thickening through merging, lateral propagation along slip planes, and eventual interactions with grain boundaries or other structural features.[5][37][43] In some high-entropy alloys with controlled local ordering, transformation-induced plasticity mechanisms can be activated where specific regions undergo displacive phase transformations from face-centered cubic to hexagonal close-packed or body-centered cubic structures under applied stress, absorbing significant strain energy in the transformation process while simultaneously generating new interfaces and refining microstructure.[5][8][21][48] At high strains, the accumulated dislocation density and interaction-hardened microstructure containing twins, transformed phase, and complex dislocation patterns contribute to enhanced strain hardening that delays necking and failure, thereby improving ductility.[5][8][43]

The critical distinction between high-entropy alloys with pronounced local ordering versus random solid solution high-entropy alloys lies in the continuity and uniformity of the deformation mechanisms. In random alloys, the transition between deformation mechanisms tends to be relatively sharp and localized, occurring at relatively high strains or stresses as uniform dislocation accumulation eventually triggers twinning or transformation.[8][31] In contrast, ordered systems activate multiple mechanisms across a wide strain range due to spatial heterogeneity in activation barriers, creating a distributed, more continuous evolution of microstructure that better accommodates strain without developing severe localization.[5][43] This distributed mechanism activation, directly enabled by local ordering heterogeneity, represents the fundamental microstructural basis for breaking the traditional strength-ductility paradox observed in these materials.

### Work-Hardening Rates and Strain-Hardening Mechanisms

The evolution of work-hardening rate with increasing plastic strain reveals striking differences between high-entropy alloys with varying degrees of local chemical ordering.[43] In random solid solution high-entropy alloys, work-hardening rates typically decrease monotonically with increasing strain, following the classical behavior observed in single-phase face-centered cubic alloys, with work hardening rates declining from initial values of several hundred megapascals per unit strain to near-zero values at high strains as dislocation multiplication saturates and dynamic recovery begins to dominate.[43] In contrast, high-entropy alloys with controlled local ordering exhibit markedly different work-hardening behavior characterized by multiple stages with intermediate plateaus or even local increases in hardening rate as new deformation mechanisms sequentially activate.[43] These multiple work-hardening stages correspond to initiation of planar slip, widespread twin nucleation, twin interaction and thickening, transformation-induced plasticity activation, and dynamic refinement of the microstructure through mechanisms including dynamic Hall-Petch strengthening from continuously refined twin structures and fine-scale deformation-induced phase transformations.[5][43]

The strain-hardening mechanisms operating in ordered systems can be understood through decomposition of the flow stress into constituent contributions from various strengthening sources.[5] Strengthening from dislocation-dislocation interactions represents the primary contributor in early deformation stages, with strengthening arising from the back-stresses generated by heterogeneous dislocation distributions and dislocation pile-ups characteristic of initial planar slip on limited slip systems.[5][43] As twin nucleation becomes widespread and proceeding, twin-boundary hardening emerges as a major strengthening contribution, resulting from the geometric confinement of slip to regions between twin boundaries, effectively implementing a dynamic Hall-Petch strengthening mechanism where the characteristic length scale continuously decreases as twin density increases.[5][43] The creation of antiphase boundaries through dislocation passage in ordered regions contributes additional strengthening beyond conventional solid-solution strengthening, with contributions quantifiable through consideration of the local fluctuations in stacking fault energy rather than the averaged value traditionally employed.[31][43] At high strains, interactions between twinning and transformation-induced plasticity mechanisms generate exceptionally high strengthening rates, with the generation of fine-scale ε-martensite laths or δ-transformed regions creating additional interfaces and refining the deformation structure to subnanometer scales in some regions.[5]

## Specific Examples and Material Systems

### Transformation-Enabled High-Entropy Alloys with Controlled Ordering

A particularly illuminating example of how local chemical ordering controls deformation mechanisms appears in face-centered cubic high-entropy alloys derived from FeMnCoCr bases with compositions tailored to exhibit metastable austenite that can undergo stress-induced transformation to hexagonal close-packed martensite during deformation.[5][21][48] The introduction of interstitial nitrogen atoms in carefully controlled quantities creates strong local chemical ordering through preferential association of nitrogen with specific metal atoms, forming (N, Zr, Ti)-enriched or (N, Mo)-enriched complexes depending on system composition.[5][8] These ordered nitrogen-metal atom complexes exhibit strong short-range ordering with high negative enthalpies of mixing, creating nanometer-scale clusters separated by distances on the order of one to ten nanometers depending on nitrogen concentration.[5][8] The presence of these ordered clusters fundamentally modifies the stacking fault energy landscape such that certain atomic planes exhibit stacking fault energies below the threshold required to stabilize the face-centered cubic phase, enabling stress-induced transformation to proceed under applied mechanical stress.[5][8]

In the specific case of Fe₅₀Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀ with controlled nitrogen doping to approximately one atomic percent, the resulting alloy exhibits extraordinary mechanical properties: yield strengths approaching or exceeding one thousand two hundred megapascals combine with uniform elongations exceeding forty percent even when tested at cryogenic temperatures of seventy-seven Kelvin.[5] The underlying deformation mechanism involves cooperative activation of transformation-induced plasticity and twinning-induced plasticity effects, with the initial stages of deformation proceeding through stress-induced formation of hexagonal close-packed ε-martensite along specific planes and directions within the disordered face-centered cubic matrix while simultaneously remaining within ordered regions.[5] The ordered regions, characterized by locally elevated stacking fault energies compared to the disordered matrix, act as stronger phases that constrain the deformation to specific configurations and provide back-stresses that inhibit runaway transformation while allowing more distributed plastic flow.[5][8] As deformation proceeds, the combination of transformation, twinning, and dislocation plasticity creates a hierarchical microstructure with features spanning multiple length scales from the subnanometer scale of antiphase boundaries through nanometer-scale twin lamellae and martensite laths to micrometer-scale regions of accumulated deformation, with each scale contributing to sustained strain hardening and preventing early localization failure.[5]

### Refractory High-Entropy Alloys with Short-Range Ordering Effects

Refractory high-entropy alloys designed for elevated-temperature applications such as MoNbTaW exhibit remarkably pronounced short-range ordering tendencies despite their body-centered cubic crystal structure, with the ordering arising from the large electronic structure differences between the constituent refractory metals and their widely different atomic radii.[7][13][25] The presence of short-range order in these systems has profound effects on dislocation mobility particularly relevant for high-temperature strength maintenance, as the thermal activation required to overcome dislocation barriers changes substantially when local ordering exists.[7] Molecular dynamics simulations and first-principles calculations have demonstrated that short-range order in MoNbTaW primarily involves Mo-Ta preferential pairing, followed by Ta-W interactions, while Mo-Nb pairing remains relatively unpreferred despite both elements being refractory metals.[13] This preference arises from the complex interplay of electronic band structure effects, magnetic interactions, and lattice mismatch considerations.[13]

The influence of short-range order on screw dislocation mobility in these refractory systems manifests as temperature-dependent changes in mobility that deviate significantly from predictions based on random solid solution theory.[7] At elevated temperatures where short-range order partially persists, screw dislocations experience pinning at certain configurations where diffuse antiphase boundary formation is energetically expensive, leading to lower mobility and higher strength maintenance compared to random reference states.[7] The energetic barriers for kink-pair nucleation that governs dislocation glide at high temperatures increase with short-range order presence, creating additional temperature dependence to strength that explains the exceptional high-temperature strength often observed in refractory high-entropy alloys with pronounced ordering tendencies.[7] Notably, the effects vary between edge and screw dislocations, with screw dislocation mobility showing greater sensitivity to short-range ordering because screw dislocations must traverse the local structural asymmetries created by ordering without easily escaping through cross-slip, whereas edge dislocations can often bypass such obstacles through wavy configurations and climb-enabled mechanisms at elevated temperatures.[7]

### Medium-Entropy Alloys Demonstrating Ordering Effects on Twinning

The CrCoNi medium-entropy alloy represents a particularly well-studied system for understanding local ordering effects on deformation twinning, as it exhibits controlled short-range ordering with Cr atoms preferentially bonding with Co and Ni rather than other Cr atoms, leading to nanometer-scale compositional heterogeneity despite absence of distinct secondary phases.[37][42][45] When subjected to tensile deformation, this alloy generates extraordinarily high densities of nanoscale deformation twins with thicknesses typically ranging from ten to fifty nanometers, densities that exceed those observed in conventional face-centered cubic metals with similar or even lower average stacking fault energies.[37][42] Transmission electron microscopy observations combined with molecular dynamics simulations have revealed that these nanoscale twins nucleate continuously throughout the deformed volume rather than thickening existing twins through progressive twin boundary migration as observed in conventional metals.[37]

The mechanism underlying this unique twinning behavior stems directly from the spatial heterogeneity of local stacking fault energies created by local ordering: while average stacking fault energy in CrCoNi remains negative or weakly positive, the distributions of local stacking fault energies at different positions within the material show substantial variance, with certain planes exhibiting stacking fault energies significantly lower than the average while other planes exhibit correspondingly higher values.[31][37] This distribution of barriers creates a landscape where twin nucleation becomes favorable at many spatially distributed sites where local stacking fault energies happen to be low, while other sites present substantial barriers to nucleation.[37] As applied stress increases incrementally, the nucleation barrier at progressively higher-energy sites becomes accessible, enabling continuous emergence of new twin nuclei throughout the deforming material.[37] This continuous nucleation mechanism explains both the exceptionally high twin density and the small average twin thickness, as twins initiate at neighboring sites before existing twins can grow substantially, creating a self-limiting mechanism that prevents formation of thick twins.[37]

The twin-twin interactions and dislocation-twin boundary interactions in CrCoNi show pronounced effects from local ordering through the generation of substantial dislocation accumulation at twin boundaries.[37][43] When trailing partial dislocations necessary to complete the twin thickening process must traverse twin boundaries while simultaneously disrupting local ordering, the energy barriers increase sufficiently that dislocation pinning and pile-up formation become prevalent.[43] These dislocation accumulations at twin boundaries create high stress concentrations that facilitate further twin nucleation at neighboring sites rather than twin thickening, propagating the continuous twin nucleation mechanism throughout the deformation process.[37][43]

## Implications for Materials Design and Property Optimization

### Strategic Design Approaches Based on Ordering Considerations

The discovery that local chemical ordering can be controlled through thermodynamic selection of alloying elements and processing conditions has enabled a new design paradigm for high-entropy alloys optimized for target applications.[2][43] Rather than focusing solely on average properties derived from nominal composition, designers can now explicitly engineer the ordering state to tune deformation mechanisms and mechanical properties over wide ranges.[2] The simple but powerful approach utilizing binary enthalpies of mixing to predict ordering propensity has proven remarkably effective: alloy design calculations based exclusively on binary phase diagram data and binary interaction parameters successfully predict ordering tendency in approximately ninety percent of examined high-entropy alloy systems.[2] This predictability enables rational exploration of the immense compositional space of high-entropy alloys to identify systems with tailored ordering characteristics suited to specific applications.[2]

For applications requiring exceptional strength combined with adequate ductility, intentional introduction of controlled local ordering through strategic alloying element selection enhances mechanical properties substantially.[2][43] The prototypical approach involves introducing alloying elements with large negative enthalpies of mixing relative to the base composition—such as aluminum additions to equiatomic CoFeNi—which preferentially associate to form ordered clusters that modify the deformation landscape without precipitating secondary phases.[2][43] This approach has demonstrated success in both face-centered cubic systems where it enhances twinning-driven plasticity and in body-centered cubic refractory systems where it modifies dislocation mobility and high-temperature strength.[2][43] For cryogenic applications where transformation-induced plasticity becomes relevant, the local ordering approach enables fine-tuning of phase stability to create metastable states where stress-induced phase transformation can proceed in controlled, distributed fashion rather than abruptly and catastrophically, thereby improving both strength and ductility simultaneously.[5][8]

### Processing Routes for Ordering Control

The state of local chemical ordering in high-entropy alloys remains controllable through thermal processing because ordering represents a thermodynamic equilibrium state that adjusts with temperature changes.[2][43] In comparison to conventional ordered intermetallics where long-range ordering must be preserved by controlled cooling to avoid disordering through recrystallization or grain boundary migration, high-entropy alloys with short-range and incipient long-range ordering prove more robust because the disorder inherent in the random composition naturally retards ordering kinetics while maintaining some local preference for favorable atomic configurations.[2] Systematic annealing studies have demonstrated that high-entropy alloys can exhibit gradual increases in ordering parameter with extended heating times at intermediate temperatures, allowing controlled adjustment of ordering strength to optimize mechanical properties for specific applications.[2][43] For instance, extended annealing of AlₓCoFeNi alloys at intermediate temperatures progressively increases both yield strength and ductility as ordering gradually develops, with the evolution arising from the competing effects of static recovery (reducing dislocation density and decreasing strength) and ordering strengthening (increasing barriers and enhancing deformation mechanism control).[2][43]

The relationship between heating temperature, time at temperature, and resulting ordering state follows expected thermodynamic behavior with lower temperatures favoring more extensive ordering and longer times enabling complete equilibration through diffusion-limited ordering processes.[2][43] For refractory high-entropy alloys like MoNbTaW where elemental diffusion rates remain extremely low even at elevated temperatures, the ordering state becomes essentially kinetically frozen below approximately half the absolute melting temperature, with subsequent cooling preserving the high-temperature ordering structure rather than allowing further ordering development.[7] This kinetic freezing allows creation of metastable ordering states quenched from specific temperatures, enabling exploration of how various degrees of ordering influence properties even though the kinetically frozen state does not represent the thermodynamic equilibrium at lower temperatures.[7]

## Conclusion: Synthesis and Future Directions

The investigation of how local chemical ordering in high-entropy alloys influences dislocation-twin boundary interactions during plastic deformation reveals a sophisticated interplay of mechanisms that dramatically expands the property space accessible to these materials compared to conventional alloys. Local chemical ordering fundamentally transforms the energy landscape that dislocations encounter, creating spatial heterogeneity in both barrier heights and local stacking fault energies that enables simultaneous activation of multiple strengthening mechanisms across wide strain ranges, thereby circumventing the traditional strength-ductility trade-off that has constrained conventional alloy development for decades. The discovery that yield strengths can increase by factors exceeding four while maintaining or improving ductility represents a paradigm shift in materials design, demonstrating that chemical engineering at the atomic scale can produce mechanical properties exceeding those of conventional single-phase engineering alloys despite the added complexity introduced by multicomponent compositions.[2][43]

The interplay between local ordering and twin formation involves multiple reinforcing mechanisms: the heterogeneity in local stacking fault energies created by ordering enables widespread twin nucleation at lower stresses than would occur in random systems, while the disruption of local ordering by dislocation passage generates antiphase boundaries and back-stresses that inhibit uncontrolled twin growth, creating a self-regulating deformation mechanism that produces high densities of nanoscale twins providing exceptional strain hardening.[37][42][43] The interactions between dislocations and twin boundaries become profoundly modified by the necessity to restore disrupted local order while traversing the boundary, creating enhanced pinning that facilitates further twin nucleation rather than transmission or absorption mechanisms observed in conventional metals.[43] These ordering-modified interactions represent a distinctive deformation mechanism specific to multicomponent systems containing local ordering, with no direct analogue in conventional metallic systems.[43]

Future research directions should focus on extending fundamental understanding of local ordering effects to additional crystal structures beyond the face-centered cubic and body-centered cubic systems that currently dominate the literature, including hexagonal close-packed high-entropy alloys and emerging complex crystal structures in compositionally complex alloys.[5][8][21][48] Advanced experimental techniques including in situ transmission electron microscopy during mechanical testing combined with real-time chemical analysis through atom probe tomography represent powerful approaches for observing deformation mechanisms and simultaneously tracking compositional distributions at nanometer scales.[5][8] Computational methods including machine-learning interatomic potentials fitted to extensive density functional theory databases enable prediction of ordering propensities and deformation mechanisms for unexplored compositions without requiring time-consuming experimental characterization.[7][25] The design of high-entropy alloys for extreme environments spanning cryogenic temperatures through elevated temperatures, radiation environments, and corrosive media should explicitly incorporate local ordering considerations as a primary design variable rather than treating ordering as an incidental complication of complex compositions.[5][7][43] The demonstration that intentional engineering of local ordering provides rational access to combinations of mechanical properties previously thought incompatible represents a transformative advance in materials design philosophy, suggesting that the enormous compositional space available to multicomponent alloys remains substantially unexploited by current approaches focusing exclusively on average composition and neglecting the atomic-scale spatial organization that proves so critical to mechanical performance.
