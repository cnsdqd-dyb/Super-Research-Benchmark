# Thermal Runaway Propagation Mechanisms and Their Role in Designing Safety Redundancy for Lithium-Ion Battery Packs: The Critical Contributions of Electrolyte and Separator Stability

Thermal runaway propagation represents one of the most critical safety challenges in lithium-ion battery pack design, requiring a comprehensive understanding of cascading failure mechanisms and careful engineering of multiple protective layers[1][32]. The catastrophic nature of thermal runaway events—where a single cell's uncontrolled exothermic reaction initiates a chain reaction through adjacent cells—demands that battery engineers develop sophisticated safety redundancy systems grounded in detailed knowledge of how heat, gases, and particulates transfer between cells[1][4]. Central to this challenge is the stability of the electrolyte and separator materials, which fundamentally determine both the initiation and propagation pathways of thermal events[8][11]. This report examines how understanding thermal runaway propagation mechanisms directly informs the design of multi-layered safety systems, with particular emphasis on the critical roles that electrolyte chemistry and separator performance play in preventing catastrophic cascade failures.

## Understanding Thermal Runaway Propagation Mechanisms and Their Six-Phase Evolution

Thermal runaway in lithium-ion batteries originates from a self-sustaining, exothermic chemical reaction that occurs when an individual battery cell loses its capacity to dissipate heat[7][55]. The process begins when mechanical damage, electrical abuse, or thermal exposure triggers a cascade of internal chemical reactions that generate increasingly more heat, creating a positive feedback loop that cannot be controlled by the cell's internal protection mechanisms[1][7]. Unlike simple overheating scenarios, thermal runaway involves the simultaneous operation of multiple degradation pathways—each contributing heat and reactive products that accelerate subsequent reactions. Understanding these distinct phases proves essential for designing effective safety redundancy, as each phase presents different challenges and requires different mitigation strategies[1][32].

The initial phase of thermal runaway occurs when the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer on the anode begins to decompose at relatively low temperatures[31][34]. This decomposition becomes exothermic around 100 to 150 degrees Celsius and releases volatile compounds that further destabilize the cell interior[31]. As temperatures continue to rise, the separator material experiences thermal stress that can lead to shrinkage or melting, depending on the polymer composition used[2][17][27]. Traditional polyolefin-based separators—commonly made from polypropylene or polyethylene—have melting points in the range of 130 to 160 degrees Celsius[17][27], which means they can fail relatively early in a thermal runaway sequence. This early separator failure represents a critical juncture in the propagation mechanism, as it removes the primary barrier preventing direct contact between anode and cathode materials. When the separator fails, the liquid electrolyte—a highly flammable mixture of organic carbonate solvents with low flash points typically between minus ten and twenty-five degrees Celsius[11][50]—becomes exposed to reactive lithium metal and hot cathode materials.

The second phase involves the onset of active thermal runaway at approximately 200 to 250 degrees Celsius, where the electrolyte itself begins to decompose in earnest[8][11]. The organic solvents in the electrolyte undergo thermal decomposition that produces flammable gases including hydrogen, methane, ethylene, and ethane[11][12]. Simultaneously, lithium salts such as lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF₆), which are ubiquitous in commercial electrolytes, undergo thermal decomposition reactions that generate phosphorus pentafluoride (PF₅), a strong Lewis acid that further catalyzes decomposition of the electrolyte solvents[8]. This phase transition is particularly critical because it marks the point where the electrolyte transforms from a liquid ionic conductor into a source of combustible gases and heat—essentially becoming a fuel for further exothermic reactions rather than a passive medium[8][11].

The third major phase occurs when cathode materials begin to decompose at higher temperatures, typically above 250 to 300 degrees Celsius[8][17]. Nickel-rich layered oxide cathodes such as nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC) become thermally unstable at these temperatures and release oxygen from their crystal lattice structure[8][17][49]. This oxygen release is particularly dangerous because it provides an internal oxidizer that can burn the surrounding electrolyte vapors, creating a self-sustaining combustion reaction that requires no external air supply[8][17][21]. In contrast, lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cathodes, which have an exceptionally stable olivine crystal structure, exhibit far superior thermal stability and maximum temperatures around 600 degrees Celsius compared to over 1000 degrees Celsius for NMC materials[17][18][30].

Beyond the cell-level chemistry, thermal propagation involves distinct short-term and long-term mechanisms[1][32]. In the first few minutes following thermal runaway initiation—the critical window defined by regulatory standards requiring passengers to exit vehicles safely within five minutes—heat transfers to neighboring cells through three primary pathways[1][32]. The first pathway is direct cell-to-cell conduction, where heat conducts through face-to-face contact between adjacent cells[1]. In cylindrical cell configurations, individual cells typically contact six neighbors, whereas prismatic and pouch cells contact two adjacent neighbors on their major surfaces[1]. This direct conduction pathway represents perhaps the most controllable mechanism for thermal propagation, as it can be interrupted with effective thermal barriers placed between cells[1][32][33].

The second short-term propagation mechanism involves hot gas and particulate ejection[1][32]. When a cell in thermal runaway generates gas at temperatures exceeding 600 degrees Celsius with pressure buildups reaching 200 kilopascals or higher, the cell vents these gases—not as a slow diffusion process but as violent ejection from dedicated vent mechanisms[9][12]. Critically, this venting also ejects hot particulates including molten aluminum from the cell casing, melted plastic components, and solid chunks of copper from the current collectors[1]. These particulates can maintain elevated temperatures for up to 60 seconds and subject surrounding cells to both thermal radiation and erosive mechanical blast damage[1]. The gas itself, if not properly managed within the pack enclosure, can spread rapidly over the tops and sides of modules, directly triggering adjacent cells through convective heating and bypassing any cell-to-cell barriers[1][32].

The third short-term pathway involves natural convection across air gaps within the pack[1][32]. Unless the pack geometry and component placement specifically prevent it, hot combustion products from the venting cell will rise and spread through the available air space, heating adjacent cells through radiation and convective heat transfer[1][32]. Regulatory guidelines recognize that unless all passengers can safely exit within five minutes, the thermal runaway event constitutes a safety failure[1][32]. However, as designers better understand these propagation mechanisms, more stringent regulations and shorter egress windows are anticipated to emerge[1][32].

Long-term propagation mechanisms become critical on timescales of 15 to 60 minutes, and they operate even after the initial violent phase has subsided[1][32]. Secondary conductive pathways—heat bridges that bypass the primary cell-to-cell thermal barriers—become dominant during this phase[1][32]. Bus bars, typically made from aluminum or copper for electrical conductivity, act as thermal bridges between cell tabs of neighboring cells[1][32]. These conductive pathways extend beyond the lateral profile of individual cells, meaning that even highly effective cell-to-cell thermal barriers have zero impact on heat transfer through bus bars[1][32]. Cooling plates, whether passive or active, present another major thermal bridge, forming a pathway that essentially tunnels underneath cell-to-cell barriers and conducts heat laterally between cells[1][32]. Additionally, structural interconnects, frame elements, and other metallic components can form secondary thermal bridges that redistribute heat throughout the pack[1].

Active cooling systems represent the final mechanism for managing long-term thermal propagation, though their availability and effectiveness depend critically on whether the battery management system and thermal management infrastructure remain intact following the initial thermal runaway[1][32]. In post-crash scenarios or situations where the thermal event damages cooling circuits, these systems may be unavailable[1][32]. However, when operational, active liquid cooling can eventually absorb excess thermal energy at a rate exceeding heat generation, providing a pathway to stabilize or cool affected cells over extended timescales[1][32].

## The Critical Role of Electrolyte Chemistry in Thermal Runaway Initiation and Propagation

The electrolyte in lithium-ion batteries represents one of the most significant—yet frequently underappreciated—factors determining both the severity of thermal runaway and the speed of propagation through a battery pack[8][11]. Traditional organic liquid electrolytes, which consist of lithium salts dissolved in mixtures of carbonate solvents such as ethylene carbonate, dimethyl carbonate, and ethyl methyl carbonate, exhibit two fundamental problems from a thermal safety perspective: they have extremely low thermal stability and they are highly flammable[8][11][50]. The thermal decomposition of these electrolytes begins at temperatures between 100 and 200 degrees Celsius—well below the maximum temperatures reached during thermal runaway—and produces gases that are themselves combustible[8][11].

The physics of electrolyte degradation during thermal runaway reveals the critical importance of electrolyte chemistry to propagation speed and severity[8][11]. When a lithium-ion battery cell enters thermal runaway, the electrolyte receives heat and begins to decompose thermally[8]. During the initial decomposition phase at moderate temperatures (100-200°C), the organic carbonate solvents lose volatile components through evaporation and decomposition, creating pressure buildup within the cell[8][12]. This pressure buildup alone is dangerous, as it forces gases and liquid electrolyte vapors out of the cell at high velocity. The decomposition products themselves include flammable gases—primarily hydrogen, methane, and light hydrocarbons—that can exceed the lower flammability limit when mixed with air[11][12].

The situation becomes dramatically more dangerous when the cell reaches temperatures above 150 degrees Celsius, where lithium salts begin their thermal decomposition[8]. In particular, LiPF₆, the most commonly used lithium salt in commercial electrolytes, undergoes decomposition that generates phosphorus pentafluoride (PF₅)[8]. Phosphorus pentafluoride is an extremely strong Lewis acid that catalyzes further decomposition of the electrolyte solvents, creating a runaway decomposition cascade where the products of one degradation reaction cause further degradation of other materials[8]. Research demonstrates that when LiPF₆ is present in organic solvents, the thermal instability of the electrolyte is substantially aggravated compared to LiPF₆-free systems[8]. This salt-catalyzed degradation represents a fundamental chemical vulnerability in traditional lithium-ion electrolytes that directly drives thermal propagation by generating heat and flammable gases at an accelerating rate.

At temperatures exceeding 200 degrees Celsius, the electrolyte decomposition transitions from gradual thermal breakdown to rapid, exothermic decomposition[8][11]. The combustion of electrolyte vapors produces additional heat, which further accelerates decomposition of unreacted electrolyte, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of heat and gas generation[8][11]. Experimental measurements show that thermal runaway begins in earnest around 240 degrees Celsius, with the self-heating rate and gas generation reaching maximum values in the 250 to 350 degree Celsius range[11]. During this peak thermal runaway phase, gas generation rates can reach 3 liters per ampere-hour of cell capacity, with pressure buildups exceeding 200 kilopascals[9]. For a typical electric vehicle cell with capacity in the 50-100 ampere-hour range, this means gas generation rates of 150 to 300 liters per minute during the most violent phase of thermal runaway.

The flammability of vented gases represents a critical propagation mechanism that conventional safety designs have only partially addressed[11]. The mixture of vented electrolyte solvents, decomposition gases, hydrogen, and hydrocarbons creates a fuel-air mixture that, when exposed to the hot cell surfaces or any ignition source, burns with intense heat release[11]. Experimental studies demonstrate that vent gases from cells entering thermal runaway are indeed combustible, with flame propagation observed when these gases ignite in air[11]. This vented gas combustion can propagate flames across the exterior of the cell casing and into adjacent cells, bypassing the thermal barriers designed to prevent cell-to-cell conduction. Additionally, the heat of combustion from burning vent gases directly heats neighboring cells, potentially initiating thermal runaway in cells that might otherwise have remained stable[11].

To address these fundamental electrolyte vulnerabilities, researchers and battery manufacturers have pursued several complementary strategies[8][11][50]. One approach involves developing non-flammable electrolyte alternatives such as room-temperature ionic liquids (RTILs), which are liquids composed entirely of ions with negligible vapor pressure[11][50]. Because ionic liquids have essentially zero volatility, they cannot form flammable vapor clouds when heated[11]. Research demonstrates that batteries filled with ionic liquid electrolytes do not contain any volatile components and therefore are not flammable, even when vented during thermal runaway[11]. Room-temperature ionic liquids also exhibit broad electrochemical stability windows, generally exceeding 4 volts, which is necessary for high-energy lithium-ion battery applications[11]. However, ionic liquids present practical challenges including higher viscosity, which can limit low-temperature performance and charging rates, and significantly higher cost compared to conventional organic electrolytes[11][50].

Another major approach involves incorporating flame-retardant additives into conventional organic electrolytes[11][50]. Phosphorus-based compounds represent the most widely studied class of flame retardants for battery electrolytes, as they suppress flames through gas-phase mechanisms while also promoting protective coating formation in the liquid phase[50]. Specific phosphorus-containing additives such as tris(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) phosphate (TFEP) and tris(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) phosphite (TTFP) have demonstrated particularly promising results, with some formulations creating effectively non-flammable electrolytes even at high temperatures[50]. Fluorine-containing variants of phosphorus flame retardants show synergistic effects that enhance both flame retardancy and electrochemical compatibility[50]. At concentrations around 15 percent in conventional solvent mixtures, some phosphorus-fluorine additives achieve flammability performance approaching zero on standard self-extinguishing time (SET) tests[50].

The relationship between electrolyte chemistry and thermal propagation speed is remarkably direct: highly flammable electrolytes that decompose easily at moderate temperatures will initiate secondary ignition in neighboring cells more readily and will propagate heat more rapidly through convective and radiative mechanisms[8][11]. Conversely, electrolytes with improved thermal stability and reduced flammability will slow the transition from cell-to-cell heat conduction to secondary combustion events, providing critical additional time for detection systems to activate and protective mechanisms to engage. This chemistry-to-propagation link fundamentally drives the requirement for thermal-stable and non-flammable electrolytes as a core component of safety redundancy design.

## Separator Technology as the First Barrier Against Thermal Runaway Escalation

The battery separator—the thin microporous membrane positioned between the anode and cathode—performs multiple critical functions in normal battery operation and especially during thermal abuse[2][27][45]. During normal cycling, the separator provides an electronic insulator that prevents direct contact between anode and cathode while maintaining sufficient ionic conductivity to allow lithium-ion transport between electrodes[2][27][45]. However, the thermal stability of the separator material determines whether this critical barrier remains functional during the extreme conditions of thermal runaway[2][27][45]. Traditional polyolefin-based separators made from polypropylene (PP) or polyethylene (PE)—selected primarily for their chemical inertness and low cost—have maximum operating temperatures around 130 to 160 degrees Celsius[2][27]. When separator temperature exceeds these thresholds, the polymer undergoes progressive shrinkage and melting, causing pores to collapse and the separator to lose both its electronic insulation and ionic conductivity[2][27][45].

The thermal degradation of polyolefin separators during thermal runaway represents a fundamental failure mechanism that directly enables subsequent catastrophic degradation[2][27]. When the separator melts and pores collapse, the anode and cathode come into direct contact, eliminating the electronic insulation that has been maintained throughout the cell's operational life[2][27]. This direct contact allows electron flow between the anode and cathode, establishing an internal short circuit that generates intense local heating[2][27]. Additionally, the collapse of separator porosity allows the electrolyte to concentrate in the contact region, exposing highly reactive lithium metal to hot cathode material without the diffusion barriers provided by intact separator pores[2][27]. The net result is dramatically accelerated exothermic reactions compared to the rate that would occur if the separator remained intact.

Modern advances in separator technology seek to address this thermal vulnerability through multiple complementary approaches[2][27][45][48]. One major direction involves coating traditional polyolefin separators with inorganic ceramic materials that provide substantially higher thermal stability[2][27][45][48]. High-purity alumina (Al₂O₃) represents one of the most promising ceramic coating materials, selected for its exceptional thermal resistance, high melting point exceeding 2000 degrees Celsius, excellent electrical insulation properties, and resistance to reactions with both electrolyte and electrode materials[2][27][45]. Experimental studies demonstrate that separator shrinkage at elevated temperatures is substantially reduced by alumina coatings; for example, an alpha-alumina-coated polyethylene separator exhibits considerably lower thermal shrinkage than uncoated polyethylene at the same temperature[27]. The thermal insulation properties of ceramic-coated separators effectively suppress heat conduction through the separator itself, which helps prevent hotspot formation and enhances overall cell safety[45].

Beyond thermal stability, ceramic coatings enhance separator safety through multiple additional mechanisms[2][27][45]. High-purity alumina and other ceramic coatings provide structural support that prevents mechanical failure even when the underlying polymer separator experiences partial softening[45]. The ceramic coating also protects against dendrite formation by preventing lithium metal whiskers from growing through the separator and creating internal short circuits[27][45]. Furthermore, the coating maintains permeability for lithium ions through repeated charge and discharge cycles, ensuring efficient battery operation even under thermal stress[45]. Recent developments have demonstrated that alumina-polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) composite separators, which incorporate aluminum oxide particles without polymeric binders, exhibit superior porosity, thermal stability, and electrolyte wettability compared to conventional polyolefin-based separators[45]. These advanced separators maintain thermal stability up to approximately 470 degrees Celsius without significant weight loss, compared to traditional polyolefin separators that typically fail around 150 degrees Celsius[45].

A complementary separator innovation involves incorporating thermal shutdown mechanisms directly into the separator structure[2][17][27]. Shutdown separators are designed to melt and close their pores at a specific temperature threshold—typically around 135 degrees Celsius—stopping both the electrochemical reaction and ion flow before the cell temperature can rise further[17][27]. This shutdown function provides an automatic protection mechanism that operates without requiring any external control system or active response[17][27]. By halting ion transport before thermal runaway begins, shutdown separators provide critical additional time for other safety systems to detect and respond to the thermal event. However, shutdown separators alone cannot prevent thermal runaway if the cell has already entered an active thermal runaway phase, as the heat generation rate will exceed the cooling capacity of the shutdown mechanism[17].

Recent research has demonstrated the potential of phase-change-assisted shutdown separators that incorporate phase-change particles into the separator structure[2][14]. These particles absorb thermal energy as they melt, providing active heat management while simultaneously triggering separator shutdown[2][14]. The phase-change material extends the time available for protective systems to respond while the separator itself transitions from open to closed, effectively creating a multi-stage thermal defense[2][14]. Additionally, some advanced separators incorporate zeolite coatings with high surface area that can adsorb water, dissolved metals, and even pre-loaded flame retardants[14]. The zeolite-integrated separator coating provides electronic insulation while maintaining high wettability for electrolyte uptake, enhancing both safety and electrochemical performance[14].

The fundamental design challenge in separator technology is balancing conflicting requirements: thermal stability and safety demand rigid, thermally resistant materials like ceramics, while mechanical robustness and ion transport demand flexible, porous materials like polymers[27][45]. Advanced separators address this through composite designs that layer thermal-stable ceramic materials with flexible polymeric substrates, or through engineered nanofiber structures that combine multiple material properties[27][45]. As thermal stability of separators improves, the heat absorbed by separator degradation extends the time available for other propagation control mechanisms—thermal barriers, gas management, and active cooling—to become effective, directly enabling more robust safety redundancy design.

## Multi-Layer Safety Redundancy Design: From Cell-Level Protections to Pack-Level Systems

Understanding thermal runaway propagation mechanisms and the roles of electrolyte and separator stability directly informs the design of comprehensive multi-layer safety redundancy systems[1][32][33][37]. Effective thermal propagation prevention requires isolating and controlling each of the multiple heat transfer pathways identified above, recognizing that no single approach can address all mechanisms[1][7][10][22][32]. This section examines how battery designers integrate understanding of propagation physics into layered protective systems operating at three distinct levels: cell-level internal safeguards, module-level isolation and containment, and pack-level detection and control systems[1][3][6][25][26][56].

### Cell-Level Internal Safety Mechanisms

At the individual cell level, lithium-ion batteries incorporate several passive safety devices designed to interrupt thermal runaway progression before heat transfer to neighboring cells becomes significant[6][26][29]. The positive temperature coefficient (PTC) device is a thermistor that exhibits low electrical resistance during normal operation but experiences dramatically increased resistance when temperature exceeds a threshold, typically above 90 degrees Celsius[6][26]. This increased resistance reduces electrical current flow and heat generation within the cell[6][26]. However, PTC devices are most effective for protection against external overcurrent events and provide limited protection during internally initiated thermal runaway, where the current path and heat generation rate may bypass the PTC's influence[6][26].

The current interrupt device (CID) represents a more direct mechanical safety mechanism, consisting of a pressure-activated fuse that physically breaks the electrical circuit when internal cell pressure exceeds a threshold—typically around 1000 kilopascals or 145 pounds per square inch[6][26][29]. The CID operates through a scored disk design that separates under pressure, disconnecting the electrical circuit and simultaneously allowing gas to vent in a controlled manner[6][26][29]. However, the CID activates based on pressure buildup rather than temperature or thermal runaway specifically, meaning it operates best for conditions where gas generation and pressure rise are the primary indicators of cell distress[26][29]. In some thermal runaway scenarios, particularly where heat generation outpaces gas generation, the CID may not activate until the cell has already reached dangerously high temperatures[6][26].

The safety vent represents the final cell-level protection mechanism, releasing gas from the cell at excessive pressure levels—typically around 3000 kilopascals—to prevent the cell casing from rupturing explosively[6]. However, the venting process itself creates new safety challenges, as the released gases are flammable and can ignite or propagate to neighboring cells[6][9][12]. Additionally, venting does not stop the underlying exothermic reactions within the cell and does not prevent heat transfer to adjacent cells through conduction or radiation[6]. These cell-level protections provide important first-line defense but are fundamentally limited in their ability to prevent thermal propagation once a cell enters active thermal runaway[6][26].

### Module-Level Isolation and Thermal Barriers

The module level—where individual cells are grouped into assemblies with shared packaging and cooling infrastructure—represents the critical design layer where thermal propagation control becomes achievable[1][32][33][37]. Module-level design incorporates two primary categories of protective mechanisms: thermal propagation barriers placed between cells, and gas management systems designed to control vented gas distribution[1][32][33][37].

Cell-to-cell (C2C) thermal barriers serve as the first line of defense against thermal propagation, acting as firewalls between adjacent cells[1][33][36]. An effective C2C barrier must simultaneously satisfy two contradictory mechanical and thermal requirements[33][36][40]. First, it must act as a compression pad, absorbing mechanical movement of cells as they swell during the charge-discharge cycle, expanding with age, and moving relative to each other due to thermal cycling[33]. Historically, this mechanical function was fulfilled by soft materials like polyurethane foams or silicone foams, which excel at absorbing strain energy but typically fail above 100 to 300 degrees Celsius[33][36][40]. Second, the barrier must provide exceptional thermal insulation—withstanding temperatures of 850 degrees Celsius or higher and maintaining low thermal conductivity even when compressed[1][33][36].

Traditional insulation materials like metals and mica sheets provide excellent high-temperature thermal performance but are essentially rigid and cannot absorb the mechanical movement required during normal battery operation[33][36][40]. The ideal C2C barrier would reside in the upper-right corner of a plot comparing maximum exposure temperature (Y-axis) and strain energy absorption (X-axis), providing heat resistance five times greater than silicone foam and mechanical absorption four orders of magnitude above mica sheets[36][40]. Materials satisfying both requirements are extremely rare, with engineered aerogel emerging as one of the few practical options[33][36][40]. Silica aerogels, such as the PyroThin material developed by Aspen Aerogels, achieve this combination through their unique nanostructure[33][36][40].

Aerogels develop their exceptional performance through a sol-gel chemistry process that creates long polymer chains of silica molecules forming a solid skeleton with extremely small pores—approximately 10,000 times smaller than other insulation materials[36][40]. This nanoscale porosity is the critical feature that enables aerogel performance: the thermal conductivity of aerogel becomes lower than still air, so when aerogel is compressed and air is squeezed out of the pores, the thermal conductivity actually improves rather than degrading as would occur with conventional insulation materials[36][40]. Simultaneously, the long silica-polymer chains that form during curing act as billions of elastic nano-springs that allow the compressed aerogel to absorb strain energy and recover its shape after decompression[36][40].

Testing demonstrates that aerogel barriers effectively contain cell-to-cell thermal propagation in realistic conditions[33][36][40]. In mini-module tests with two adjacent prismatic cells compressed in a fixture to simulate end-of-life pressure conditions, a 2.35-millimeter aerogel barrier successfully prevented thermal propagation despite one cell being triggered into active thermal runaway[33][36][40]. The trigger cell reached temperatures exceeding 800 degrees Celsius, with peak temperatures generating intense heat flux toward the adjacent cell[33][36][40]. However, the aerogel barrier absorbed this heat, allowing the adjacent cell to reach only 130 degrees Celsius at the critical five-minute mark and preventing it from entering thermal runaway[33][36][40]. While this mini-module configuration isolates the propagation mechanism to direct cell-to-cell conduction and does not fully replicate the secondary conduction pathways present in actual packs, the demonstration proves that thermal runaway can be stopped at the cell-to-cell level, allowing designers to address other propagation mechanisms separately[33][36][40].

Beyond cell-to-cell thermal barriers, module-level safety design must address gas management—the control and venting of flammable gases released during thermal runaway[1][7][9][32][37]. Gas management represents an underappreciated but critical component of thermal propagation control, as uncontrolled gas spread directly heats neighboring cells and can short-circuit all other protective mechanisms[1][32]. When a cell vents, the gases—which include hydrogen, methane, and other hydrocarbons—spread rapidly through the available space above the cells, heating all adjacent surfaces through convection[1][32]. The hot gases can reach temperatures exceeding 600 degrees Celsius and carry significant heat content[9]. Unless pathways for gas flow are carefully designed and limited, hot gases will spread to all surrounding cells essentially simultaneously, potentially triggering thermal runaway across entire modules[1][32].

Advanced gas management approaches employ several techniques to extend the time required for vented gases to reach adjacent cells[1][9][32][37][43]. One approach uses isolation baffles that divide the gas space into separate regions, converting rapid gas propagation into more controlled flow through defined pathways[9][37][43]. Another technique routes vented gases through multiple separation stages where particle filters remove hot particulates while the gas itself cools through expansion and heat transfer to the enclosure walls[9][37][43]. Some designs incorporate dedicated exhaust pathways that vent gas out of the module through external ducts, removing the thermal energy from the vicinity of adjacent cells[9][37][43]. Additionally, staged venting architectures with multiple pressure relief thresholds provide tight control over pressure and gas flow, releasing gas at lower rates initially when pressures begin to rise, then providing larger venting capacity only if pressures continue to increase during full thermal runaway[9][37][43].

### Pack-Level Detection, Electrical Isolation, and Active Thermal Management

The battery pack level—encompassing all cells and modules integrated into a complete energy storage system—requires detection and response systems that can identify thermal anomalies and activate isolation mechanisms before thermal propagation becomes uncontrollable[3][6][13][16][25][56]. The Battery Management System (BMS) serves as the computational brain of thermal runaway detection and response, continuously monitoring cell voltages, currents, and temperatures to identify abnormal conditions requiring intervention[3][25][56].

However, traditional BMS architectures face fundamental challenges in detecting thermal runaway with sufficient speed[13][16]. Thermal runaway can develop rapidly—the transition from normal cell operation to active thermal runaway can occur within 30 to 60 seconds—while temperature sensors have inherent response delays and may not be optimally positioned to detect the early stages of thermal runaway within individual cells[13][16][23]. Conventional current and voltage monitoring cannot reliably distinguish normal high-rate discharge or charging from incipient thermal runaway, as both conditions generate similar electrical signatures[13][16]. This detection challenge has driven research into multiple complementary sensing approaches[13][16][51].

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) represents an advanced sensing technique that detects internal faults by applying small alternating current signals across the battery and analyzing the impedance response across a wide frequency range[51]. EIS provides multidimensional data about internal resistance, charge transfer kinetics, and ion diffusion, revealing degradation and faults that would not appear in conventional voltage-current measurements[51]. Experimental studies demonstrate that EIS can achieve up to 97.5 percent early detection accuracy for internal short circuits in lithium-ion battery packs, with some cell chemistries reaching 100 percent detection accuracy[51]. By detecting internal faults and short circuits before they develop into thermal runaway, EIS-based systems provide additional time for protective responses to activate[51].

Acoustic emission monitoring represents another complementary sensing approach that detects the mechanical signatures of internal degradation within battery cells[16][51]. As dendrites grow, separators begin to fail, or gas bubbles form within the electrolyte, these mechanical processes generate acoustic signals that can be detected by sensitive accelerometers or acoustic sensors[16][51]. Acoustic precursor detection has demonstrated ability to warn of thermal runaway initiation minutes or hours before temperature reaches critical thresholds[16][51]. While acoustic monitoring requires careful sensor placement and signal processing to avoid false positives from mechanical vibration or other noise sources, it represents a promising approach for very early detection of developing faults[16][51].

Once thermal anomalies are detected, pack-level response systems must rapidly isolate affected cells or modules from the rest of the system to prevent current flow and limit continued heat generation[3][6][25][37]. Electrical isolation can be achieved through high-speed contactors or solid-state switches that disconnect affected cells from the electrical circuit within milliseconds of fault detection[3][37]. In modular battery architectures, isolation of individual modules prevents thermal events in one module from propagating through the electrical interconnections to adjacent modules[3][25][37]. Selective cell disconnection strategies that identify and isolate individual faulty cells while allowing the remaining cells in a series string to continue operating represent more advanced approaches that maintain system operation despite individual cell failures[23][37].

Pack-level thermal management systems employ active liquid cooling or air cooling to remove excess heat and prevent temperature rise throughout the pack[7][14][38][39][42]. Traditional approaches use cold plates attached to battery cells with coolant circulating through the plates to remove heat[14][39]. More advanced approaches employ immersion cooling, where battery cells are submerged directly into a dielectric coolant, providing superior thermal contact and more uniform heat distribution[39]. Phase-change materials (PCMs) represent a passive thermal management approach where materials with high latent heat—such as paraffin wax with melting points around 67 degrees Celsius—absorb thermal energy as they melt, providing passive heat buffering without requiring active pumping[38][41][42].

Multi-stage thermal management architectures coordinate these approaches to optimize performance across normal operation and thermal emergencies[14][38]. For example, five-mode coolant orchestration systems monitor cell temperatures and select between Cooling mode (active heat removal during normal operation), Heating mode (warming cold cells during cold-weather charging), Equalization mode (managing temperature gradients), Preservation mode (minimal active cooling to maximize efficiency), and Safety mode (maximum cooling during thermal events)[14]. By varying pump speed, coolant temperature, and circulation time, these systems maintain tighter cell-to-cell temperature gradients without increasing pack height or weight[14].

## Integration of Material Science and Thermal Management: Designing Robust Safety Architectures

The transition from understanding thermal runaway propagation mechanisms to implementing effective safety redundancy requires seamless integration of advanced materials, mechanical design, and active control systems[22][30][37][40]. This integration process involves deliberate material selection at each hierarchical level, from the chemical composition of electrolytes to the choice of thermal barrier materials to the design of pack-level containment structures[22][30][37][40].

Material selection for thermal barriers must account for application-specific requirements and cell chemistry characteristics[10][22][30][37][40]. Different cell chemistries exhibit different thermal runaway temperatures: lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells reach maximum temperatures around 600 degrees Celsius with relatively gentle particle ejection, while nickel-rich NMC and NCA cells exceed 1000 degrees Celsius with violent particulate release[17][30]. This chemistry-dependent thermal behavior directly drives different requirements for barrier materials, pack architecture, and management strategies[17][30]. LFP batteries, with their superior thermal stability and gentler thermal runaway characteristics, may require less aggressive thermal isolation, allowing designers to reduce thermal barrier mass and cost while maintaining safety margins[17][30].

Electrolyte selection represents a fundamental design decision that establishes the baseline safety characteristics of the entire battery pack[8][11][50]. Traditional organic electrolytes with low thermal stability and high flammability require aggressive propagation control measures—thick thermal barriers, extensive gas management infrastructure, and sophisticated detection systems[8][11]. In contrast, developing batteries with non-flammable electrolytes—such as ionic liquids, solid electrolytes, or electrolytes with high-performance flame-retardant additives—shifts the safety baseline substantially, reducing the risk of secondary combustion and allowing simpler propagation control mechanisms to achieve equivalent safety performance[8][11][50][53].

Separator selection similarly influences the overall safety redundancy architecture[2][27][45][48]. Traditional uncoated polyolefin separators require careful module-level design to address the risk of separator failure during thermal runaway[2][27][45]. In contrast, ceramic-coated separators and advanced composite separators maintain structural integrity at substantially higher temperatures, reducing the risk of internal short circuits and providing more time for other safety mechanisms to respond[2][27][45].

The design of electrical interconnects and thermal pathways represents a critical but often overlooked integration point[1][22][30][32][37]. Bus bars, cooling plate attachments, and structural elements create secondary thermal conduction pathways that can bypass cell-to-cell thermal barriers[1][32][37]. Recognizing this reality, sophisticated pack designs now incorporate intentional isolation of these secondary pathways—either through thermally insulating interface materials between bus bars and cell groups, or through careful electrical architecture that minimizes the number of conductive pathways between cells that would otherwise serve as thermal bridges[1][22][37].

Redundant safety systems architecture ensures that failure of any single protective mechanism does not result in uncontrolled thermal propagation[3][6][25][28][56]. For example, effective pack-level safety requires that detection systems, electrical isolation mechanisms, and thermal management systems all function independently and can each provide meaningful protection if others fail[3][25][28][56]. In modular UPS systems for data centers, redundancy architecture achieves N+1 or 2N configurations where loss of a single module does not compromise system operation, while independent BMS controllers monitoring each module provide fault isolation even if the master controller fails[3][46][56]. This redundancy philosophy—where multiple independent pathways provide protection against thermal propagation—directly reflects understanding of the multiple mechanisms through which thermal runaway can propagate and spread.

## Advanced Approaches: From Solid-State Batteries to AI-Driven Detection Systems

As understanding of thermal runaway propagation mechanisms has deepened, researchers have explored more ambitious approaches to fundamentally suppress or prevent thermal runaway rather than simply controlling its propagation. Solid-state batteries represent one major frontier, replacing the flammable liquid electrolyte with solid electrolyte materials that inherently cannot generate flammable vapor clouds[24][21]. In solid-state batteries, the absence of liquid electrolyte eliminates the possibility of catastrophic secondary combustion events where vented electrolyte vapors ignite in air[24][21]. Additionally, solid electrolytes maintain mechanical and electrical integrity at substantially higher temperatures compared to liquid electrolytes, potentially allowing cells to function safely even when conventional safety mechanisms would trigger shutdown[24].

However, solid-state batteries introduce new safety considerations[21][24]. The lithium metal anode employed in most solid-state battery designs exhibits extremely high reactivity with oxygen, meaning that even in the absence of liquid electrolyte, thermal runaway at high temperatures can trigger direct lithium-oxygen combustion reactions[21][24]. These reactions generate even more heat than the electrolyte combustion in conventional cells, potentially creating a more severe thermal runaway event[21][24]. Solid electrolytes can also degrade at high temperatures, releasing their own reactive oxygen or other species that participate in thermal runaway[21][24]. Additionally, the mechanical nature of solid-electrolyte batteries creates new failure modes: cracking of rigid electrolytes due to volume changes, dendrite formation that punctures electrolytes, and delamination of interfaces between solid electrolyte and electrodes[21][24].

ProLogium, a solid-state battery developer, addresses these challenges through what it terms "Dual Safety Architecture"—combining all-ceramic separators with superfluidized all-inorganic solid-state electrolytes that include embedded Active Safety Mechanism (ASM) materials[21]. The all-ceramic separator maintains mechanical integrity and electrical insulation at high temperatures while effectively blocking dendrite penetration[21]. The proprietary superfluidized all-inorganic electrolyte provides intrinsic non-combustibility, meaning it cannot burn even at high temperatures[21]. The embedded ASM material represents a critical safety innovation: at critical temperatures during thermal runaway, the ASM material activates and preferentially reacts with cathode and anode reactants, interrupting the thermal runaway chain reaction before propagation can accelerate[21]. In accelerating rate calorimetry tests, ProLogium's fourth-generation solid-state battery system demonstrated unprecedented thermal stability with no thermal runaway, no spontaneous heat peaks, and significantly reduced total exothermic energy compared to conventional lithium-ion batteries[21].

Artificial intelligence and machine learning increasingly augment BMS capabilities to detect thermal anomalies far earlier than conventional monitoring approaches[16][17][54]. AI-powered BMS systems analyze subtle patterns in voltage, current, and temperature data that precede thermal runaway by minutes or hours, predicting potential failures before they develop into critical safety events[16][17][54]. Machine learning models trained on large datasets of normal battery operation and fault conditions can identify the distinctive electrical signatures of developing dendrites, incipient separator failures, or early-stage internal short circuits with much higher sensitivity than rule-based detection algorithms[16][17][54]. Some advanced AI systems achieve predictions hours in advance of critical failures, providing ample time for controlled shutdown, reduced charging rates, or battery replacement before thermal events occur[16][17][54].

Vision transformer architectures—a class of deep learning models originally developed for computer vision—have been adapted for battery management to identify internal short circuits and predict thermal runaway with very high accuracy[54]. These models process time-series voltage, current, and temperature measurements as if they were "images" of battery state, recognizing patterns indicative of developing faults[54]. The advantage of transformer-based approaches is their ability to learn long-range dependencies and temporal patterns that traditional machine learning models may miss, enabling detection of slow-developing faults like gradual dendrite growth or progressive separator degradation[54].

## Testing, Validation, and Standards Development for Thermal Propagation Control

As understanding of thermal runaway propagation has advanced, regulatory bodies and standards organizations have increasingly focused on developing repeatable, reproducible test procedures that assess thermal propagation behavior under realistic conditions[3][35][43]. The electric vehicle safety regulations (EVS-GTR) currently require manufacturers to demonstrate that battery systems provide warning to vehicle occupants with sufficient time (5 minutes) for safe vehicle egress in the event of single-cell thermal runaway[35]. However, developing test procedures that consistently initiate single-cell thermal runaway due to internal short circuits—the most critical propagation scenario—has proven challenging[35].

Multiple approaches to thermal runaway initiation have been evaluated[3][12][35][43]: nail penetration (creating direct internal short circuit), external heating (simulating thermal abuse), and overcharging (driving cells to extreme states of charge)[35]. Each method produces different thermal runaway characteristics, including different time-to-ignition, peak temperatures, gas generation rates, and particle ejection velocities[12][35]. The heating rate substantially affects results: cells heated at 5°C per minute exhibit different propagation characteristics compared to cells heated at 20°C per minute, even when reaching the same peak temperature[12]. Standardizing on specific heating rates, heating areas, heating media, and nail specifications remains an active area of discussion within standards bodies[35].

Parallel testing approaches have emerged that employ thermal imaging and high-speed X-ray imaging to visualize thermal runaway evolution within battery cells and packs[20]. High-speed X-ray radiography with temporal resolution on the order of milliseconds allows researchers to observe the internal thermal evolution of cells as they enter thermal runaway, tracking the position of heat sources and their propagation to adjacent cells[20]. This technology reveals that thermal runaway propagation occurs at different speeds depending on cell connection type: cells connected in series experience higher peak temperatures and longer propagation times compared to parallel-connected cells, due to the electrical current flow characteristics during thermal runaway[20].

These advanced testing approaches inform more rigorous safety standards. The National Fire Protection Association's NFPA 855 standard, with its 2026 edition emphasizing partial volume deflagration analysis, reflects the growing sophistication of thermal propagation modeling[43]. Rather than assuming all vented gas distributes uniformly throughout the pack enclosure, NFPA 855's deflagration analysis considers realistic gas dispersion scenarios, allowing designers to specify vent sizing and placement based on actual gas flow dynamics[43]. This physics-based approach to safety design represents a substantial evolution from previous prescriptive standards that specified fixed vent sizes regardless of the actual gas generation characteristics of specific cell chemistries and designs[43].

## Comprehensive Integration: Safety Redundancy Framework Synthesis

The comprehensive safety redundancy framework for lithium-ion battery packs emerges when thermal runaway propagation mechanisms are fully integrated with electrolyte and separator stability considerations, layered protective mechanisms, and detection-and-response systems. This framework recognizes that no single protective mechanism is sufficient to prevent thermal propagation in all circumstances, and that effective safety requires multiple independent pathways, each capable of providing meaningful protection if others fail[1][3][6][32][37][56].

At the material chemistry level, the selection of thermal-stable, non-flammable electrolytes reduces the baseline risk of secondary combustion and flammable gas generation, allowing all downstream protective mechanisms to operate with greater safety margins[8][11][50]. The use of thermally stable separators—particularly those with ceramic coatings or advanced composite structures—prevents premature separator failure and maintains the integrity of the critical barrier between anode and cathode even as temperatures rise toward 600-1000 degrees Celsius depending on cell chemistry[2][27][45].

At the cell level, pressure relief vents, current interrupt devices, and positive temperature coefficient protectors provide first-line protection against cell-level overvoltage, overcurrent, and overtemperature conditions[6][26]. These cell-level mechanisms are fundamentally passive—requiring no external control system or active energy input—and therefore function even if all external systems fail[6][26].

At the module level, cell-to-cell thermal barriers interrupt direct heat conduction between adjacent cells, extending propagation delay times critical to meeting egress requirements[1][32][33][36]. Gas management systems control the distribution of vented gases, preventing rapid convective heating of all module cells from a single venting cell[1][9][32][37]. Combined, these module-level mechanisms typically delay thermal propagation by 10-30 minutes depending on design specifics and thermal barrier performance, providing ample time for higher-level systems to detect and respond[1][32][33].

At the pack level, sophisticated detection systems monitor multiple parameters—cell voltage, current, temperature, impedance, acoustic signature—to identify thermal anomalies with speed and sensitivity far exceeding conventional approaches[13][16][25][51][56]. When anomalies are detected, electrical isolation mechanisms rapidly disconnect affected cells or modules from the rest of the system, preventing continued current flow and subsequent heat generation[3][25][37]. Active thermal management systems continuously remove heat during normal operation and can be ramped to maximum capacity in response to detected thermal events, potentially preventing thermal escalation by removing heat faster than exothermic reactions can generate it[14][38][42].

At the vehicle or system level, architectural redundancy ensures that loss of any single protection mechanism does not result in catastrophic failure[3][28][56]. Dual or triple electrical pathways, redundant BMS controllers, independent thermal management circuits, and segregated safety device control loops each provide independent protection[3][28][56]. This redundancy philosophy, applied across all hierarchical levels from cell chemistry through pack architecture to vehicle systems, creates resilient battery safety systems capable of maintaining safe operation even when individual protective mechanisms fail or perform below design specifications[3][28][56].

## Conclusion

Thermal runaway propagation mechanisms fundamentally determine the design requirements for safety redundancy in lithium-ion battery packs, creating a direct linkage between understanding of failure physics and implementation of protective systems. The six-phase evolution of thermal runaway—from SEI decomposition through electrolyte degradation to cathode decomposition and finally to thermal propagation across module and pack scales—reveals multiple critical intervention points where carefully designed protections can delay or prevent cascade failure.

Electrolyte and separator stability emerge as critical variables determining thermal runaway severity and propagation speed. Conventional organic electrolytes with low thermal stability and high flammability fundamentally drive the need for aggressive propagation control measures, while non-flammable electrolytes and thermally stable separators shift the safety baseline, reducing pressure on mechanical barrier systems. This chemistry-to-system relationship means that battery developers cannot separate electrolyte development from pack-level safety design—the chemical properties of the electrolyte directly influence what mechanical, electrical, and thermal protections must be implemented downstream.

Effective thermal propagation control requires multi-layer redundancy where cell-level passive protections, module-level thermal barriers and gas management, and pack-level detection and response systems each address specific propagation mechanisms. No single protective layer suffices; the complementary nature of different mechanisms—where thermal barriers address conduction pathways while gas management addresses convection, electrical isolation interrupts continued current flow, and active cooling removes accumulated heat—creates resilient systems capable of maintaining safe operation even as individual mechanisms partially fail or are overwhelmed by extreme conditions.

The integration of advanced materials, sophisticated detection systems, and intelligent control algorithms represents the frontier of thermal runaway mitigation. Solid-state batteries with embedded active safety mechanisms, AI-powered fault prediction, and physics-based safety design standards reflect the field's evolution from empirical safety approaches to first-principles understanding of failure mechanisms. As battery energy density continues to increase and applications expand to ever more demanding service conditions, this science-based approach to safety design—grounded in understanding of thermal runaway propagation and informed by electrolyte and separator stability considerations—will remain essential to enabling safe, reliable, and high-performance battery systems that support the clean energy transition while maintaining the highest standards of safety.
