# Laser Treatment Indications in Diabetic Retinopathy: Balancing Disease Progression Prevention with Visual Field Deficit Risks

This comprehensive report examines the clinical indications for laser photocoagulation in diabetic retinopathy while carefully weighing the prevention of disease progression against the documented risks of visual field defects and other treatment-related complications. The evidence from landmark clinical trials including the Diabetic Retinopathy Study (DRS), Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS), and more recent Diabetic Retinopathy Clinical Research Network (DRCR.net) protocols demonstrates that laser treatment remains a critical tool for preventing severe vision loss in both proliferative and nonproliferative forms of diabetic retinopathy, though its role has evolved with the emergence of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy. Contemporary clinical practice requires careful stratification of patients based on disease severity, baseline visual function, glycemic control, and patient-specific factors to optimize outcomes while minimizing adverse effects. This report synthesizes current evidence regarding when laser treatment should be initiated, how it should be applied, and how recent technological advances and combination therapies have refined the risk-benefit calculation for individual patients.

## Historical Development and Mechanisms of Laser Photocoagulation in Diabetic Retinopathy

Laser photocoagulation emerged in the 1970s as the first effective treatment for sight-threatening diabetic retinopathy, fundamentally transforming the prognosis for patients with this leading cause of vision loss in the working-age population[1][2][8]. The Diabetic Retinopathy Study, conducted between 1972 and 1975 at 15 medical centers across the United States and enrolling over 1,700 patients, provided definitive evidence of laser treatment efficacy[5][8]. This landmark trial compared panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) using both xenon arc and argon laser technologies against observation in patients with severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) or proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)[5][8]. The results were compelling: patients who received PRP demonstrated a severe vision loss rate of only 6.4% over two years, compared to 16.3% in untreated eyes, representing a reduction in the risk of severe visual loss of more than 50%[5][8][10]. At four-year follow-up, treated eyes with high-risk characteristics showed only 20% severe vision loss compared to 44% in control eyes, establishing PRP as the gold standard for PDR management[5].

The physiological mechanisms underlying laser photocoagulation's therapeutic effects have been elucidated through decades of clinical investigation and basic research[1][7][20]. The widely accepted primary mechanism involves destruction of photoreceptor cells and outer retinal tissue by the laser photocoagulation, which fundamentally reduces the oxygen demand of the retina[7][20]. The glial scar tissue that develops during the repair process allows increased oxygen diffusion from the choriocapillaris to the inner retina, thereby relieving the hypoxic state that characterizes ischemic retinopathy[7][20]. As oxygen tension increases in the treated retina, vasoactive factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and protein kinase C (PKC) are downregulated, and the stimulus for neovascularization becomes suppressed[7][20]. A complementary mechanism involves the direct destruction of cells that produce neovascularization stimulators, while simultaneously generating scar tissue that releases potential neovascularization inhibitors including transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), erythropoietin (EPO), and pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF)[7][20]. By destroying the largely unused but metabolically demanding ischemic extramacular retina, PRP reduces the total area of ischemic tissue, which consequently reduces total VEGF production in the eye and thereby diminishes the impetus for pathological neovascularization[5][8].

The Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study, conducted shortly after the DRS and published in 1985, extended these findings to nonproliferative disease and provided crucial guidance about the timing of laser intervention[3][8][9][14]. This landmark trial enrolled 3,711 patients and randomized each participant's eyes to either early or deferred photocoagulation, creating the first detailed protocols for treating both focal macular edema and proliferative disease based on specific clinical findings[3][8]. The ETDRS demonstrated that focal and grid laser photocoagulation reduced the risk of moderate vision loss—defined as a decline of three or more lines of visual acuity—by approximately 50% in patients with clinically significant macular edema[3][7][14]. Importantly, the ETDRS established that only 15% of patients with clinically significant macular edema who received focal/grid laser treatment experienced moderate vision loss at three-year follow-up, compared to 30% of untreated eyes[3][14]. These results established laser photocoagulation as the foundational treatment for both proliferative and nonproliferative forms of diabetic retinopathy for nearly two decades before the introduction of anti-VEGF therapy.

## Indications for Panretinal Photocoagulation in Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy

Panretinal photocoagulation remains the mainstay treatment for proliferative diabetic retinopathy, representing one of the most robust clinical indications in contemporary ophthalmology[1][2][5][8][31][32]. The precise identification of high-risk characteristics determines the urgency and timing of PRP treatment. High-risk proliferative diabetic retinopathy is characterized by three specific features: neovascularization of the disc (NVD) affecting more than one-quarter to one-third of the disc area, any NVD associated with vitreous or preretinal hemorrhage, or any neovascularization elsewhere (NVE) associated with vitreous or preretinal hemorrhage[5][13][16][31]. Patients with these high-risk characteristics face a markedly elevated probability of severe vision loss without treatment and represent the clearest indication for prompt PRP[13]. The DRS data demonstrated that at two years, only 11% of treated eyes with high-risk PDR developed severe vision loss compared to 26% of untreated eyes, and this differential widened to 20% versus 44% by four years of follow-up[5][8].

The delineation between early proliferative diabetic retinopathy, which lacks high-risk characteristics, and high-risk PDR has important treatment implications that balance disease control against the risk of iatrogenic visual field defects[1][5][8]. Early proliferative retinopathy, characterized by new vessel formation that does not yet meet criteria for high-risk status, has a substantially different natural history. The ETDRS trial established that early PRP was not superior to deferred PRP in preventing severe vision loss in patients with early-proliferative disease without high-risk characteristics, and the study recommended against treating patients with early-proliferative retinopathy or lesser levels of disease due to the side effects of laser therapy itself[5][8]. Specifically, at two years, 3% of treated eyes and 7% of untreated eyes with early proliferative retinopathy developed severe vision loss, and at four years this diverged to 7% treated versus 21% untreated[5][8]. However, the relatively modest benefit in early-proliferative disease did not justify routine treatment given the known risks of PRP, leading the ETDRS group to advise that scatter photocoagulation should not be initiated in mild cases of NPDR to balance potential adverse effects and minimal benefits gained by treatment at early stages[5][8].

Severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy, defined by the 4-2-1 rule (four quadrants of retinal hemorrhages and microaneurysms, two or more quadrants of venous beading, or one or more quadrants of intraretinal microvascular abnormalities), represents an intermediate situation where PRP can be justified based on the high risk of progression to high-risk PDR[5][8][9][19]. The presence of severe NPDR indicates that the retina has developed substantial ischemic burden and carries greater than 50% risk of progression to PDR within one year, with risk exceeding 75% for very severe NPDR[19]. In these patients, PRP can be employed as a preventive measure, though the risk-benefit calculation must still be carefully considered. Very severe NPDR with specific additional findings may warrant early laser treatment in select situations, such as when a patient's contralateral eye had a poor outcome from untreated PDR, or when follow-up compliance is expected to be difficult[1][32][37].

The application of PRP in special clinical circumstances demonstrates how treatment indications evolve based on disease complications and patient-specific factors. In eyes with vitreous hemorrhage secondary to PDR, laser photocoagulation plays a unique role that differs from its application in uncomplicated proliferative disease[1][32][37]. Vitreous hemorrhage itself does not impair the view sufficiently to prevent laser application in all cases, and PRP can help prevent further vitreous hemorrhage by halting the proliferation of fibrovascular tissue and preventing the progression of tractional retinal detachment[1][32][37]. The Protocol N study of the DRCR.net showed that in eyes with PDR and vitreous hemorrhage, there was no clinically significant advantage of ranibizumab over saline in reducing the need for vitrectomy at 16 weeks, suggesting that early laser application remains a viable approach when the view permits[1][32]. Pars plana vitrectomy with endolaser remains the definitive procedure for non-clearing vitreous hemorrhage, particularly when substantial neovascularization or retinal detachment is present[36][42].

## Indications for Focal and Grid Laser in Diabetic Macular Edema

The treatment of diabetic macular edema with focal and grid laser photocoagulation represents one of the most important clinical applications of laser technology, though the precise indications have evolved substantially with the emergence of anti-VEGF therapy[1][3][6][14][20][42]. The ETDRS established focal and grid laser photocoagulation as the foundational treatment for clinically significant macular edema, demonstrating a 50% reduction in moderate vision loss when compared to observation[3][6][7][14]. Clinically significant macular edema is formally defined as retinal thickening within one disc diameter of the fovea with thickness exceeding 250 micrometers, hard exudates within one disc diameter of the fovea with adjacent retinal thickening, or a zone of retinal thickening one disc diameter or larger, any part of which is within one disc diameter of the fovea[3][6]. The ETDRS focal/grid laser technique, which remains the most extensively validated approach, involves careful identification of focal leaking sites, particularly microaneurysms, which are directly ablated with light-intensity, small-spot photocoagulation, followed by a more extensive grid pattern of mild laser burns in areas of diffuse leakage[3][6][7][38][41].

Non-center-involving clinically significant macular edema represents one of the clearest contemporary indications for laser as first-line therapy[1][6][32][37][40]. Focal laser photocoagulation targeting microaneurysms located between 500 and 3,000 micrometers from the fovea remains highly effective for this phenotype of macular edema[1][6][40]. The ETDRS demonstrated that focal laser photocoagulation is particularly effective when macular edema exhibits hard exudates clustered in a ring or circinate pattern around leaking microaneurysms, as these spatially defined lesions are amenable to precise focal treatment[6]. More recent analyses have confirmed that focal laser treatment can greatly benefit non-center-involving focal macular edema with this characteristic pattern[6][40]. Importantly, the long-term follow-up data from the ETDRS demonstrated that, in patients with noncenter-involving clinically significant macular edema treated with focal laser, the macular edema did not progress to involve the foveal center as frequently as it did in untreated eyes, suggesting that early focal laser may prevent subsequent vision-threatening center involvement[3][40]. This provides a strong rationale for treating non-center-involving disease before it encroaches on the fovea and causes irreversible photoreceptor damage[3][40].

Center-involving diabetic macular edema with good baseline visual acuity represents a situation where the risk-benefit calculation for laser treatment has shifted substantially in recent years[1][27][32][44][47]. Patients with center-involving DME and excellent visual acuity at baseline, defined as 20/25 or better, can be considered for first-line laser treatment using only subthreshold micropulse laser technology when central retinal thickness is 350 micrometers or less, and only when combined with excellent glycemic control with HbA1C of 7% or less[1][32][37][46][47]. The DRCR.net Protocol V trial provided transformative evidence by demonstrating that observation alone, with deferred anti-VEGF treatment only if visual acuity declined, yielded comparable two-year visual acuity outcomes to immediate anti-VEGF treatment in patients with center-involving DME and baseline visual acuity of 20/25 or better[27][44][47][53]. This landmark finding changed clinical practice by supporting a "watchful wait" approach in this favorable-prognosis population, with subsequent cost analysis revealing that initial observation could result in savings of approximately $10.33 billion in a 10-year analysis compared to immediate aflibercept treatment[27][44].

Contemporary guidelines recommend laser treatment as second-line or adjuvant therapy for center-involving diabetic macular edema when intravitreal anti-VEGF or corticosteroid therapy has proven insufficient[1][32][37][46][47]. Specifically, laser can be considered for macular edema not fully responding to anti-VEGF therapy after four to six injections of anti-VEGF agents or two to three injections of intravitreal steroids[1][32][37][46][47]. Additionally, laser can be used as adjuvant therapy to intravitreal injections whenever retinal thickness is less than 400 micrometers, with the goal of reducing the number of intravitreal injections required and delaying the interval until DME recurrence[1][32][37][46][47]. The rationale for this adjuvant approach is supported by the finding that approximately 50% of patients with center-involving DME require additional laser treatment even after intensive anti-VEGF therapy under research trial conditions[32][37]. Some evidence suggests that combining focal/grid laser photocoagulation with anti-VEGF therapy may reduce the total number of anti-VEGF injections needed over a treatment period compared to anti-VEGF monotherapy, potentially providing synergistic benefit[6][32].

## Visual Field Defects and Other Complications of Laser Treatment

The adverse effects of laser photocoagulation, particularly visual field defects and impaired night vision, represent the primary factors that temper enthusiasm for laser treatment and necessitate careful consideration of indications and timing[4][7][10][23][35]. Conventional continuous-wave laser photocoagulation is destructive to retinal cells and can result in scotomas—visual field defects that patients perceive as spots or areas of missing vision—which may become enlarged over time through a process termed laser creep[10][23][29]. The mechanism of visual field loss from PRP involves the deliberate destruction of peripheral retina to reduce the metabolic demands of the eye, and consequently the affected retina cannot contribute to visual function in the periphery[4][35]. The extensive treatment required in PRP necessarily impacts a large area of peripheral retina, making some degree of peripheral vision loss almost inevitable[4][35]. In historical analyses from the DRS and ETDRS, comparing visual fields in untreated eyes with those treated with PRP, the difference in visual field scores between PRP-treated and untreated eyes was significant at four months after treatment, but this difference became no longer apparent by year three of follow-up[35]. This suggests that the initial visual field loss is acute but that natural disease progression in untreated eyes eventually results in similar visual field deficit magnitudes[35].

Night vision impairment and decreased contrast sensitivity represent additional important adverse effects of PRP that substantially impact patients' quality of life[4][10][23][35]. Many patients receiving PRP report difficulty with night driving, decreased ability to adjust to dim or bright lighting, and impaired distance judgment[4][7][10][23][35]. These complications occur because the laser-treated peripheral retina, which contains a higher proportion of rod photoreceptors important for low-light vision, becomes nonfunctional[4][23][29]. Acute intraocular pressure elevation can occur within hours after laser photocoagulation and represents another potential complication, though this is usually transient[7]. More concerning are the rare but serious complications including retinal fibrosis, choroidal detachments, cystoid macular edema, macular pucker, and vitreous bleeding from neovascularization recurrence[7][20][23].

The risk of laser treatment worsening or precipitating diabetic macular edema represents a paradoxical but well-documented complication that requires particular clinical attention[7][10][20][23][32]. Studies have reported that due to the high intensity of treatment received in a single session during PRP, there can be an increased risk of complications including macular edema[7][20]. This has led to the modern recommendation that PRP be divided into multiple sessions—optimally two sessions separated by approximately two weeks—rather than attempting complete scatter photocoagulation in a single sitting[32][46]. By dividing treatment, ophthalmologists can reduce the intensity of treatment received at any single session and thereby minimize the risk of treatment-induced macular edema while still achieving complete peripheral ablation[32][46]. The distinction between focal/grid argon laser therapy can leave small scotomas that patients perceive as spots, and these scotomas can become enlarged over time due to laser creep, potentially causing long-term vision impairment if foveal regions are inadvertently treated[10][23][29]. The foveal avascular zone must be carefully spared, maintaining a minimum margin of at least 500 micrometers from the treatment laser burns[46].

## The Evolution Toward Anti-VEGF Therapy and Changing Indications for Laser Treatment

The introduction of intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy, beginning with ranibizumab (Lucentis) and followed by aflibercept (Eylea) and bevacizumab (Avastin), fundamentally altered the treatment landscape for diabetic retinopathy and changed the indications and timing of laser treatment[1][5][10][18][31][32]. The DRCR.net Protocol I trial, published in 2010, demonstrated that intravitreal ranibizumab combined with prompt or deferred focal/grid laser was superior to laser alone or intravitreal triamcinolone plus laser for treating center-involving diabetic macular edema[1][29][31]. The ranibizumab-treated eyes experienced greater improvements in visual acuity and greater reductions in central macular thickness compared to laser monotherapy[1][29][31]. This landmark finding established anti-VEGF as the first-line therapy for center-involving DME in many treatment paradigms, relegating laser to a second-line or adjuvant role[1][10][32][37][44].

The landmark DRCR.net Protocol S study, published in 2015, created a significant paradigm shift in the treatment of proliferative diabetic retinopathy by demonstrating that intravitreal ranibizumab (0.5 mg given monthly) was noninferior to panretinal photocoagulation in terms of visual acuity outcomes in patients with PDR without center-involved DME[1][18][25][31][37]. The Protocol S trial randomized 305 adults with PDR into a ranibizumab group (191 eyes) and a PRP group (203 eyes), with the ranibizumab group also receiving PRP if treatment failed and ranibizumab as needed for concomitant DME[1][25][31]. The ranibizumab group demonstrated mean visual acuity improvement of +2.8 letters at two years compared to only +0.2 letters in the PRP group, suggesting not only noninferior outcomes but potentially superior visual outcomes with anti-VEGF as first-line therapy[1][25][31]. Five-year results of Protocol S showed that severe vision loss and PDR complications remained rare in both treatment groups, but the ranibizumab group had lower rates of developing center-involving diabetic macular edema and significantly less visual field loss compared to the PRP group[25]. This evidence caused many ophthalmologists to prefer anti-VEGF injections over laser, and laser has become increasingly relegated to a second-line therapy for proliferative disease in many practice settings[1][10][32][37].

However, anti-VEGF therapy is not without significant limitations that preserve an important role for laser treatment despite its known complications[1][10][32][37]. Anti-VEGF agents are short-acting medications, requiring frequent intravitreous injections, typically administered monthly or bimonthly in clinical trial settings but often administered with longer intervals in real-world practice[1][10][32][37][51][56]. The median injection frequency for DME in clinical practice ranges from 2.2 to 4.4 injections per year, substantially less than the 9-12 injections per year in pivotal trials, and this reduction in treatment frequency has been associated with worse visual outcomes in some studies[51][56]. Anti-VEGF therapy carries the risks of endophthalmitis and thromboembolic stroke, which do not occur with laser photocoagulation[1][32][37]. The costs associated with anti-VEGF therapy are substantial, with individual injections costing over $1,000 compared to approximately $50 for bevacizumab[10][29][55]. The treatment burden on patients is considerable, requiring frequent office visits and repeated intravitreal injections, which leads to patient dissatisfaction, poor compliance, and ultimately treatment failure in a substantial proportion of patients[1][10][32][37][51][59][60].

Laser treatment remains superior to anti-VEGF therapy when patient compliance and follow-up are expected to be problematic[1][29][32][37][46][47]. In particular, PRP has fundamentally different durability characteristics compared to anti-VEGF therapy—while anti-VEGF requires continuous treatment to maintain effect, PRP generally provides long-lasting suppression of neovascularization after a completed treatment course[1][31][32][37]. Patients with anticipated difficulty with follow-up, including pregnant women with PDR, those with documented poor glycemic control, those with significant socioeconomic barriers to care, or those in geographic regions with limited access to frequent ophthalmology visits, remain excellent candidates for laser as first-line therapy[1][32][37][47][50][53]. In patients who are unlikely to return for regular follow-up appointments, anti-VEGF therapy alone should not be the treatment of choice, and PRP should be administered instead[31]. The fundamental limitation of anti-VEGF therapy for PDR is the loss of effect after treatment discontinuation—when anti-VEGF therapy is stopped, neovascularization tends to recur, whereas the effects of PRP persist[31][32].

## Contemporary Indications and Patient Selection: Individualizing the Risk-Benefit Calculation

Modern laser therapy for diabetic retinopathy must be individualized based on careful assessment of disease severity, baseline visual function, glycemic control, treatment compliance, and patient-specific factors[1][32][37][46][47]. For proliferative diabetic retinopathy, the presence of high-risk characteristics—including NVD affecting more than one-quarter to one-third of the disc area, any NVD with vitreous or preretinal hemorrhage, or any NVE with vitreous or preretinal hemorrhage—clearly indicates the need for prompt treatment with either PRP or anti-VEGF therapy[1][5][13][16][31][32][37]. In eyes with high-risk PDR but no concomitant center-involving DME, PRP remains the mainstay treatment, though anti-VEGF can be used as an alternative if compliance is expected to be excellent[1][32][37][46]. When PDR is associated with non-center-involving clinically significant macular edema, focal laser may be used in addition to PRP to specifically target the leaking microaneurysms responsible for the edema[1][32]. However, if the PDR is associated with center-involved DME, then intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy is recommended until both neovascularization and macular edema have resolved, after which PRP can be applied if there is evidence of poor glycemic control, poor compliance, or anticipated difficulty with follow-up[1][32][37].

For patients with severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy but without proliferative features, more recent evidence supports a selective approach to laser treatment rather than routine treatment of all patients at this stage[1][5][15][25][29]. The natural history of severe NPDR indicates that there is greater than 50% risk of progression to high-risk PDR within one year, making close observation essential[9][19][29]. The ETDRS group recommended against routine laser treatment in early-proliferative disease due to the side effects of treatment, and this principle extends to severe NPDR—treatment should be considered on a case-by-case basis depending on patient factors including age, comorbidities, glycemic control, and reliability of follow-up[5][8]. Early treatment with anti-VEGF agents for severe NPDR without DME has emerged as an alternative approach supported by randomized evidence, with the PANORAMA trial demonstrating that aflibercept treatment in patients with moderately severe to severe NPDR without center-involved DME resulted in 2-step or greater improvement on the Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale in more eyes compared to sham injections[15][25].

Diabetic macular edema treatment indications have undergone significant evolution following the recognition that many patients with excellent baseline visual acuity do not benefit from immediate treatment[1][27][44][47]. According to DRCR.net Protocol V, patients with center-involving DME and visual acuity of 20/25 or better can be safely observed with initiation of treatment (either anti-VEGF or laser) only if visual acuity declines[27][44][47][53]. This represents a major shift from earlier practice patterns and reflects the understanding that many patients remain stable without treatment and that early treatment of asymptomatic patients does not improve long-term visual outcomes[27][44][47]. For patients with center-involving DME and visual acuity worse than 20/40, treatment is indicated, and anti-VEGF therapy is typically first-line, though laser (particularly subthreshold micropulse laser) or intravitreal steroids can be considered in selected cases[1][32][44][47]. For non-center-involving clinically significant macular edema, focal laser photocoagulation remains an excellent first-line choice, particularly when the pattern of edema includes focal microaneurysm leakage with surrounding hard exudates[1][6][32][40][47].

Recent advances in laser technology have refined the risk-benefit calculation by enabling more precise, less destructive laser applications while maintaining therapeutic efficacy[1][7][20][23][32]. Subthreshold diode micropulse laser (SDM) delivers laser energy below the threshold required for permanent tissue destruction, selectively targeting the retinal pigment epithelium without causing collateral damage to overlying photoreceptors and neurosensory retina[1][7][20][23][32][43][46]. The micropulse delivery involves brief pulses of laser energy alternating with off-cycles that allow tissues to cool, thereby avoiding cumulative thermal injury[23][46]. Studies have demonstrated that subthreshold micropulse laser is equally effective as conventional continuous-wave laser for treating diabetic macular edema, with significantly fewer adverse effects[1][7][20][23][32][43]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed similar visual acuity outcomes and central macular thickness reductions after treatment with subthreshold diode micropulse laser compared to conventional laser photocoagulation[6][17]. The DIAMONDS trial, a pragmatic randomized controlled trial involving 266 patients in the United Kingdom, demonstrated that subthreshold micropulse laser was clinically equivalent to standard threshold macular laser in terms of visual acuity improvement, macular thickness reduction, and quality of life, though it required a slightly higher number of laser treatment sessions[43].

Pattern scanning laser systems and navigated laser systems with multimodal imaging guidance represent additional technological advances that improve precision and reduce complications[1][7][17][20][29][46]. The NAVILAS system, for example, uses real-time fundus imaging and automated targeting to deliver pattern-based PRP to areas of retinal ischemia identified by widefield fluorescein angiography[17][29][46]. This allows for fewer, more uniform laser burns delivered in shorter treatment time with less discomfort and potentially less overall retinal tissue damage[29][46]. Recent uncontrolled studies have demonstrated that navigated laser guidance results in fewer anti-VEGF injections required to maintain remission of macular edema compared to conventional techniques[17][29]. Sub-threshold retinal therapy (SRT) utilizes pulsed laser delivery to achieve subthreshold therapeutic effects without visible retinal burn, preserving the underlying retinal architecture while achieving therapeutic benefit[7][20]. The SRT protocol has been validated as secure and efficacious for treating diabetic macular edema with multiple optional retreatments and fewer adverse effects compared to conventional laser, including preservation of vision, absence of scotomas, reduced discomfort, and maintenance of the nerve fiber layer, cone and rod cells, and choroid[7][20].

## Glycemic Control and Patient Factors in Treatment Decision-Making

The importance of optimal glycemic control in determining when laser treatment is indicated cannot be overstated, as both the progression of diabetic retinopathy and the response to treatment are fundamentally dependent on systemic metabolic control[1][3][32][37][42][46][47]. The UKPDS trial demonstrated that each 1% reduction in updated mean HbA1c was associated with a 21% reduction in risk for any diabetes-related endpoint[31]. The recommendation that laser therapy for DME be combined with excellent glycemic control (HbA1C of 7% or less) reflects the understanding that without metabolic optimization, laser treatment outcomes are suboptimal and disease progression may continue despite local retinal treatment[1][32][46]. Conversely, rapid improvement in glycemic control, such as that achieved with intensive glucose-lowering therapy in the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial, can be associated with early worsening of retinopathy, though the long-term benefits substantially outweigh the short-term risks[50][53]. During pregnancy, the risk of retinopathy worsening is increased nearly twofold in women with suboptimal glycemic control at conception, underscoring the importance of achieving excellent metabolic control before conception and maintaining it throughout gestation[50][53]. These physiological principles inform the modern recommendation that laser treatment be reserved for situations where systemic diabetes management is reasonably optimized or when the laser treatment is addressing acute complications (such as high-risk PDR) that justify treatment despite suboptimal systemic control.

Patient compliance and the ability to maintain regular follow-up represent critical factors in determining whether laser or anti-VEGF therapy should be preferred[1][32][37][39][51][54][57][60]. Analysis of reasons for noncompliance with laser treatment in patients with diabetic retinopathy has identified multiple barriers to treatment completion, with only about 55% of patients who begin laser treatment completing the full recommended course[39]. These barriers include lack of awareness about the disease, belief that treatment is unnecessary due to good remaining vision, direct and indirect costs, distance from treatment centers, discomfort from dilating drops, effort to attend multiple clinic visits, and fear of laser treatment and its effects on quality of life[39][57]. Similarly, loss to follow-up from anti-VEGF therapy is substantial, with increasing age and socioeconomic disadvantage representing significant risk factors[51][60]. In a study examining proliferative diabetic retinopathy patients treated with PRP and/or anti-VEGF injections, 16.3% were lost to follow-up, and loss to follow-up was significantly correlated with baseline visual acuity loss and patient's lack of trust and satisfaction with treatment[60]. These findings underscore the importance of counseling patients about the importance of completing treatment and maintaining follow-up, and of considering patient-specific factors when choosing between laser and anti-VEGF modalities.

## Combination Therapy: Integrating Laser and Anti-VEGF Approaches

Recent years have witnessed growing interest in combination therapies that integrate laser photocoagulation with anti-VEGF or steroid agents, based on the hypothesis that such combinations may provide superior outcomes, reduced treatment burden, or better durability compared to monotherapy approaches[1][6][23][32][29][37][46]. The rationale for combination therapy is compelling: anti-VEGF agents rapidly suppress neovascularization but have limited durability when treatment is discontinued, while laser provides permanent structural changes to the retina but has slower onset and may not be optimal for acute complications. Multiple clinical trials have examined whether combination therapy provides synergistic benefit, though results have been mixed and somewhat inconsistent[1][6][23][32][37][46][47]. The DRCR.net Protocol I demonstrated no short-term benefit in combining prompt macular laser with anti-VEGF therapy for center-involved DME, suggesting that when using anti-VEGF as the primary modality, immediate laser addition did not accelerate visual recovery[23][37][47]. However, subsequent analysis revealed that at one-year follow-up, 37-56% of center-involved DME eyes receiving intensive anti-VEGF therapy under research trial conditions still required focal/grid laser photocoagulation, indicating that laser maintains an important role in rescue therapy for inadequate anti-VEGF response[6][32][37][47].

Studies investigating specific scenarios for combination therapy have provided more nuanced guidance about when combining modalities may be beneficial[1][6][23][32][37][46]. Some evidence suggests that supplementing anti-VEGF monotherapy with laser therapy may produce a more durable response, potentially reducing the total number of anti-VEGF injections required over a treatment period[1][6][32][37]. In clinical practice, many ophthalmologists employ focal/grid laser therapy as an adjunct to anti-VEGF therapy specifically targeting focal microaneurysm leakage with surrounding hard exudates, in the hope that this targeted approach will reduce the overall anti-VEGF treatment burden[6][32][46]. For patients with focal parafoveal macular edema, the combination of anti-VEGF and focal laser targeting the specific microaneurysm leakage points may provide particularly good outcomes[6][40]. The real-world study from the International Retina Group (OBTAIN study) supported these findings, demonstrating that macular laser treatment was as effective as anti-VEGF injections in patients with retinal thickness less than 300 micrometers, and that combining both modalities may optimize outcomes in selected cases[40].

For proliferative diabetic retinopathy, the role of combination therapy has evolved as evidence about anti-VEGF monotherapy has accumulated[1][10][25][29][32][37][46]. Some studies have suggested that combination treatment with anti-VEGF and PRP may reduce treatment burden compared to anti-VEGF monotherapy alone in patients who are highly compliant, though other studies have shown no significant benefit of combination treatment[1][29][37][46][47]. The evidence generally supports a more selective approach: anti-VEGF therapy alone can be used as first-line treatment in patients with excellent expected compliance and access to frequent follow-up, while laser (PRP) should be considered when compliance is questionable or when the long-term durability of PRP is advantageous[1][29][31][32][37]. For high-risk PDR, the combination of anti-VEGF and PRP may be considered in certain situations, such as when there is active neovascularization with vitreous hemorrhage and the need to expedite clearing of hemorrhage (to allow safer laser application) while simultaneously reducing the neovascular stimulus[1][32][37]. Recent innovations in treatment delivery, such as navigated laser systems combined with anti-VEGF therapy, have shown promise in reducing the overall treatment burden while maintaining clinical efficacy[17][29][46].

## Special Considerations: Pregnancy, Type 1 Versus Type 2 Diabetes, and Advanced Disease

Pregnancy in women with diabetes creates unique circumstances where laser treatment indications and safety profiles must be carefully reconsidered in light of fetal safety concerns[50][53]. Pregnancy itself is a risk factor for retinopathy progression, with physiological changes including metabolic, vascular, immunologic, and hormonal alterations that can worsen existing diabetic retinopathy[50][53]. Approximately 55% of pregnant women with moderate to severe NPDR experienced worsening of retinopathy during pregnancy compared to only 21% of those with mild NPDR at conception[50][53]. Despite these risks, laser treatment remains the preferred modality in pregnant women with sight-threatening diabetic retinopathy, as anti-VEGF agents have not been definitively proven safe in pregnancy due to the lack of large randomized trials and concerns regarding potential fetal effects[50][53]. Panretinal photocoagulation is a safe treatment option in pregnant patients and remains the mainstay for preventing disease progression in pregnant women with PDR or severe NPDR[50][53]. Focal laser for clinically significant macular edema in pregnancy can be considered, particularly in the second or third trimester when risks to the fetus are minimized[50][53]. Current recommendations from the American Diabetes Association support laser treatment when needed at earlier stages of disease in pregnant patients than would typically be treated in nonpregnant patients[50][53].

The distinction between type 1 and type 2 diabetes influences both the natural history of diabetic retinopathy and the response to various treatments, which has implications for laser treatment indications[36][50][55]. Type 1 diabetic patients with vitreous hemorrhage appear to benefit more from early vitrectomy than type 2 diabetic patients, with improved visual outcomes demonstrated at both two- and four-year follow-up[36][55]. When considering PRP for vitreous hemorrhage, type 1 diabetic patients should be considered for earlier intervention, while in type 2 diabetic patients, a period of observation (approximately 3-4 months) to allow spontaneous hemorrhage clearance may be reasonable given that the majority of type 2 diabetic patients eventually require vitrectomy if hemorrhage persists beyond this period[36][55]. These different natural histories may also have implications for the timing of laser treatment in other contexts, though specific guidance for type-specific laser treatment algorithms is limited in the literature.

Advanced diabetic eye disease, including tractional retinal detachment and combined tractional-rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, represents a situation where laser's role is fundamentally altered from its application in earlier disease stages[33][36][42]. In these advanced scenarios, vitrectomy with endolaser represents the definitive surgical intervention, as the structural remodeling of the retina by proliferative scar tissue cannot be adequately addressed by external laser alone[33][36][42]. Pre-operative laser may be considered in select cases to promote regression of neovascularization and provide more stability during membrane dissection and segmentation[33]. The unique challenge with advanced disease is the risk of the "crunch phenomenon," where regression of neovascularization after laser or anti-VEGF therapy can paradoxically worsen tractional detachment by allowing fibrotic membranes to contract more effectively[33]. This counterintuitive complication highlights the complexity of treating advanced diabetic eye disease and underscores the importance of specialized vitreoretinal surgical expertise in managing these cases.

## Emerging Therapies and Future Directions in Diabetic Retinopathy Treatment

The landscape of diabetic retinopathy treatment continues to evolve with the development of novel therapeutic approaches that may eventually reshape the indications for laser treatment[51][52][56]. Susvimo, an intravitreal port delivery system (PDS) that provides sustained anti-VEGF therapy, has recently received FDA approval for diabetic macular edema and diabetic retinopathy based on results from the PAGODA and PAVILION trials[51][52][56]. The PAGODA trial demonstrated that Susvimo (requiring refill exchange every six months) was noninferior to monthly anti-VEGF injections for treating DME, with no cases of endophthalmitis or retinal detachment reported[51][56]. In the PAVILION trial for diabetic retinopathy, 80% of patients in the Susvimo arm achieved at least a two-step improvement on the Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale compared to only 9% in the control group, with rates of DME and PDR also significantly lower in the treatment group[51][56]. Such long-acting delivery systems, by reducing the injection burden from monthly to semi-annual refill exchanges, may improve patient compliance and outcomes, potentially reducing the relative advantage of laser therapy when treatment burden is a consideration[51][56].

Gene therapy approaches currently under investigation hold the promise of one-time or infrequent administration of anti-angiogenic therapy, potentially providing durable disease control without the need for repeated injections or laser treatment[51][56]. ABBV-RGX-314, a suprachoroidal gene therapy delivering aflibercept transgene expression, demonstrated in bilateral dosing studies that 78% of patients were injection-free at nine months and achieved 97% reduction in overall treatment burden[51][56]. Multiple other gene therapy approaches are in Phase II and Phase III trials, including 4D-150 which combines dual-transgene expression for more comprehensive VEGF inhibition, and early results have shown 70-89% of patients remaining injection-free through 52 weeks[51][56]. While these approaches show tremendous promise, they remain investigational and long-term safety data continue to accumulate. If successfully approved and widely adopted, such durable therapies might reduce the relative advantages of laser treatment for patients who can afford or access gene therapy, though laser would likely retain value in populations with limited access to these novel modalities.

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting VEGF and related angiogenic pathways represent another promising treatment class in development for diabetic retinopathy[51][56]. Vorolanib (EYP-1901, from EyePoint Pharmaceuticals) demonstrated in the Phase II DAVIO2 trial that approximately 44% of patients remained injection-free through 14 months following suprachoroidal administration[51][56]. Axitinib (CLS-AX, from Clearside Biomedical) showed in the Phase IIb ODYSSEY trial an 84% reduction in injection burden with 67% of patients requiring no supplemental therapy out to six months[51][56]. The advantage of TKIs is their oral or intravitreal administration options, potentially providing flexibility in dosing and administration routes. These emerging therapies are unlikely to eliminate the need for laser treatment entirely, as different patients and treatment scenarios will likely benefit from different modalities depending on disease severity, patient compliance, access to care, and economic factors.

## Conclusion

The indications for laser photocoagulation in diabetic retinopathy remain firmly grounded in decades of rigorous clinical evidence from landmark trials, though contemporary practice has evolved substantially with the emergence of anti-VEGF therapy and continues to evolve with novel treatment approaches under investigation. For proliferative diabetic retinopathy with high-risk characteristics, panretinal photocoagulation remains the gold standard treatment, providing durable disease control and substantial reduction in severe vision loss risk, though anti-VEGF therapy is now an accepted alternative when patient compliance is expected to be excellent. For diabetic macular edema, laser treatment—particularly using modern subthreshold micropulse or navigated approaches—remains highly effective for non-center-involving disease and for center-involving disease in carefully selected patients with good baseline vision, though anti-VEGF therapy has become first-line for many patients with vision-threatening disease. The careful balance between the prevention of disease progression and the documented risks of visual field defects, reduced night vision, and scotomas requires individualized patient assessment considering disease severity, baseline visual function, systemic metabolic control, expected compliance with treatment and follow-up, and patient-specific factors.

The most contemporary evidence supports a nuanced, individualized approach that moves beyond rigid protocol adherence and instead tailors treatment selection to patient circumstances. Patients with severe nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy without current vision threat may be observed closely without immediate treatment in many cases, particularly those with good glycemic control and reliable follow-up. Those with center-involving diabetic macular edema and excellent baseline visual acuity can often be safely observed, with treatment deferred until visual decline occurs. Patients with predicted poor compliance or limited access to frequent follow-up remain better served by laser treatment despite its inherent complications, given the durability of laser effects compared to short-acting anti-VEGF therapy. Modern laser technologies including subthreshold micropulse laser, pattern scanning systems, and navigated laser platforms substantially reduce the adverse effects of conventional continuous-wave laser while maintaining therapeutic efficacy, shifting the risk-benefit calculation toward more favorable outcomes. Combination therapy approaches integrating laser with anti-VEGF agents may optimize outcomes in selected scenarios, particularly when targeting specific anatomical patterns of leakage or attempting to reduce overall treatment burden.

Future developments in sustained-delivery anti-VEGF systems, gene therapy, and oral tyrosine kinase inhibitors will continue to reshape treatment paradigms and potentially reduce the relative role of laser photocoagulation, particularly in developed healthcare systems with robust patient access to these modalities. However, laser treatment will remain an essential tool, particularly in underserved populations with limited access to frequent intravitreous injections or advanced systemic therapies. The fundamental principles guiding laser treatment decision-making—early detection through screening, individualizing treatment based on disease severity and patient factors, ensuring optimal systemic metabolic control, and maintaining patient engagement through education and support—remain as important today as they were when laser treatment first transformed the prognosis of diabetic retinopathy nearly five decades ago[1][3][5][8][10].
