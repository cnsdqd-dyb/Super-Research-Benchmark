# Stress-Strain Relationships in Ice Flow Models and Their Constraints on Sea Level Rise Predictions

The relationship between stress and strain rate in glacial ice remains one of the most critical yet uncertain parameters in predicting future sea level rise from the Antarctic and Greenland Ice Sheets. Recent discoveries that ice flow is significantly more sensitive to applied stress than previously modeled have profound implications for our ability to project ice sheet discharge and resulting coastal flooding. This comprehensive examination explores how the constitutive relationships governing ice deformation, particularly the celebrated Glen flow law and alternative rheological formulations, directly constrain projections of sea level rise when combined with our evolving understanding of basal sliding mechanics. The complexity arises because ice does not behave as a simple viscous fluid, multiple deformation mechanisms operate simultaneously under different stress and temperature regimes, and the basal interface where ice meets bedrock exhibits highly nonlinear sliding behavior that remains incompletely characterized. Understanding how modifications to stress-strain relationships translate through ice sheet models into quantified uncertainties in sea level projections represents a critical frontier in glaciological science, with implications that extend from near-term coastal planning to century-scale climate adaptation strategies.

## The Evolution of Viscous Flow Laws for Ice

### Historical Development and Glen's Flow Law

The foundation of modern ice sheet modeling rests upon empirical relationships between stress and strain rate established through laboratory experiments on polycrystalline ice. In 1958, British glaciologist John Wesley Glen developed what would become the most widely used equation for describing viscous ice deformation, a power-law relationship known as Glen's flow law.[1] This equation, originally calibrated from laboratory experiments conducted under relatively limited conditions, relates strain rate to applied stress through a nonlinear power law with a stress exponent of approximately three.[3] For decades, this relationship provided the standard for understanding ice flow across the entire range of conditions found in natural glaciers and ice sheets, from the interiors of massive continental ice sheets to the rapidly flowing ice streams and shelf regions that discharge ice to the ocean.

The long historical reliance on Glen's law reflects both the practical utility of the equation and the substantial barriers to empirical validation in natural glacier settings. With only a handful of glaciologists working in the field during the mid-twentieth century and the extreme remoteness of most large ice sheets, few opportunities existed to calibrate Glen's law against field observations in anything beyond isolated locations.[1] Laboratory experiments provided the primary source of constraint, yet these experiments were conducted at strain rates and stresses that differed substantially from those expected in natural ice masses. The recognition that Glen's law might require revision did not emerge until satellite technology enabled systematic observation of ice sheet dynamics across vast spatial scales, providing the observational foundation necessary to evaluate whether laboratory-calibrated relationships truly captured the physics of natural glacier deformation.

### Recent Revisions Through Satellite Data Analysis

A landmark study by researchers at MIT fundamentally challenged the assumed stress exponent in Glen's law by leveraging unprecedented satellite imagery coverage over Antarctic ice shelves.[1][4] Rather than conducting additional laboratory experiments, these scientists took advantage of the explosion in satellite data availability since the early 2000s, when the Larsen B Ice Shelf collapse in 2002 was documented by only two satellite images separated by a month. Contemporary satellite systems now provide imagery over the same region every six days, enabling precise measurements of ice velocity changes in response to stress variations.[1][4] This new wealth of observational data revealed that **ice flow in the most dynamic regions of Antarctica, particularly the ice shelves that buttress the interior ice sheet, is more sensitive to stress changes than the standard Glen's law formulation predicts.**[1][4]

The implications of this increased stress sensitivity extend far beyond academic refinement of existing equations. If ice accelerates more readily in response to modest increases in driving stress, then the threshold for triggering rapid ice discharge becomes lower, and transient changes in ice shelf buttressing or grounding line position could produce larger velocity responses than current models suggest.[1][4] The MIT researchers emphasized that while this revision to the stress exponent improves our ability to model ice flow, the change is unlikely to raise maximum estimates of sea level rise beyond levels already incorporated into climate models, as the equation modification acts as a constraint on uncertainty rather than a wholesale change in projections.[1][4]

## Stress-Strain Relationships and Deformation Mechanisms

### Nonlinear Rheology and Temperature Dependence

Ice behaves as a highly nonlinear fluid whose viscosity depends not only on the applied stress but also on temperature, crystal orientation, grain size, and the presence of impurities and water.[1][4][5] The power-law form of Glen's equation captures this nonlinearity through the stress exponent, yet this simple relationship masks profound complexity in the underlying deformation mechanisms. The most widely accepted value of the stress exponent, \(n = 3\), emerged from laboratory experiments conducted at relatively high stresses compared to those found in most of the Antarctic and Greenland Ice Sheets.[16] When extrapolated to the lower stresses characteristic of ice sheet interiors, different deformation mechanisms dominate, and the simple power law begins to fail.

Recent laboratory work has identified multiple creep mechanisms operating in polycrystalline ice, each with distinct stress dependencies and grain size sensitivities.[3][16] Dislocation creep, characterized by movement of oxygen atoms within the crystal lattice through self-diffusion, exhibits a stress exponent near four and remains insensitive to grain size.[16] Grain boundary sliding (GBS), by contrast, displays a stress exponent near two and exhibits strong grain size dependence, with smaller grains deforming more readily.[3][16] As natural ice undergoes strain and dynamic recrystallization, grain size evolves, causing the effective creep mechanism to transition between dislocation-dominated and GBS-dominated regimes.[3][16] This grain size evolution explains why laboratory-calibrated flow laws, which typically preserve grain size through the limited strain (approximately one percent) achieved before reaching secondary creep, may not accurately represent the deformation of ice that has accumulated large strains in natural settings.

A comprehensive reassessment of ice flow laws based on seventy years of laboratory data revealed that none of the single-mechanism flow laws used in modern ice sheet models fit the experimental observations particularly well across the full range of stresses and temperatures of interest.[16] Instead, composite flow laws that explicitly represent multiple deformation mechanisms and their temperature and grain-size dependencies provide substantially better fits to laboratory data and predict strain rates substantially different from those obtained from the standard Glen's law when extrapolated to natural ice sheet conditions.[16] At typical glaciological stresses of less than 0.1 megapascals (MPa), multicomponent flow laws predict strain rates an order of magnitude faster than those from single-component Glen's law formulations.[16] This dramatic divergence highlights how the choice of stress-strain relationship fundamentally constrains predictions of ice discharge and, by extension, sea level rise.

### Temperate Ice and Linear Viscosity

An unexpected discovery emerged from large-scale laboratory deformation experiments on temperate ice—ice at its pressure-melting temperature containing liquid water at grain boundaries.[9] Such temperate ice exists near the beds and within the margins of fast-flowing ice streams and glaciers, precisely the regions where ice discharge into the ocean is concentrated. Contrary to decades of reliance on nonlinear power-law descriptions, these experiments revealed that temperate ice exhibits **linear-viscous behavior (stress exponent near one) across the range of liquid water content and stress expected in natural glacier settings.**[9] This linearity likely results from diffusive pressure melting and refreezing at grain boundaries that accommodates strain while maintaining relatively uniform stress within ice grains.[9] If temperate ice truly behaves as a linear-viscous fluid under natural conditions, this represents a fundamental departure from the nonlinear Glen's law used in most ice sheet models and could substantially alter predictions of how fast-flowing ice streams respond to perturbations.

The implications for ice sheet modeling prove particularly significant in light of observations that basal ice temperatures in many Antarctic outlet glaciers and ice streams approach the pressure-melting point, suggesting that substantial portions of these rapidly flowing regions may consist of temperate ice.[5] If the stress-strain relationship in these critical zones transitions from nonlinear to linear with temperature, then the viscosity structure of the ice fundamentally changes, potentially affecting the transmission of stress from the ice sheet interior to the grounding line and ice shelf. The possibility that temperate ice behaves linearly remains controversial, however, and represents an area requiring additional laboratory validation and field verification before incorporation into large-scale ice sheet models.

## Basal Sliding and Friction Laws

### Weertman-Type Power Law Formulations

The sliding of ice across its bed represents the second major mode of ice motion, and in many ice streams and outlet glaciers, basal sliding contributes more to total ice velocity than internal deformation.[21] The theoretical foundation for understanding basal sliding emerged from studies of regelation—the pressure-induced melting and refreezing that permits ice to slide over bedrock bumps—and work by J. Weertman showing that sliding velocity should relate to basal shear stress through a power-law relationship.[21] The Weertman sliding law, expressed as \(\tau_b = C_m N u_b^{1/m}\), where \(\tau_b\) is basal shear stress, \(N\) is effective pressure at the bed, \(u_b\) is sliding velocity, and \(C_m\) is a friction coefficient, has been incorporated into nearly all large-scale ice sheet models.[15][21]

The power-law exponent \(m\) in Weertman-type sliding laws typically ranges between one and three, with values near one corresponding to Newtonian (linearly viscous) sliding and higher values approaching plastic behavior where basal friction approaches a constant limit.[15][55] The choice of this exponent profoundly influences how ice sheets respond to forcing: higher \(m\) values produce more plastic behavior where friction becomes relatively insensitive to velocity once a threshold is exceeded, while lower values produce more viscous behavior where friction increases indefinitely with sliding speed.[15][55] Studies comparing predictions across different sliding law formulations reveal that **changes in the sliding exponent within the plausible range can lead to doubling of flow-driven mass loss within the first centuries of warming, compared to standard parameter choices.**[8]

A critical limitation of Weertman-type power laws emerges when basal water pressure becomes sufficiently high to cause widespread cavitation, where pockets of water occupy a substantial fraction of the bed.[55] Under such conditions, basal friction should reach an upper limit and remain relatively constant or even decline with further increases in sliding velocity, behavior more consistent with Coulomb friction than with power-law friction.[55] Standard Weertman formulations allow basal friction to increase indefinitely as sliding accelerates, producing physically unrealistic stresses at the bed. Addressing this limitation requires either reformulating the sliding law or replacing it entirely with regularized Coulomb friction that incorporates cavitation effects.[55]

### Regularized Coulomb Friction and Transition Behavior

Regularized Coulomb friction laws, which incorporate an upper bound on basal friction related to cavitation effects, have emerged as more physically realistic representations of basal sliding, particularly for fast-moving ice streams where effective pressures may be very low.[55][58] These laws transition from power-law behavior at low sliding speeds, where basal friction increases with velocity, to approximately constant friction at high sliding speeds exceeding a characteristic threshold velocity.[55][58] The transition speed depends on basal effective pressure and the characteristics of the subglacial drainage system, both of which remain difficult to observe and quantify directly.

Hindcast simulations comparing different sliding laws on well-constrained systems demonstrate that regularized Coulomb friction reproduces observed ice acceleration more accurately than power-law friction when forced with observed ice shelf thinning and grounding line retreat.[58] Specifically, regularized Coulomb friction more faithfully captures the spatial extent and magnitude of velocity increases away from the grounding line region, whereas linear power-law friction concentrates stress changes at the grounding line, limiting inland propagation of speedup.[55][58] This distinction matters for projections because it determines whether velocity increases spread gradually inland or remain confined to narrow grounding line regions where boundary effects dominate.

### Effective Pressure and Subglacial Hydrology

The coupling between basal sliding and subglacial hydrology through the effective pressure term \(N = P_o - P_w\) (where \(P_o\) is ice overburden pressure and \(P_w\) is basal water pressure) represents another critical uncertainty constraining sea level rise predictions.[32][35] Water pressure reduces the normal stress holding ice against the bed, thereby reducing basal friction and accelerating sliding. The spatial distribution and temporal evolution of basal water pressure depend on the rate of meltwater production, the architecture of the subglacial drainage system (which may range from a well-organized network of channels to a distributed system of linked cavities), and the hydraulic head gradient driving water flow.[32] Most ice sheet models represent subglacial hydrology through simplified parameterizations rather than explicit simulation of drainage system evolution.

A detailed study comparing different approaches to representing effective pressure in ice sheet models revealed that the choice of hydrology parameterization substantially modulates basal sliding response to climatic forcing, though the response pattern does not necessarily follow universal systematic trends.[32] Models incorporating explicit subglacial water pressure predictions from hydrology models produce significantly different spatial patterns of basal sliding compared to those using simpler parameterizations of effective pressure based on ice thickness geometry alone.[32] These differences in sliding patterns translate into different predicted ice discharge rates and grounding line positions, ultimately affecting sea level rise projections. The large uncertainty space associated with subglacial hydrology representation thus represents a substantial irreducible source of uncertainty in ice sheet projections, at least until direct observations of subglacial water systems become feasible.

## How Stress-Strain Uncertainties Propagate to Sea Level Rise Projections

### Sensitivity Analysis and Parameter Uncertainty

Direct investigation of how uncertainties in stress-strain relationships propagate through to sea level rise projections reveals that flow law parameters exert first-order control on projected ice discharge.[8][26] A modeling study using the Parallel Ice Sheet Model deliberately excluded surface mass balance changes and focused solely on how variations in flow law parameters translate to flow-driven mass loss under warming scenarios.[8] The researchers found that **changes in flow parameters within the observed range can lead up to a doubling of flow-driven mass loss within the first centuries of warming, compared to standard parameters, with the spread of ice loss due to flow parameter uncertainty matching the increase in mass loss due to surface warming.**[8]

This finding demonstrates that the epistemic uncertainty associated with our incomplete understanding of ice rheology rivals or exceeds the uncertainty from future climate forcing. Different models making different assumptions about the appropriate stress exponent or other flow law parameters will project substantially different rates of ice sheet response, creating a range of possible futures even when forced with identical climate scenarios. The sensitivity of ice loss to the stress exponent appears robust across different model configurations and ice sheet geometries, suggesting that improving constraints on this fundamental parameter would substantially reduce projection uncertainty.

### Multi-Model Ensemble Studies

The Ice Sheet Model Intercomparison Project for LARMIP-2 brought together sixteen state-of-the-art ice sheet models to examine uncertainty in Antarctic ice loss attributable to basal ice shelf melting under multiple warming scenarios.[7] Rather than asking each model to simulate the full range of ocean melt forcing possibilities, researchers had each model generate a response function describing how ice discharge responds to a unit increase in basal melt rate, then convolved these response functions with probabilistic ensemble forcing scenarios. This approach efficiently characterized model uncertainty while acknowledging irreducible uncertainty in ocean melt forcing. The intercomparison revealed that while median projections remained relatively consistent across models, the range of responses spanned by individual models for the same forcing proved very large, with the upper tail of the distribution extending substantially higher than the median.

Importantly, the spread in model responses cannot be attributed solely to differences in flow law parameters, though these contribute significantly. Model differences in how grounding lines are simulated, how ice shelf buttressing is represented, and how longitudinal stress transmission is handled all contribute to ensemble spread. Nevertheless, flow law formulations remain a primary driver of model differences, particularly when models employ different approaches to representing temperature-dependent viscosity changes or crystal orientation fabric effects that have not been explicitly included in the standard Glen's law.

### Calibration Uncertainty and Parameter Identifiability

A sophisticated approach to quantifying calibration uncertainty in ice sheet models applied Bayesian inference to estimate posterior covariance of inverted parameters in a realistic West Antarctic setting with approximately one hundred thousand unknown parameters (the spatially distributed basal drag and ice stiffness coefficients).[33] The researchers employed a Hessian-based approach to estimate parameter uncertainty given observational constraints from satellite ice velocity data. Their findings revealed that significant differences in satellite ice velocity products, particularly at ice margins where measurement uncertainty is highest, can lead to substantially different posterior distributions of calibrated parameters and, consequently, different projections of future ice sheet evolution.[33]

This work illuminates a critical but often-overlooked source of uncertainty: even when models are carefully calibrated against observations, the inverse problem of inferring spatially varying parameters from velocity observations remains fundamentally ill-posed, with non-unique solutions. Multiple combinations of basal drag and rheology parameters can produce approximately identical agreement with observations, yet these parameter combinations produce different model predictions when forced with future climate scenarios. The spread in future projections arising from calibration uncertainty can rival the spread arising from different external forcing scenarios, highlighting the inadequacy of presenting ice sheet projections as single best-estimate values rather than as probability distributions reflecting all sources of uncertainty.

## Stress-Strain Relationships in Complex Geometries

### Longitudinal Stress Coupling and Nonlocal Stress Balance

The focus on depth-averaged stress-strain relationships in simplified geometries obscures important complexities that emerge in realistic ice sheet configurations characterized by rapid spatial variations in ice velocity and thickness.[19] Longitudinal stresses—stresses acting along the direction of ice flow that reflect the resistance from downstream ice and the acceleration of ice entering faster-moving regions—can exert substantial control on ice flow despite being neglected in the Shallow Ice Approximation (SIA) that governed early ice sheet modeling.[19] The Shallow-Shelf Approximation (SSA) better represents longitudinal stresses but fails to capture vertical variations in velocity that become important in grounding line regions and transition zones between flowing and non-flowing ice.

Exact solutions to the Shallow Shelf Approximation equations under nonlinear ice rheology reveal how the power-law form of the stress-strain relationship fundamentally shapes stress transmission through the ice.[19] The nonlinear rheology produces feedback effects between stress state and deformation rate that determine the characteristic length scale over which longitudinal stresses propagate—the "coupling length."[19] For a given applied stress, the nonlinear relationship means that regions with higher stress experience disproportionately higher strain rates, which concentrates deformation into narrow bands and limits how far stress perturbations propagate inland from the grounding line. The magnitude of this coupling length directly determines whether perturbations at the coast trigger rapid ice sheet collapse or remain confined to narrow grounding line regions, making it fundamentally important for projecting ice sheet response to future ocean warming.

### Crystal Orientation Fabric and Anisotropic Viscosity

Natural glacier ice develops anisotropic crystal orientation patterns (called fabric) as it undergoes large cumulative strains under prolonged deformation.[31][34][46] The orientation pattern reflects the stress history experienced by the ice, with crystals rotating toward favorable orientations for the applied stress configuration. Ice with well-developed fabric deforms much more readily than isotropic ice of the same bulk temperature, exhibiting enhancement factors (multipliers applied to the isotropic viscosity) of three to ten or more depending on the stress configuration.[13][46] Simple isotropic formulations of Glen's law cannot capture this enhanced deformability and systematically overestimate the strength of highly strained basal ice and ice stream margins.

The anisotropic rheology manifests not merely as a uniform enhancement of the bulk viscosity but through orientation-dependent stress sensitivity, where the effective stress exponent depends on whether ice is being compressed, extended, or sheared.[13] Ice undergoing simple shear (as occurs along ice stream margins and in some parts of ice shelf interiors) deforms far more readily than ice undergoing compression, yet most ice sheet models either neglect fabric effects entirely or apply uniform enhancement factors that fail to capture this directional dependence.[13][46] Preliminary implementations of physically motivated constitutive relations accounting for fabric effects show promise for improving model accuracy, but such approaches introduce additional computational complexity and require specification of how fabric evolves under varying stress regimes—knowledge that remains incomplete.

## Uncertainties Constraining Present and Future Sea Level Rise

### The Irreducibility of Certain Uncertainties

Comprehensive assessment of sources driving uncertainty in ice sheet projections through structured expert judgment identified processes whose uncertainties dominate projection spread as functions of ice sheet, time horizon, and temperature scenario.[25] For Greenland, surface melting and the critically important surface albedo feedback dominate uncertainty through the twenty-first century, though Greenland ice dynamics (including changes in sliding rates) emerge as significant sources of long-term uncertainty.[25] For West Antarctica, ice shelf buttressing effects—which themselves depend on how ice shelves respond to changes in stress fields—dominate uncertainty, with the power of basal sliding and sensitivity of sliding to effective pressure changes representing critical unknowns.[25] For East Antarctica, dynamics generally remains a smaller contributor to uncertainty, though under high-warming scenarios, East Antarctic discharge becomes significant enough to substantially affect global sea level projections.

The irreducibility of certain uncertainties stems from fundamental gaps in understanding rather than limitations in current data. We lack direct observations of subglacial stress fields, water pressure distributions, and bed material properties at depth across most of the ice sheets, making it impossible to fully constrain the processes governing basal sliding. Similarly, the difficulty of observing how ice fabric evolves under field stress conditions leaves uncertainty in the appropriate enhancement factors and directional viscosity dependences for natural ice. Until substantial additional field work and laboratory validation enable tighter constraints on these fundamental processes, uncertainty in ice discharge will remain substantial.

### Basal Drag Coefficient Uncertainty

A systematic inverse modeling study of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet examined how the choice of sliding law formulation, the representation of effective pressure from subglacial hydrology models versus simple geometric parameterizations, and the strength of regularization in the inversion procedure each influence the inferred spatial distribution of basal drag coefficients.[27] The researchers found that nonlinear friction laws (including Budd-type and Coulomb friction) produced substantially less variable spatial patterns of inferred basal drag compared to linear laws, and that incorporating explicit subglacial effective pressure from a hydrology model rather than geometric approximations yielded smoother, more physically interpretable basal drag distributions.[27] These findings suggest that choices about how basal friction is parameterized not only affect model projections directly but also influence the calibrated parameter values, creating subtle interactions between model formulation and parameter uncertainty.

The spatial distribution of basal drag remains highly uncertain because velocity observations alone cannot uniquely determine the basal drag pattern; multiple distributions of drag can produce identical or nearly identical ice velocity fields depending on the stress-strain relationship assumed.[27] Regularization methods impose penalties on spatial roughness or departure from prior assumptions, reducing the non-uniqueness but at the cost of smoothing away potentially real variations in basal properties. The choice of regularization strength thus subtly influences which basal drag patterns are preferred by the inversion, with implications for projections of future ice discharge.

## Implications for Twenty-First Century and Beyond Projections

### Time-Dependent Evolution of Sea Level Contribution

Projections of Antarctic ice sheet contribution to sea level through 2100 under moderate warming (SSP2-4.5 scenario) incorporating consideration of internal climate variability reveal that the Antarctic Ice Sheet currently gains mass despite being forced by oceanic warming, as increases in surface accumulation from enhanced precipitation outpace increases in ice discharge driven by basal melting.[28] This compensation produces a wide range of projected sea level contributions depending on climate model choice and internal variability realization, ranging from −1.34 to −8.46 centimeters by 2100, with the negative values indicating net mass gain rather than loss.[28] Internal climate variability affects Antarctic sea level contribution by 45 to 93 percent depending on the climate model, highlighting that internal ocean variability—unforced variations intrinsic to the climate system itself—exerts significant control on twenty-first century projections, a source of uncertainty distinct from parameter and model structural uncertainty.

For longer time horizons extending to 2300, projections become increasingly sensitive to stress-strain uncertainties because cumulative ice discharge becomes substantial.[25] The balance between surface accumulation changes driven by atmospheric warming and ice discharge driven by ocean forcing shifts such that discharge dominates, creating potential for accelerating mass loss if positive feedbacks in the stress-strain relationship amplify ice sheet response. The timeframe over which stress-strain uncertainties become large compared to other uncertainties depends on the particular ice sheet region and forcing scenario, but generally extends on the order of centuries to millennia.

### Constraint from Observational Records

The rapid satellite-era growth in observational data constrains stress-strain relationships through comparison of modeled and observed ice velocity fields, but significant limitations persist. Satellite observations typically provide ice surface velocities integrated through the full ice thickness, which cannot be inverted uniquely to determine the vertical profile of deformation without additional information about basal sliding. Temperature profiles needed to constrain the temperature dependence of viscosity require difficult borehole measurements rarely available. Radar surveys provide some information about ice thickness and internal layering but reveal little about ice crystal fabric, grain size, or other microstructural properties affecting rheology.

The most powerful constraints on stress-strain relationships have come from laboratory experiments, yet these operate at strain rates and stresses that differ from natural conditions, introducing uncertainty in extrapolation. The recent satellite-era identification that ice flow is more sensitive to stress than predicted by standard Glen's law improved model-data agreement, but questions persist about whether all aspects of the revised stress-strain relationships have been correctly identified and whether they apply uniformly across different ice sheet regions and thermal regimes.

## Future Research Directions and Uncertainty Reduction Pathways

### Laboratory Experimentation and Natural Ice Studies

Continued advancement in understanding ice rheology requires complementary laboratory and field approaches. Large-scale laboratory experiments on temperate ice have provided unexpected findings about linear viscosity, but these results require validation through field studies and fuller understanding of how temperate ice properties relate to observable ice sheet behavior. Microstructural studies documenting how ice crystal orientation and grain size evolve under natural stress conditions remain limited, yet such understanding proves essential for constraining enhancement factors and anisotropic rheology models. Development of deep boreholes that enable direct measurement of ice temperature, crystal structure, and mechanical properties at depths representative of ice stream beds would provide invaluable constraints currently lacking.

Field-based measurements of basal stress and effective pressure using borehole instruments remain technically challenging but would dramatically constrain basal sliding relationships. Current understanding of subglacial hydrology relies heavily on inference from surface observations; direct observations of drainage system architecture, water pressure, and sediment properties would substantially reduce uncertainty in how ice sheet dynamics respond to ocean and atmospheric forcing changes.

### Model Development and Parameter Estimation

Ice sheet models incorporating newer formulations of ice rheology that explicitly represent multiple deformation mechanisms and grain size evolution show promise for reducing projections' dependence on simple power-law assumptions. However, these more complex models require additional parameters, creating new calibration challenges. Development of inversion methods that can robustly estimate spatially varying rheological parameters despite non-uniqueness challenges represents an important frontier. Machine learning approaches show promise for learning effective parameterizations from high-resolution simulations that capture physics not resolved by coarse global models.

The integration of explicit subglacial hydrology models into ice sheet simulations, while computationally expensive, enables more physically motivated treatment of basal sliding than geometric parameterizations. As computational resources expand, such integrated modeling approaches will likely become standard, though their validation against limited field observations will remain challenging.

## Conclusion

The stress-strain relationship governing ice deformation, formalized through Glen's flow law and its more recent refinements, fundamentally constrains predictions of ice sheet response to future climate change and resulting sea level rise. Recent discoveries that ice flow exhibits greater stress sensitivity than previously modeled, combined with growing recognition that multiple deformation mechanisms operate under natural conditions, have substantially revised our understanding of how ice accelerates in response to perturbations. The nonlinear and temperature-dependent nature of ice viscosity, combined with the complex interplay between basal sliding and effective pressure, creates a system where relatively small changes in forcing can trigger substantially amplified ice discharge responses—or alternatively, where the system remains relatively stable depending on parameter values and initial conditions.

The propagation of stress-strain uncertainties through ice sheet models to sea level rise projections reveals that our incomplete understanding of ice rheology represents an uncertainty source rivaling or exceeding uncertainties from future climate forcing. The spread in projected ice sheet responses across different models employing different stress-strain relationships demonstrates that constraining these fundamental relationships should rank among the highest research priorities in glaciology. Yet despite seventy years of laboratory investigation and two decades of satellite observations, substantial ambiguities remain regarding the correct formulation of stress-strain relationships for temperate ice, the appropriate treatment of crystal fabric effects, and the proper description of the transition from power-law to Coulomb-dominated basal friction as effective pressure decreases.

The implications extend beyond academic refinement of equations: ice sheet projections currently provided to policymakers and coastal planners necessarily reflect the envelope of uncertainty encompassed by different stress-strain assumptions, producing probability distributions whose widths substantially exceed those that would derive from climate forcing uncertainty alone. Narrowing these distributions requires sustained commitment to laboratory work validating stress-strain relationships under conditions approaching natural glacier environments, field deployment of instruments measuring ice properties and basal conditions, and development of models sophisticatedly representing multiple deformation mechanisms. Until such improvements occur, uncertainty in twenty-first century sea level rise contributions from ice sheets will remain substantial, emphasizing the need for coastal adaptation strategies robust across a wide range of possible futures.
