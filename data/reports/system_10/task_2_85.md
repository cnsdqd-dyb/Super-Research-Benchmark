# Lagged Soil-Vegetation Feedback and Amplification of Prediction Uncertainty in Global Carbon Budget Models

This comprehensive analysis examines whether the lagged response of soil-vegetation feedback amplifies prediction uncertainty in global carbon budget models. The evidence overwhelmingly indicates that temporal lags in ecosystem carbon responses represent a substantial source of unrecognized uncertainty that significantly complicates model predictions. Rather than straightforward cause-and-effect relationships between climate and carbon cycling, ecosystem carbon fluxes respond to environmental changes through complex temporal dynamics where the effects of current climatic conditions may not manifest for months to years, or even longer. These lagged responses interact with soil carbon decomposition processes, vegetation productivity changes, and nutrient cycling in ways that are poorly represented in most current Earth system models, thereby amplifying the range of uncertainty in projected carbon budgets and climate feedbacks. The problem is particularly acute because most models treat soil and vegetation responses to environmental change as instantaneous phenomena, missing critical temporal dynamics that could substantially alter projections of whether terrestrial ecosystems will act as carbon sinks or sources under future climate scenarios.

## Understanding Temporal Lags in Terrestrial Carbon Cycling

The terrestrial carbon cycle exhibits profound temporal complexity that extends far beyond simple instantaneous responses to environmental forcing. Ecosystem carbon fluxes do not respond immediately to changes in temperature, precipitation, or atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations. Instead, multiple processes operate across vastly different temporal scales, creating cascading delays that propagate through the carbon system. These lagged responses emerge from the fundamental biology and physics of how ecosystems process and store carbon. When environmental conditions change, plants must first adjust their physiological processes, then modify their growth allocation patterns, and finally alter the quantity and quality of organic matter entering the soil. Meanwhile, soil microbes responding to these changes exhibit their own temporal dynamics as they adjust their metabolic rates and decomposition activities. The accumulation of these individual delays at different scales creates emergent properties of the ecosystem carbon cycle that simple models cannot capture.

Research examining grassland ecosystems across China reveals that these lagged effects are substantial and ecosystem-dependent. Temperature effects on carbon dioxide exchange at multiple sites showed lags of several months to even half a year, while moisture effects were typically exhibited in a more immediate manner, except at semiarid sites where lagged effects were also significant[2]. The plasticity of ecosystem responses could buffer and postpone the effects of climate on ecosystem carbon fluxes, but this lagged effect is often ignored in model development and climate projections[2]. This finding has profound implications because it suggests that ecosystem carbon responses to climate extremes and variability are fundamentally different from what models predict. If ecosystems take months or even a year before their carbon flux adjustments reach equilibrium with current climate conditions, then the notion of a stable carbon sink or source at any given time becomes questionable. The ecosystem is perpetually playing catch-up with environmental changes, meaning that rapid climate shifts could find the terrestrial system in a state of fundamental disequilibrium.

The different magnitudes of lag effects for different climatic factors create additional complexity. At alpine sites in China, temperature exhibited continuous and long-lasting impacts on carbon fluxes, while soil moisture effects were more immediate[2]. This differential response means that the same environmental event—perhaps a warm, dry season—could trigger different temporal responses in plant productivity and soil respiration, potentially causing counteracting carbon cycle feedbacks that differ in timing and magnitude. Such temporal mismatches between carbon inputs and outputs could substantially alter the net carbon balance compared to what models assuming instantaneous responses would predict. In regions where temperature and moisture are decoupled in their temporal effects, the resulting carbon response becomes highly complex and difficult to predict without explicitly modeling these lag structures.

## Mechanisms Generating Lagged Responses in Soil-Vegetation Interactions

The soil-vegetation system generates lagged responses through multiple interconnected mechanisms that operate across different temporal and spatial scales. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for appreciating why lagged responses amplify uncertainty rather than merely shifting predictions by a constant amount. One primary mechanism involves vegetation state variables that mediate the response of carbon cycling to environmental change. When temperature increases, vegetation does not immediately increase its photosynthetic rate; instead, plants must first adjust enzyme activities, then synthesize new proteins and structural tissues, gradually building the physiological capacity to maintain higher growth rates under the new thermal regime. This physiological adjustment process typically takes weeks to months. Beyond individual plant physiology, vegetation communities must also shift their species composition in response to sustained environmental change, a process that occurs over decades to centuries as competitive balances shift among existing species and new species migrate into regions[26].

Leaf area index, which fundamentally controls the capacity of vegetation to intercept light and conduct photosynthesis, responds to environmental changes through phenological and morphological adjustments that lag behind the environmental forcing. Precipitation can generate lag effects on ecosystem carbon dioxide exchange by altering leaf area index, changing canopy chemistry, and shifting soil microbial communities[2]. These indirect pathways create substantial delays because changes in microbial community composition require multiple generations of cells and can take years to fully manifest. Plant-available water stored in soils also creates temporal lags because precipitation-induced increases in soil water content follow a predictable lag relative to precipitation events themselves, depending on soil permeability and drainage characteristics[2]. In the most immediate term, increased precipitation wets surface soils quickly, but water reaching deeper soil layers involved in vegetation water uptake may lag by weeks or months, creating a temporal mismatch between the wettest conditions experienced by plants and the precipitation that created those conditions.

Tundra ecosystems demonstrate particularly complex lagged responses where multiple mechanisms interact in surprising ways. Early greenup or delayed snowmelt affects vegetation growth and terrestrial carbon cycles, but the magnitude of this effect depends on local climatic limits[26]. In some regions, early greenup amplifies net ecosystem productivity because temperature and water availability are co-limiting, so warming that extends the growing season provides disproportionate benefit. In other regions, early greenup has minimal effect on productivity because light availability rather than temperature is the limiting factor. These regional differences mean that global models attempting to represent a uniform response of productivity to phenological changes will inevitably misrepresent the carbon cycle response in many locations. Moreover, delayed snowmelt can offset the productivity gains from early warming by reducing the total length of the growing season, creating compensating effects that cancel out if modeled as independent responses but interact nonlinearly when vegetation must adjust to the combination of both changes simultaneously[26].

Carbon in vegetation persists in biomass for finite periods before being returned to the atmosphere through respiration or transferred to soil carbon pools through litter fall and root decomposition. This residence time of carbon in vegetation biomass represents one of the largest uncertainties in the global carbon cycle[9]. Modeled baseline global average forest biomass carbon turnover times vary from 12.2 to 23.5 years between terrestrial biosphere models, an almost twofold range that translates into vastly different projections of how quickly carbon sequestered in vegetation will eventually return to the atmosphere[14]. Different models generate these divergent estimates because they incorporate different assumptions about plant mortality rates, allocation to woody versus non-woody tissues, and the response of these processes to environmental change[14]. Since vegetation carbon residence time directly determines how long sequestered carbon remains in the biosphere before contributing to decomposition and soil carbon dynamics, uncertainty in this fundamental parameter creates uncertainty in the timing and magnitude of carbon releases from terrestrial ecosystems. If one model predicts that forest biomass carbon averages twelve years of residence time while another model predicts twenty-three years, their projections of future carbon release from forests under sustained growth will diverge substantially over decades, even if they initially agree on current biomass stocks.

## Lagged Effects in Soil Carbon Decomposition and Persistence

Soil carbon dynamics demonstrate perhaps the most profound temporal complexities in the terrestrial carbon cycle, with different soil layers cycling carbon at rates differing by orders of magnitude. Radiocarbon measurements spanning fifty years from a hardwood forest in the northeastern United States reveal that soil litter carbon cycles on decadal timescales with a transit time of approximately seven years for the Oi and Oe layers, whereas carbon at the organic-mineral interface (Oa/A horizon) cycles on centennial timescales with a system age of approximately one hundred and four years, and mineral soil carbon in the top ten centimeters cycles on even longer timescales with a transit time of approximately three hundred and two years[32]. These starkly different turnover rates mean that the soil carbon system exists in multiple temporal regimes simultaneously. Litter layer carbon responds relatively quickly to environmental changes, providing an early-warning system for ecosystem responses to environmental forcing. But the dominant pool of soil carbon, stored in mineral soil horizons, responds so slowly that environmental changes occurring today will take centuries to fully influence the carbon storage capacity of these pools[32].

This temporal heterogeneity in soil carbon turnover creates fundamental challenges for model development and validation. Most Earth system models represent soil carbon through simplified compartmental structures that may not adequately capture this diversity of timescales. A model that represents soil carbon as a few undifferentiated pools with moderate turnover times will fundamentally misrepresent the system where litter, mineral-associated organic matter, and deeply buried organic carbon all respond to environmental change at different rates. The implied lag structure in model predictions becomes artificially compressed into shorter timescales than actually observed in nature, causing models to overestimate how quickly soil carbon responds to environmental changes and potentially overestimate the magnitude of soil carbon feedbacks to climate change.

The relationship between soil carbon stability and its chemical and physical characteristics interacts with temporal lags in complex ways. Historically, soil scientists believed that more complex carbon compounds such as lignin-rich plant tissues persisted in soil longer than simple compounds like sugars and amino acids, but contemporary understanding recognizes that carbon residence time is more influenced by protection from microbial processing than by chemical makeup of organic matter[60]. Protection can arise through physical occlusion within soil aggregates where microbes cannot access the carbon, or through strong binding to soil mineral surfaces, particularly iron and aluminum oxides. These protective mechanisms develop over time as soil microbes decompose the organic matter that initially entered the soil, creating a feedback where the very process of decomposition makes remaining carbon more persistent[60]. This means that freshly added litter decomposes rapidly at first, with progressively slower decomposition as the easily accessible and readily metabolizable compounds are consumed. After a year or two, the remaining carbon has been enriched for compounds and structures that resist microbial attack, and this biochemical shift creates a step-change in decomposition rate. The lag between initial carbon input and this transition to slow decomposition introduces another temporal complexity that models must capture.

Soil carbon decomposition responds to temperature through a temperature sensitivity parameter known as Q10, which describes the multiplier effect on decomposition rate for every ten-degree Celsius increase in temperature. Studies integrating field observations of warming effects on soil carbon suggest a global Q10 value of approximately 2.2 with confidence intervals from 1.6 to 2.7, but when these field-derived Q10 values are incorporated into Earth system model simulations, they reveal extremely high intra-model uncertainty in projected soil carbon stocks[21]. The intra-model uncertainty driven by Q10 parameter variation was as great as the variation between different models, demonstrating that even specifying a single best-estimate parameter value does not eliminate fundamental uncertainty about how soil carbon will respond to warming[21]. Moreover, Q10 itself may not remain constant across temperature ranges, soil types, and climate regimes, introducing nonlinearities into the temperature response of soil carbon that further complicate predictions. Different models implicitly assume different Q10 values through their parameterizations, and these differences propagate forward to generate substantially different long-term projections of soil carbon under climate change scenarios.

## How Lagged Responses Amplify Uncertainty in Model Predictions

Lagged responses amplify uncertainty in global carbon budget models through multiple mechanisms that interact to compound rather than average out. The fundamental issue is that uncertainties in lagged responses are not randomly distributed around a mean value; instead, they are often directional and can either amplify or dampen carbon cycle feedbacks depending on the specific circumstances. When multiple lagged responses operate simultaneously with different lag times and different dependencies on environmental drivers, the possibilities for divergent outcomes multiply rapidly. A model that underestimates the lag time for vegetation productivity to respond to warming might overestimate how much additional carbon will be fixed by vegetation in the coming decades. This overestimation of carbon fixation would then increase the predicted size of the land carbon sink. But if that same model simultaneously underestimates the lag time for soil carbon decomposition to respond to warming, it might overestimate how quickly additional soil respiration will offset the increased carbon fixation, partially canceling the overestimate of vegetation response. However, because these responses typically have different lag times and operate across different temporal and spatial scales, the cancellation is rarely exact. The result is that models often converge on similar projections of net carbon balance while operating through different mechanistic pathways with different underlying uncertainties[43].

This problem of convergence obscuring inconsistency has emerged clearly in analysis of CMIP5 and CMIP6 model predictions of soil carbon change. Although global-scale soil carbon projections have become more similar across models in the newer CMIP6 generation, the underlying mechanisms driving these projections remain substantially different[43]. Some models predict increased soil carbon primarily through greater plant productivity responses to CO2 fertilization, while others predict soil carbon increases primarily through reduced decomposition at high latitudes, and yet others predict increases through changed vegetation community composition. These mechanistically different pathways might all converge on similar global soil carbon changes, but they would diverge substantially when examined at regional scales or under alternative climate scenarios. Such mechanical convergence creates a false sense of confidence in model projections because the agreement suggests underlying model confidence in both the direction and magnitude of change, when in reality the agreement masks substantial disagreement about how the system works[43].

The lagged nature of soil-vegetation feedbacks creates particular problems for prediction because these feedbacks involve multiple components operating at different timescales. Consider a warming scenario where temperature increases cause vegetation productivity to initially increase due to extended growing seasons and enhanced photosynthesis. This increased productivity transfers more carbon to soils through litter fall and root exudates. However, the same warming also accelerates soil decomposition rates through increased microbial activity. If vegetation responds quickly but soil carbon decomposition lags, the model will temporarily show net carbon accumulation that does not represent true carbon sequestration because it results from a temporary mismatch between carbon inputs and outputs rather than a fundamental shift in the carbon balance. When soil decomposition eventually catches up to increased carbon inputs, the accumulated carbon will be rapidly respired, turning what appeared to be permanent soil carbon storage into transient carbon accumulation[32].

Lagged responses also amplify uncertainty because they create internal memory in the carbon system that makes the future state path-dependent. The history of environmental forcing matters, not just the current conditions. A region that experienced sustained warming followed by cooling will not have the same carbon dynamics as a region that experienced the reverse temporal sequence, even if both regions experience the same mean temperature. This path-dependence violates a key assumption in many climate models, which implicitly assume that carbon cycle responses depend only on current climate state and not on the trajectory by which that state was reached. When models fail to capture path-dependence, they systematically misproject the carbon cycle response to scenarios where climate change is not monotonic. Any scenario involving temporary cooling periods or pauses in warming will likely be misrepresented by models that lack explicit temporal lag structures.

## Model Representation of Soil Carbon and the Structural Uncertainty Problem

Current Earth system models represent soil carbon responses to warming in a common manner that fundamentally limits their ability to capture lagged responses and their attendant uncertainties. Soil carbon decomposition to CO2 is typically modeled as following a single first-order response curve, similar to half-life plots for radioactive decay, where the time taken for a constant fraction of soil carbon to decompose decreases with warming[3][8][11]. This mathematical structure implies that decomposition accelerates smoothly with temperature without the complex temporal dynamics observed in real soils. The reality of soil carbon turnover is far more complex, involving multiple pools with different turnover rates, different temperature sensitivities, and different responses to moisture and nutrient availability. By forcing all this complexity into a first-order exponential decay structure, models necessarily misrepresent the temporal dynamics of soil carbon response to environmental change.

The major modeling uncertainty generating low confidence in projected soil carbon losses arises because the most common assumption about soil carbon turnover in Earth system models is outdated and inconsistent with contemporary soil biogeochemistry[3][8][11]. Models rely on representations of soil carbon stabilization mechanisms that do not incorporate modern understanding of how soil carbon is formed and protected. The traditional view held that complex, chemically resistant compounds like lignin and other recalcitrant organic matter persisted longest in soil, suggesting that the most important determinant of soil carbon persistence was the chemistry of the input material. But contemporary research demonstrates that physical protection from microbial access and strong binding to soil minerals, rather than chemical recalcitrance, determine whether soil carbon persists for decades or centuries[60]. Despite this fundamental shift in scientific understanding, most Earth system models have not been updated to represent these new mechanistic insights. Instead, the models continue using simplified parameter values that supposedly capture soil carbon turnover without actually representing the underlying processes controlling how carbon becomes stabilized in soil.

This structural misrepresentation creates what researchers term "structural uncertainty," which is distinct from parameter uncertainty. Parameter uncertainty reflects uncertainty about the specific numerical values to use for parameters within an agreed-upon model structure. If everyone agrees that soil carbon decomposes according to a first-order rate equation, then parameter uncertainty involves disagreement about what the rate constant should be. But structural uncertainty involves disagreement about whether a first-order rate equation is even the appropriate model structure. Addressing structural uncertainty requires fundamentally different model architectures that represent multiple soil carbon pools with different stabilization mechanisms, not merely tuning parameter values within existing model structures. The distinction matters greatly for understanding how lagged responses amplify uncertainty, because different model structures can generate very different temporal dynamics even if they produce similar long-term carbon balances[3][8][11].

Representing soil nitrogen dynamics alongside soil carbon represents one avenue for addressing structural uncertainty, but it introduces additional complexity. Models that include coupled carbon-nitrogen biogeochemistry generally project lower terrestrial carbon storage because nitrogen availability constrains plant productivity response to elevated CO2[3][8][11]. Including nitrogen dynamics in models both increases uncertainty in some dimensions—by introducing additional uncertain parameters and processes—while simultaneously building confidence in projections by representing mechanisms that models without nitrogen cycles systematically ignore[3][8][11]. This paradoxical situation where including more realistic mechanisms increases uncertainty in the model ensemble but increases confidence in individual model projections exemplifies the challenge of improving representation of lagged responses. More mechanistic models that better capture temporal lags will likely show larger spreads in projections across the model ensemble, creating an apparent increase in uncertainty even as confidence in the underlying mechanisms increases.

## Observations Documenting Lagged Carbon Cycle Responses

Empirical evidence from ecosystems worldwide demonstrates that lagged responses are not minor details but rather central features of how terrestrial ecosystems respond to environmental change. Analysis of the 2001-2015 tropical carbon balance using satellite and ground-based measurements reveals that lagged effects regulate the inter-annual variability of the tropical net carbon exchange substantially[31]. Using a data-constrained ecosystem carbon modeling framework, researchers decomposed inter-annual changes in tropical carbon fluxes into concurrent responses to current-year environmental conditions and lagged responses to conditions from previous years. They found that lagged effects on net primary productivity from previous-year environmental conditions were substantial, with lagged net primary productivity accounting for 113% of the net biome productivity lagged effect variability, indicating that lagged productivity responses were actually more important than concurrent responses in driving tropical carbon cycle inter-annual variability[31]. This finding suggests that most of the year-to-year variation in tropical carbon balance results not from how the ecosystem responds to current conditions, but from how it is still adjusting to conditions from months or years previously.

Tundra ecosystems along the Alaska Arctic coast display complex lagged carbon responses to phenological changes that vary dramatically depending on local climatic limits and water stress. The carbon response to early greenup was amplified at higher latitudes where temperature and water strongly colimit vegetation growth, while delayed snowmelt effects on ecosystem carbon were alleviated by relief of water stress[26]. This means that the same phenological change—early spring warming—produces different carbon cycle responses in different locations depending on which of multiple limiting factors is most restrictive in that location. Furthermore, the response to delayed snowmelt showed complex lagged dynamics, where immediate water stress from snow cover lingering late into spring was partially offset by later relief of water stress that enhanced productivity during the remainder of the growing season[26]. The net carbon response to delayed snowmelt thus depends critically on the timing of when snow melts relative to when the ecosystem transitions into active growth. These complex regional and temporal patterns cannot be captured by models that assume uniform responses of carbon cycling to phenological changes across space and time.

Documentation of vegetation lag effects to climate changes reveals patterns of temporal delay that vary substantially by ecosystem and vegetation type. In humid, warm, and treed ecosystems, plant responses to climate can take up to six months, while in drier and more sparsely vegetated regions, responses are more immediate[5][23][29]. This variation in lag times across ecosystems has profound implications for model predictions because it means that global models using uniform lag times for all ecosystems will systematically misrepresent carbon cycle responses in many regions. The mechanisms generating these ecosystem-specific lag times involve differences in how quickly vegetation adjusts phenology, how water and nutrient availability respond to climate changes, and how quickly new vegetation growth can be generated. Forests in humid climates likely show longer lags because trees take longer to adjust their physiology and growth patterns, while grasses and herbaceous vegetation in arid regions respond more rapidly because they have shorter generation times and can adjust physiology more quickly.

Recent observations of soil carbon dynamics from a long-term forest study demonstrate rapid changes in the fastest-cycling soil carbon pools even as slower-cycling pools remain relatively stable. Over the five decades of radiocarbon measurements at Hubbard Brook Experimental Forest, litter layer soil carbon stocks declined while mineral soil carbon remained relatively stable at the watershed scale[32]. This divergent response across soil layers indicates that environmental changes in the region are primarily affecting the rapidly cycling litter layer while not yet substantially affecting deeper mineral soils. This pattern could reflect changing conditions for litter decomposition rates, changes in litter input quantities, or shifts in soil microbial communities that preferentially consume recently added organic matter. The important implication for carbon budgets is that the early warning signals of ecosystem carbon cycle disruption appear in the rapidly cycling pools, whereas the delayed response of slowly cycling mineral soils means that large components of the soil carbon system will continue responding to current conditions for decades after those conditions have changed.

## Regional Heterogeneity in Lagged Response Times and Model Uncertainty

The spatial heterogeneity of soil carbon decomposition rates across the United States exemplifies how lagged responses will vary geographically and complicate global predictions. Soil samples from twenty National Ecological Observatory Network sites across the United States, when incubated under identical laboratory conditions, decomposed at rates differing by up to tenfold[6]. Machine learning analysis of twenty-six soil traits revealed that factors beyond simple soil type and pH, including fungal abundances and forms of iron and aluminum, emerged as key predictors of decay rates[6]. These factors are tightly tied to mineral-associated organic carbon pools that can persist in soil for decades or centuries, while particulate organic carbon pools from plant pieces break down within years[6]. The existence of this tenfold variation in decomposition rates implies that soil carbon responses to warming will differ dramatically across regions, making global models that apply uniform decomposition parameters substantially wrong for many ecosystems[6].

Using artificial intelligence models trained on laboratory results, researchers mapped soil carbon decay rates and carbon use efficiency across the contiguous United States at two-and-one-half-mile resolution[6]. The resulting maps revealed striking geographic patterns where soils in the Southwest break down organic carbon more quickly and send more of it into the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, while soils in the Northwest and the East break carbon down more slowly and keep more of it in microbial biomass[6]. These spatial patterns imply that climate change impacts on soil carbon will vary dramatically by region, with some areas experiencing rapid soil carbon losses under warming while others experience carbon retention. A global model that predicts, for example, average soil carbon increases of forty to eighty petagrams by 2100 obscures this underlying regional heterogeneity. Some regions will likely lose soil carbon substantially while others gain, and the geographic distribution of these changes will affect global carbon cycle feedbacks in ways that simple global averages cannot represent[6].

Different vegetation types show contrasting temporal responses to temperature stress that further complicate global predictions. Deciduous broadleaf forests exhibit a response lag of approximately one month to temperature changes, and the correlation between temperature and vegetation response increases as this lag time increases[20]. This pattern suggests that different vegetation types may have fundamentally different lag structures that cannot be represented by a single global vegetation model. Younger trees with higher allocation to leaves relative to woody biomass might show shorter lags because they can adjust growth patterns more quickly, while older forests dominated by woody biomass might show longer lags because they accumulate carbon in persistent structures that respond slowly to environmental change. Agricultural ecosystems showed lower resistance to temperature stress compared to forest ecosystems, indicating that disturbance history and ecosystem structure fundamentally influence not just the sensitivity of carbon cycling to temperature changes but also the timing and duration of lags in ecosystem response[20].

## Uncertainty in Carbon Cycle Feedbacks and Equilibrium Behavior

The carbon cycle feedback mechanism, whereby warming accelerates decomposition and releases additional CO2 that drives further warming, represents one of the largest sources of uncertainty in climate projections. Incorporating carbon-cycle feedback uncertainties into CMIP5 climate models substantially increases the range of projected warming by the end of the twenty-first century[16]. Under business-as-usual emissions, the low-end warming projection increases from 0.9°C to 0.8°C with carbon-cycle feedbacks included, while the high-end warming projection increases from 6°C to 7.7°C, representing a 1.7 degree increase in the upper bound of warming uncertainty[16]. This widening of the uncertainty range is asymmetric, with much larger increases in the high-end warming projections, suggesting that carbon-cycle feedbacks particularly amplify uncertainty in high-warming scenarios. The asymmetry reflects the underlying nonlinearity of carbon cycle responses, where small errors in representing decomposition rates or vegetation responses become magnified under large temperature changes. Lagged responses contribute to this amplified uncertainty because models may systematically misrepresent whether ecosystems are currently in equilibrium with prevailing climate conditions or are adjusting to conditions from earlier periods.

The concept of equilibrium climate sensitivity, describing the warming that would ultimately occur if CO2 were doubled and the climate system allowed to fully equilibrate, becomes problematic when lagged responses and delayed equilibration are considered. Different timescales of climate system response—from days for atmospheric adjustment to millenia for ice sheet responses—mean that no single "equilibrium" exists[25]. Models attempting to predict carbon cycle responses typically employ equilibrium climate sensitivity values between 2 and 5°C for CO2 doubling, representing the range of model estimates. However, the transient climate response to cumulative carbon emissions (TCRE), which predicts warming per unit of cumulative emissions actually released, differs from equilibrium sensitivity because it incorporates ocean heat uptake and lagged responses[7]. The TCRE remains relatively robust across different emission scenarios, but only if assumptions about non-CO2 forcing and other carbon cycle properties hold. When lagged responses are included explicitly, the TCRE becomes less predictable because the timing of carbon cycle feedbacks influences whether they enhance or dampen the transient warming response.

Long-term simulations extending beyond the standard 2100 time horizon reveal that carbon-climate feedbacks including methane cycles and permafrost thaw could amplify peak warming substantially above current projections[49]. A recent Earth system model simulating climate and carbon changes over the next millennium shows that under low-to-moderate emissions scenarios typically considered "safe," peak warming could exceed 2°C substantially when all carbon cycle feedbacks are included[49]. The study found that if climate sensitivity is higher than current best estimates of 3°C, carbon reduction must accelerate even more quickly than previously thought to keep warming below 2°C[49]. These results highlight how lagged responses and slowly developing feedbacks create risks beyond what conventional projections assuming faster equilibration would suggest. The long temporal lags inherent in permafrost thaw, soil carbon decomposition, and vegetation migration mean that warming committed by current emissions will continue manifesting through carbon cycle feedbacks long after those emissions stop.

## Carbon Residence Time as a Fundamental Source of Uncertainty

The time that carbon persists in different ecosystem pools before returning to the atmosphere fundamentally determines how carbon cycle feedbacks develop. If carbon remains in vegetation for only a few years, then changes in vegetation structure influence the atmospheric carbon balance within decades. But if carbon remains in soil for centuries or millennia, then changes in soil carbon stocks will influence the atmosphere on much longer timescales, creating lags between environmental forcing and carbon cycle response that could span multiple human generations. Understanding carbon residence times and their variation across ecosystems and environmental conditions is thus critical for predicting when and how carbon cycle feedbacks will develop. However, substantial uncertainty persists in estimates of carbon residence time, creating cascading uncertainty in long-term carbon projections.

Vegetation carbon residence time uncertainty alone explains more variation in global vegetation carbon projections than differences in vegetation productivity across global vegetation models[9]. When seven global vegetation models are analyzed, uncertainties in projected changes in carbon residence times account for 30% more variation in modeled vegetation carbon change than responses of net primary productivity alone, increasing to 151% more variation for non-hybrid model formulations[9]. This surprising finding indicates that most of the spread among models in projected future vegetation carbon stocks results not from disagreement about how much carbon plants will fix through photosynthesis, but from disagreement about how long that carbon will remain in vegetation before being respired or transferred to soil. Different models disagree about whether carbon residence times will increase or decrease under future climate change, with some models projecting declining residence times as warming stimulates mortality and turnover rates, while others project increasing residence times as elevated CO2 enhances tree survival rates[9]. These divergent projections about residence time generate vastly different implications for vegetation carbon stocks and the timing of carbon release to the atmosphere.

In forests specifically, biomass carbon turnover times vary almost twofold across different models, with projections ranging from 12.2 to 23.5 years for baseline 1985-2014 conditions[14]. Differences in phenological processes controlling allocation to leaves and fine roots are as important as differences in tree mortality for explaining variation in total turnover time among models[14]. This phenological variation means that different models represent how much carbon vegetation allocates to persistently stored woody biomass versus temporarily stored leaves and roots, fundamentally determining how long sequestered carbon remains in the biosphere. Under climate change, these residence times are projected to change in inconsistent ways across models, with some projecting substantial declines in turnover time under warming while others project increases[14]. Based on available simulations, it is not possible to draw robust conclusions about likely future changes in forest carbon turnover time, and thus biomass change, for different regions[14]. This epistemic uncertainty in forest carbon residence time propagates forward to create uncertainty in how much carbon forests will release to the atmosphere as they respond to climate change.

The uncertainty in carbon residence time affects not just the long-term magnitude of carbon stocks but also the timing of carbon release, which has implications for near-term carbon budgets and climate policy. A model predicting that forest biomass carbon averages twelve years of residence time would project that all of today's forest carbon stock would be recycled through respiration within approximately one century. A model predicting twenty-three years of residence time would project longer carbon retention, with substantial carbon still in forests two centuries from now. These different projections imply radically different implications for whether forests can serve as durable carbon sinks for climate mitigation. If residence times are short, forests cannot sequester carbon long-term because stored carbon rapidly cycles back to the atmosphere. If residence times are long, forests could serve as more durable carbon sinks. Given these high stakes and the substantial uncertainty in residence times, this parameter warrants much greater attention in model development.

## Data Assimilation and Parameter Uncertainty in Carbon Cycle Prediction

Efforts to reduce parameter uncertainty through data assimilation reveal that many carbon cycle parameters cannot be adequately constrained by available observations, creating a fundamental limit on model predictability. When Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods were applied to assimilate eight biometric datasets into a terrestrial ecosystem model, only less than half of thirty parameters could be constrained despite extensive observations including foliage biomass, wood biomass, fine root biomass, microbial biomass, litter fall, litter, soil carbon, and soil respiration[19]. The parameters that could not be constrained involved processes that were not directly measured or were poorly correlated with the available measurements. For parameters that were constrained, higher measurement errors led to higher uncertainties in parameter estimates and forecasted carbon pool sizes[19]. Moreover, the long-term predictions of slow-turnover pools were affected less by measurement errors than fast-turnover pools, but assimilated data contributed less information for pools with long residence times in long-term forecasts[19]. These results indicate that carbon residence time plays a key role in regulating how measurement uncertainties propagate through to model predictions, with the most uncertain forecasts occurring for slowly cycling pools that have minimal observational data constraining their dynamics.

The limited ability to constrain carbon cycle parameters from observational data reflects fundamental observability issues in the terrestrial carbon system. Direct observations of soil carbon stocks provide information about the current total carbon storage but not about the rates at which that carbon is being cycled through the system. Soil respiration measurements indicate gross decomposition rates but not net carbon change because they are confounded with inputs of fresh carbon from roots and fungi. Vegetation carbon stocks can be estimated from remote sensing and ground measurements, but translating observed carbon stocks to understand underlying processes requires additional assumptions about allocation patterns and turnover rates that cannot be directly observed. The result is that multiple different process-based models can reproduce the same observed carbon stocks through completely different mechanisms, making it impossible to uniquely constrain model parameters from stock measurements alone[19]. Only through measurement of actual processes—through techniques like radiocarbon dating, stable isotope tracing, and rate measurement experiments—can the underlying parameters be determined, and such process-level measurements exist for only a tiny fraction of global ecosystems.

Measurement errors in archived soil samples used for radiocarbon dating create additional uncertainty in quantifying soil carbon dynamics over decades. Soil carbon stocks measured at a single point in time integrate all past carbon cycling dynamics, making it difficult to decompose total stocks into components contributed by different time periods or processes. Radiocarbon measurements from the bomb pulse era provide a marker for tracking carbon turnover because nuclear weapons testing in the 1950s and 1960s created a spike in atmospheric radiocarbon that subsequently declined as nuclear weapons testing ceased[32]. Carbon that entered the soil during this bomb pulse will have different radiocarbon signatures depending on when it entered, allowing researchers to infer turnover rates. However, soil mixing processes, preferential preservation of certain carbon compounds, and heterogeneity in soil carbon across space and depth all complicate interpretation of radiocarbon measurements. Moreover, the bomb pulse method works well for carbon turned over on the scale of decades but provides less information about slowly cycling pools that have turnover times of centuries to millennia.

## Nonlinearities and Regime Shifts Amplifying Uncertainty

Lagged responses interact with nonlinearities in carbon cycle processes to create potential for regime shifts and sudden acceleration of carbon release that models inadequately represent. The response of soil carbon to warming appears nonlinear, with models showing that the magnitude of soil carbon sensitivity to temperature change can increase with the amount of warming experienced[39]. This increasing sensitivity with warming could reflect multiple mechanisms: at higher temperatures, the pool of easily decomposable carbon may be preferentially consumed, leaving behind only more persistent carbon that becomes increasingly vulnerable as temperature continues to rise; alternatively, soil moisture limitations at higher temperatures could become increasingly severe, creating threshold effects in decomposition; or changing vegetation community composition could favor plant types that allocate carbon differently. Regardless of mechanism, if soil carbon sensitivity to warming increases nonlinearly with temperature, then models using linear or mildly nonlinear temperature dependencies will systematically underestimate carbon losses at high warming levels.

The response of forest carbon to drought demonstrates potential nonlinearities where small additional stress pushes systems past critical thresholds. Different vegetation models show that although changes in net primary productivity (NPP) are relatively consistent across models, changes in vegetation carbon residence time and thus total vegetation carbon differ dramatically[9]. This divergence suggests that different models incorporate different assumptions about how tree mortality and recovery rates respond to moisture stress and drought. Models that assume mortality rates increase dramatically above critical drought thresholds show different residence time responses than models where mortality increases gradually with stress. Because drought stress in many regions is expected to increase under climate change, getting the nonlinearity of mortality response correct becomes increasingly important for accurate projections. A model that underestimates the slope of the mortality-drought relationship would underestimate how much vegetation carbon will be released as trees are killed by increased drought stress.

Phase transitions in ecosystem structure represent potential regime shifts where gradual changes in environmental conditions trigger sudden changes in ecosystem state and carbon dynamics. For example, gradual warming at high latitudes could trigger transition from tundra to boreal forest or from boreal forest to grassland, fundamentally altering the carbon storage capacity and turnover rate of the ecosystem[52]. These transitions often exhibit hysteresis, where the ecosystem does not transition back to the original state even if conditions reverse, because species migrations and changes in soil properties are slow to reverse once they have occurred. Lagged responses contribute to hysteresis effects because changes in soil carbon stocks, microbial community composition, and nutrient cycling all persist long after the environmental driver of the initial transition has reversed. A region that transitions from forest to grassland under warming may not return to forest even if temperatures subsequently cool, because the altered soil conditions created by grassland vegetation may prevent forest reestablishment. Such lagged hysteresis effects are very difficult to represent in models because they require tracking ecosystem history and state changes across decades to centuries.

## Implications for Projecting Long-Term Carbon Budgets and Climate Targets

The recognition that lagged responses substantially amplify prediction uncertainty has profound implications for how remaining carbon budgets are estimated and applied to climate policy. The remaining carbon budget—the amount of CO2 that can be emitted while limiting warming to a particular level like the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C or 2°C targets—depends on both current climate state and the projected response of the climate system to additional emissions[7]. When lagged carbon cycle feedbacks are included, the remaining carbon budget becomes substantially smaller because some of the projected warming from previously emitted CO2 will manifest through carbon cycle feedbacks that increase atmospheric CO2 concentrations decades into the future. If lagged responses also create substantially greater uncertainty in how much additional warming carbon cycle feedbacks will eventually contribute, then the range of plausible remaining carbon budgets expands significantly, creating challenges for policy decisions that depend on specific budget targets.

Current approaches to quantifying remaining carbon budgets typically employ a framework where the temperature response to cumulative carbon emissions (TCRE) is estimated from historical data and then applied to future scenarios[7]. However, if lagged responses create nonlinearities such that TCRE varies with the rate of emissions and the specific emissions pathway, then this simple linear extrapolation becomes problematic. Some research suggests that TCRE may not remain constant across very different emission scenarios, particularly at very low emission rates and very high emission rates where processes operating outside the historical range might become important[50]. If TCRE is pathway-dependent, then the remaining carbon budget becomes ambiguous because the same total allowed emissions would produce different warming amounts depending on the temporal pattern of emissions. Scenarios that emit carbon gradually over many decades might produce different atmospheric CO2 concentrations at a given cumulative emission level than scenarios that emit rapidly then drop sharply, due to differential ocean and land carbon uptake responding to the temporal pacing of emissions.

The asymmetry between emissions and carbon dioxide removals compounds these challenges. Recent evidence suggests that emissions of CO2 are more effective at raising atmospheric CO2 than CO2 removals are at lowering atmospheric CO2[52]. This asymmetry originates from state-dependencies and nonlinearities in carbon cycle processes, where the response of carbon sinks to increased CO2 differs from their response to decreased CO2. An ecosystem might reduce its carbon uptake rate substantially in response to increased atmospheric CO2 if nutrient limitation becomes apparent or if drought stress increases, but when atmospheric CO2 subsequently decreases, the ecosystem might not immediately increase its uptake rate due to lagged vegetation recovery and soil carbon accumulation processes. The asymmetry means that an extra amount of carbon dioxide removal is required to compensate for a positive emission of a given magnitude to attain the same change in atmospheric CO2[52]. Given that lagged responses and ecosystem recovery timescales are substantial, this asymmetry might be quite pronounced, implying that achieving a given atmospheric CO2 concentration through net negative emissions requires substantially more carbon removal than the magnitude of historical emissions that raised CO2 to current levels.

## Future Model Development and Observational Needs

Addressing the amplified uncertainty created by lagged soil-vegetation feedback responses requires fundamental restructuring of how carbon cycle processes are represented in Earth system models. Beyond tuning parameters within existing model structures, substantially improved model architectures must explicitly represent multiple soil carbon pools with different stabilization mechanisms and different responses to temperature, moisture, and nutrient availability[3][8][11]. Rather than first-order exponential decay structures, soil carbon models should represent the transition from labile to stabilized carbon pools and the time-dependent development of mineral associations that confer persistence. Similarly, vegetation models must move beyond treating productivity and mortality as instantaneous responses to current climate conditions, instead incorporating lagged phenological responses, demographic processes operating across multiple decades, and vegetation community shifts that respond to sustained climate conditions. These structural improvements will likely increase the spread among models in ensemble projections, at least initially, because different research groups will implement different mechanistic representations while knowledge remains incomplete. However, confronting models with diverse mechanisms against observational data will gradually reveal which mechanisms are closest to reality and build confidence in projections.

Long-term observational networks tracking ecosystem carbon dynamics across decadal timescales are critical for understanding lagged responses and constraining model parameters. The radiocarbon measurements spanning fifty years from Hubbard Brook demonstrate the value of multi-decadal time series for detecting changes in soil carbon dynamics and understanding how different soil horizons respond at different rates[32]. Expansion of such long-term monitoring to diverse ecosystems globally would provide essential data on how carbon residence times and pool-specific turnover rates respond to climate change. Particular emphasis should be placed on monitoring slowly cycling carbon pools in mineral soils and deep layers, because these pools are least affected by short-term fluctuations and most representative of long-term carbon cycle responses. However, radiocarbon measurements are labor-intensive and expensive, limiting the number of sites that can be monitored. Development of alternative techniques for estimating carbon residence times through stable isotope tracing, long-term incubation experiments, and spectroscopic characterization of carbon compounds could substantially expand the geographic coverage of observations.

Remote sensing technologies offer potential for detecting ecosystem state changes that might signal underlying changes in carbon cycling. Vegetation indices tracking changes in leaf area, biomass, and productivity across large regions provide spatial context for understanding how ecosystems are responding to climate change. However, remote sensing measures only the vegetation state and not directly the underlying carbon dynamics. Integration of remote sensing with ground-based measurements of carbon fluxes from eddy covariance towers creates a more complete picture of ecosystem responses. The Global Carbon Project's compilation of global carbon budget estimates integrating multiple data streams and models has revealed substantial divergences between different approaches to estimating land and ocean carbon sinks, particularly for the northern extra-tropics[10]. These divergences likely reflect different ways that models and observational approaches represent lagged responses and temporal dynamics. Continued investment in integrating satellite observations, ground-based measurements, and model simulations through data assimilation frameworks offers the most promising path forward for constraining lagged response estimates and reducing uncertainty.

## Conclusion

The lagged response of soil-vegetation feedback fundamentally amplifies prediction uncertainty in global carbon budget models through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Ecosystem carbon cycling operates across temporal scales ranging from hours for photosynthetic responses to millennia for soil carbon persistence, yet most models treat these diverse processes as if they respond instantaneously to environmental forcing. The accumulation of temporal lags at different scales creates emergent system dynamics that cannot be captured through simple addition of individual component responses. Evidence from long-term ecosystem observations documents that temperature effects on carbon cycling lag by months to half years, that vegetation responses to climate take up to six months in humid forests, and that soil carbon in different layers cycles on vastly different timescales from years to centuries. This temporal complexity interacts with fundamental uncertainties in carbon residence times, soil decomposition temperature dependencies, and vegetation mortality responses to create propagating uncertainty in long-term carbon projections.

Models attempting to represent these complex dynamics through simplified structures generate systematic biases where different models converge on similar global projections while operating through mechanistically different pathways. This false convergence masks underlying disagreements about how the terrestrial carbon system functions and conceals substantial uncertainty that becomes apparent when projections are disaggregated to regional scales or alternative scenarios. The structural uncertainties embedded in how models represent soil carbon turnover and vegetation dynamics dwarf parameter uncertainties, indicating that efforts to reduce uncertainty through better parameter estimation alone will achieve limited success. Instead, fundamental restructuring of model architectures to explicitly represent multiple soil carbon pools, lagged vegetation responses, and ecosystem state-dependent processes is required. Such restructuring will likely increase short-term spread among model projections as different mechanistic implementations are tested against observations, but will ultimately build greater confidence in predictions by better representing underlying processes.

The recognition that lagged responses amplify uncertainty has critical implications for climate policy and carbon budget targets. If carbon cycle feedbacks operating on multidecadal timescales will substantially increase cumulative warming beyond what models assuming faster equilibration would predict, then remaining carbon budgets for limiting warming must account for these long-term feedbacks. Current policy frameworks often work within relatively short timeframes—decades at most—whereas many carbon cycle processes operate on centennial to millennial timescales. Permafrost thaw, soil carbon decomposition in deep layers, and vegetation migration all respond to current climate forcing with substantial delays, creating commitment to future warming that extends far beyond the twenty-first century. Incorporating this long-term perspective into carbon budget accounting and climate policy frameworks remains an outstanding challenge that requires both improved scientific understanding and political will to act on information about impacts extending beyond typical policy planning horizons.
