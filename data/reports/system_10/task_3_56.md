# Phenotypic Plasticity Parameters of Invasive Species and Their Effects on Ecological Model Risk Prediction Accuracy

The accuracy of ecological models in predicting invasive species distributions and impacts remains fundamentally challenged by the complex nature of phenotypic plasticity—the ability of organisms to produce different phenotypes in response to environmental variation. While invasive species demonstrate significantly higher phenotypic plasticity than non-invasive species in many contexts[1][4], the relationship between this plasticity and actual invasion success is far more nuanced than previously assumed. Contemporary ecological niche models and species distribution models typically fail to account for the dynamic phenotypic responses of invasive species to novel environments, resulting in predictions that systematically underestimate or overestimate invasion potential across geographic and temporal scales. This comprehensive review examines how phenotypic plasticity parameters influence the predictive accuracy of ecological risk models, explores the mechanisms through which plasticity affects model performance, and identifies critical gaps between current modeling approaches and the biological reality of invasive species dynamics. The integration of phenotypic plasticity into ecological models represents a critical frontier in invasion biology, with implications for both scientific understanding and practical management of biological invasions worldwide.

## Phenotypic Plasticity as a Fundamental Mechanism of Invasive Species Success

Phenotypic plasticity represents a crucial yet often underappreciated dimension of invasive species ecology. Defined as the property of organisms to produce distinct phenotypes in response to environmental variation[9], phenotypic plasticity operates across multiple biological levels and trait categories. A comprehensive meta-analysis examining 75 invasive and non-invasive species pairs demonstrated that invasive species exhibit significantly higher phenotypic plasticity across growth, morphological, and physiological traits compared to their non-invasive counterparts[1]. The plasticity demonstrated by invasive species manifests most prominently in response to resource availability, with invasive species showing substantially greater variation in growth and biomass allocation when resources fluctuate[1]. However, this enhanced plasticity does not uniformly translate into superior fitness outcomes, as non-invasive species frequently maintain greater fitness homoeostasis across resource-limiting environments, suggesting that plasticity and fitness optimization represent distinct and sometimes competing selective pressures[1].

The mechanistic understanding of phenotypic plasticity has evolved significantly with the development of reaction norm frameworks that formalize the relationship between environmental conditions and phenotypic expression[19][22]. Reaction norms provide quantifiable representations of how traits change across environmental gradients, enabling researchers to partition phenotypic variation into components attributable to genetic differences, environmental influences, and their interactions. This framework proves particularly valuable for understanding invasive species because it permits explicit modeling of how individual genotypes respond to novel environmental conditions encountered during range expansion. The theoretical foundations suggest that phenotypic plasticity itself can evolve in response to environmental heterogeneity, with populations exposed to heterogeneous environments developing greater phenotypic flexibility than populations in homogeneous habitats[7]. This evolutionary potential means that invasive populations may increase their plasticity over successive generations as they colonize novel environments, fundamentally altering the relationship between initial introduction characteristics and subsequent invasion dynamics.

The complexity of phenotypic plasticity becomes apparent when examining environment- and trait-specific responses. Research comparing invasive and native plant species under different environmental conditions reveals that the phenotypic plasticity hypothesis—the prediction that invasive species universally exhibit greater plasticity—does not hold across all contexts[25][33]. When exposed to nutrient enrichment, invasive plants showed greater plasticity specifically in leaf mass fraction and root mass fraction compared to natives[33]. However, this advantage disappeared entirely under water or light limitation treatments[33]. Such findings suggest that invasive species possess specialized plasticity in response to particular resource conditions rather than exhibiting generalized plastic responses across all environmental dimensions. This specificity has profound implications for modeling, as it indicates that models must capture trait-by-environment interactions rather than assuming uniform plasticity across all traits and conditions.

## Traditional Species Distribution Models and Their Fundamental Limitations for Invasive Species

Species distribution models have become the dominant tool for predicting invasive species ranges and assessing invasion risk, yet their theoretical foundations create systematic biases when applied to invasive species dynamics. Traditional correlative species distribution models operate on the assumption that species exist in equilibrium with their environment—that is, they occupy all available suitable habitats while avoiding unsuitable ones[37][50][55]. This equilibrium assumption proves problematic for invasive species in multiple ways. During early invasion stages, species may occupy only a fraction of their potential environmental range due to dispersal limitations, establishment lag, or ongoing range expansion dynamics[27][28][37]. Conversely, invasive species may temporarily occupy unsuitable environments if they arrive through human-mediated transport and establishment, violating the fundamental assumption that observed occurrences represent true environmental suitability[27][39].

The predictive accuracy of species distribution models varies considerably depending on the modeling technique employed and the validation approach selected. When MaxEnt and Biomod2 ensemble models were compared using occurrence data from an invasive insect species, MaxEnt achieved only 3.72 percent accuracy in predicting suitable habitat distribution when trained on data from the invaded range alone, while the ensemble model achieved 53.04 percent accuracy using the same data[2]. However, when global occurrence data from both native and invaded ranges were incorporated, MaxEnt accuracy improved to 79.05 percent and the ensemble model reached 95.44 percent accuracy[2]. This dramatic improvement demonstrates that data comprehensiveness fundamentally constrains model performance, but also reveals a critical problem: models trained on native range data may not accurately predict invaded range potential if niche shifts or evolutionary changes have occurred[37][56][59].

Field validation studies provide sobering assessments of model performance for invasive species. When species distribution model predictions were field-validated for an invasive mosquito species using independent occurrence data at range edges, different modeling techniques produced substantially divergent predictions[50]. MaxEnt and generalized additive models performed better in cross-validation and field validation compared to Random Forest and boosted regression tree approaches[50]. More troublingly, models trained on early-stage invasion data frequently underestimated the later realized range expansion of invasive species, suggesting that models systematically fail to capture the full potential distribution until extensive spatial spread has already occurred[37][50]. The implications are stark: ecological models designed to predict invasion risk during early stages—when management interventions could prove most effective—frequently lack the predictive accuracy necessary to guide decision-making.

The niche conservatism assumption embedded in most species distribution models presents another critical limitation. This assumption proposes that species maintain similar environmental niches between their native and invasive ranges. Yet empirical evidence demonstrates inconsistent patterns of niche conservatism, with some invasive species maintaining their native niches while others exhibit substantial climatic niche shifts in invaded ranges[37][56][59]. When invasive species expand their climatic niche in invaded regions, models calibrated exclusively on native range data systematically underestimate the potential invasive distribution[37][56]. Conversely, when species undergo niche shifts, including native range data in model calibration may produce overpredictions by incorporating environmental conditions no longer suitable for the now-adapted invasive population[37]. The decision regarding which data to use for model training—native range only, invaded range only, or both—lacks a principled theoretical foundation and must be made on a species-by-species basis, undermining the generalizability of modeling approaches[27][39].

## Incorporating Phenotypic Plasticity into Contemporary Distribution Models

Recognition of phenotypic plasticity's importance has motivated the development of novel modeling approaches that explicitly incorporate trait variation and plastic responses. ΔTraitSDMs represent an emerging generation of models that explain species distributions based on phenotypic plasticity and local adaptation of fitness-related traits measured across broad geographical gradients[10][32][35]. These models leverage data from common garden experiments—standardized growing conditions applied to individuals from multiple populations—to quantify how traits and trait plasticity vary geographically. By incorporating intraspecific trait variation alongside environmental variables, ΔTraitSDMs can project potential species ranges under novel climate conditions while accounting for the adaptive capacity of populations to adjust their phenotypes in response to changing environments[10][32][35].

The performance of ΔTraitSDMs generally delivers a less alarming assessment of climate change impacts than traditional species distribution models, indicating that phenotypic plasticity may enable population persistence under novel conditions[10][32]. This occurs because ΔTraitSDMs explicitly model how populations with greater phenotypic plasticity can maintain fitness across broader environmental ranges than less plastic populations. However, the practical application of ΔTraitSDMs to invasive species prediction remains limited because they require extensive phenotypic data collected across environmental gradients—data that is rarely available for newly introduced invasive species. The logistical and financial constraints of collecting such data mean that ΔTraitSDMs have been primarily applied to tree species in temperate regions where institutional research infrastructure supports long-term common garden experiments[10][32].

An alternative approach involves mechanistic niche models that integrate species' physiological performance across environmental gradients using process-based calculations[27][28][30]. Rather than statistically correlating species occurrences with environmental conditions, mechanistic niche models predict where species can exist by calculating whether particular locations provide microclimate conditions within the species' physiological tolerance ranges[30]. These models explicitly incorporate how organism morphology, behavior, and physiology interact with physical environments to determine survival and reproduction[30]. For invasive species, mechanistic niche models offer advantages because they can predict distributions under novel environmental conditions without requiring the species to have previously occupied those conditions[27][28][30]. However, mechanistic models require detailed knowledge of species physiology and typically demand more input data than correlative approaches, limiting their application to well-studied invasive species[27].

## The Complex Relationship Between Phenotypic Plasticity and Model Accuracy

The relationship between phenotypic plasticity and the predictive accuracy of ecological models proves more intricate than simple intuition might suggest. While one might assume that higher phenotypic plasticity in invasive species would lead to broader predicted distributions and greater model uncertainty, the empirical evidence reveals multiple countervailing mechanisms. When traditional species distribution models were compared across multiple invasive species, trait values—rather than trait plasticity—more effectively predicted invasive species' performance in changing environments[15][18]. This finding suggests that the absolute expression level of a trait may better determine invasion success than the species' ability to modify that trait in response to environmental conditions[15][18]. For example, an invasive plant with inherently high growth rate might succeed regardless of plasticity level, while a native plant with lower baseline growth rate might not benefit from plasticity in compensating for competitive disadvantage.

The environment-dependent nature of phenotypic plasticity further complicates model incorporation. Research demonstrating that invasive plant species exhibit greater plasticity specifically in response to nutrient enrichment but not to water stress or light limitation[25][33] reveals that a single plasticity parameter cannot characterize invasive species responses across all environmental conditions. This specificity presents a fundamental challenge for ecological models: accurately incorporating phenotypic plasticity would require not simply including a plasticity variable, but rather specifying trait-specific plasticity responses for each relevant environmental dimension. The number of parameters required for such detailed models increases exponentially with the number of traits and environmental variables considered, creating overfitting risks and data requirements that exceed available information for most invasive species[27].

Phenotypic plasticity can also introduce unpredictability into invasive species distributions, potentially decreasing rather than increasing model accuracy. Theoretical work examining the evolution of plasticity reveals that in spatially heterogeneous environments, the balance between phenotypic plasticity and local genetic adaptation determines population dynamics[8]. Environments characterized by predictable variation select for phenotypic plasticity, whereas variable and uncertain environments select against plasticity in favor of local adaptation[8]. Critically, invasive species colonizing novel environments often experience uncertainty about future environmental conditions they will encounter, potentially favoring evolution toward local adaptation rather than plasticity[8]. When introduced populations undergo rapid adaptation to novel environments, model predictions based on pre-invasion characteristics become increasingly inaccurate[31]. The genetic architecture underlying dispersal and local adaptation traits determines the evolvability of these traits and thus the predictability of range expansions; populations with gene-regulatory network architecture supporting greater sensitivity to mutation demonstrate less predictable invasion dynamics than populations with simpler additive genetic architecture[31].

## Adaptation and Niche Shifts: When Plasticity Masks Evolutionary Change

The distinction between phenotypic plasticity—an organism's capacity to adjust its phenotype within a single lifetime—and local adaptation—the evolution of genotypes better suited to specific environments—presents a critical challenge for invasive species modeling. When invasive populations undergo rapid evolutionary adaptation to novel environments, model predictions assuming niche conservatism systematically fail. Evidence demonstrates that invasive species can experience substantial shifts in their climatic niche between native and invaded ranges[56][59], with many species not yet fully occupying available suitable habitat in their invaded range[56]. These niche shifts arise through multiple mechanisms, including genetic drift, natural selection acting on introduced genetic variation, and hybridization among multiple introduced populations[56]. Notably, distinguishing between niche expansion driven by phenotypic plasticity versus niche expansion driven by evolutionary adaptation proves empirically challenging from distribution data alone[56].

The pre-adaptation hypothesis—the idea that invasive species pre-evolved characteristics in their native range that predispose them to success in invaded ranges—offers another confounding mechanism. Examination of tench populations reveals that introduced populations with varying genetic diversity and different genetic compositions have established successfully across strikingly different climatic and ecological conditions, suggesting that lack of prior native-range adaptation to invaded habitats may not consistently hinder invasion success[34]. This pattern indicates that invasive populations may rapidly evolve adaptation to novel environments independent of their previous native-range history[34]. However, populations exposed to high environmental heterogeneity in their source regions demonstrate greater phenotypic plasticity, suggesting that heterogeneous source environments may select for plastic genotypes predisposed to success in colonizing equally heterogeneous novel environments[7].

## Intraspecific Trait Variation and Population-Level Heterogeneity

Most traditional species distribution models treat species as biologically homogeneous units, assuming that all individuals and populations respond identically to environmental conditions[54]. This assumption proves increasingly problematic as research accumulates showing that intraspecific trait variation—variation in traits within species across populations and among individuals—substantially influences how species respond to environmental change[26][29]. Intraspecific trait variation encompasses both phenotypic plasticity (variation within individuals across environments) and local genetic adaptation (variation among populations), creating multidimensional trait spaces that cannot be adequately captured by single species-level parameters[26]. Some traits exhibit greater variability within species than among species, suggesting that ignoring within-species variation introduces substantial prediction error[23][29]. When incorporated into ecosystem function models, intraspecific trait variation substantially improves predictions of ecosystem-level processes like decomposition, suggesting it likely also improves predictions of species interactions driving invasion success[26].

Populations of the same species occupying different regions of the invaded range may differ substantially in their phenotypic characteristics and capacity for further range expansion, yet conventional species distribution models cannot represent such population-level heterogeneity[27][28][39]. Multiple introductions of the same species can result in populations differing in genetic composition, hybridization status, and evolutionary history, each potentially exhibiting different invasion potentials[27][28][39]. When ecological niche models for an invasive turtle species were trained on data from different regions of the invaded range, models generated contrasting predictions of expansion potential, emphasizing the critical importance of recognizing intraspecific population differentiation[27]. For invasive species management, this heterogeneity creates challenges for predicting the trajectory of invasion: a population of an invasive species established in one region may exhibit dramatically different expansion potential than a population of the same species established elsewhere, despite identical species identity.

## The Equilibrium Assumption: When Invasive Species Violate Fundamental Model Assumptions

Testing the equilibrium assumption—the core prediction that species occupy all suitable habitat—through retrospective validation reveals that invasive species systematically violate this assumption for extended periods. Examination of an invasive plant species with 130 years of occurrence records demonstrates that even well-established invasive species have not reached equilibrium with their environment[55]. Models trained on early-stage invasion data (2004-2010) demonstrated limited ability to predict later-stage invasive distributions (2011-2015) for an invasive wasp species, with predicted suitability of evaluation data points lower than predicted suitability for calibration data[37]. This systematic mismatch indicates that invasive species continue expanding into areas with lower predicted suitability than their current range, suggesting that dispersal limitation, demographic stochasticity, and ongoing adaptation continue driving range expansion even when species have had decades to occupy all available suitable habitat[37].

The temporal dynamics of invasive species expansion reveal that phenotypic plasticity interacts with dispersal and establishment processes in ways models cannot capture[27][39]. A newly arriving invasive individual with greater phenotypic plasticity might establish successfully in a marginal microenvironment, subsequently serving as a dispersal source for populations colonizing geographically adjacent areas[27]. Over generations, such processes could facilitate range expansion into areas predicted unsuitable by models based on early invasion stage data, as populations accumulate adaptive mutations and plastically adjust to local conditions. Conversely, if the initial introduced individual(s) lacked phenotypic plasticity for the specific conditions encountered, establishment might fail despite those conditions falling within the species' fundamental niche, resulting in erroneous model predictions of unsuitable habitat preventing establishment[27].

## Data Biases and Occurrence Record Limitations

The practical application of species distribution models to invasive species faces fundamental challenges from systematic biases in occurrence data. Sampling biases—arising from differential survey effort in different regions, tendency of researchers to focus on areas of concentrated invasion, and inconsistent documentation of non-invasive species occurrences—systematically distort the relationship between species occurrences and environmental conditions[40]. When species distribution models are trained on biased occurrence data, they learn the sampling bias pattern as much as the true environmental preferences[27][39][40]. Citizen science data can help address sampling biases by providing occurrence information from regions surveyed by non-experts, but citizen data introduce their own biases related to population density and accessibility[40].

A critical but often overlooked problem involves distinguishing between established populations, sink populations, and ephemeral occurrences in invasive species occurrence datasets[27][39]. An invasive species occurrence record might represent a long-established, self-sustaining population; a recent arrival not yet established; a population dependent on repeated human reintroductions; or a transient individual unlikely to produce offspring[27][39]. Traditional occurrence-only species distribution models cannot distinguish among these categories, treating all occurrences as equivalent indicators of environmental suitability[27][39]. For invasive species with strong phenotypic plasticity, sink populations might persist in apparently unsuitable environments through repeated reproduction of plastic phenotypes that survive but do not represent true evolutionary adaptation[27][39]. This means that occurrence data from sink populations could mislead models into predicting much broader suitable distributions than would actually support population growth and persistence.

The number and spatial coverage of occurrence records constrains model accuracy in ways particularly problematic for invasive species[2]. Early-stage invasive species typically have relatively few occurrence records concentrated in areas near initial introduction points, violating the data comprehensiveness requirements for accurate species distribution modeling[2]. Using only invaded-range data results in systematic underprediction of suitable habitat because the invasive range does not yet represent the full potential distribution[2]. Using only native-range data may similarly fail if phenotypic plasticity or evolutionary adaptation enables occupation of conditions absent from the native range[2]. The optimal solution—using both native and invaded range data combined with mechanistic knowledge of niche shifts—requires knowledge typically unavailable until extensive invasion has occurred[2].

## Implications for Risk Assessment and Management Decision-Making

The limitations of current ecological models in accurately predicting invasive species distributions have direct implications for risk assessment frameworks designed to prevent invasions and prioritize management resources. Traditional quantitative risk assessment approaches rely on species distribution models to identify areas at highest invasion risk and allocate management resources accordingly[46]. When models systematically underestimate the potential invasive range due to failure to account for phenotypic plasticity and evolutionary adaptation, risk assessment frameworks may neglect critical areas requiring prevention efforts[46]. Conversely, overprediction of suitable habitat could allocate scarce management resources to prevention efforts in areas where invasion risk remains minimal[46]. The stage-specificity of invasion processes means that different mechanisms operate at different points during invasion establishment and spread; early-stage prevention efforts must rely on risk models based on pre-invasion characteristics, yet these characteristics may poorly predict success in actual colonization contexts[46][57].

Phenotypic plasticity introduces particular complications for biosecurity decisions regarding species importation and risk categorization. A species might be assessed as low risk based on laboratory measurements of its ecological niche in the native range, yet possess substantial phenotypic plasticity that enables successful establishment in novel environments qualitatively different from its native habitat[27][30][46]. Conversely, a species with high phenotypic plasticity might be considered high risk despite characteristics that would support only marginal populations, if the assessment did not account for how plasticity permits persistence in suboptimal conditions[27][30][46]. The environment- and trait-specific nature of phenotypic plasticity means that generic risk assessment frameworks cannot adequately capture plasticity without detailed knowledge of which traits exhibit plasticity under which environmental conditions—knowledge rarely available for species under consideration for importation[25][33][46].

## Future Directions: Integrating Physiological, Genetic, and Ecological Perspectives

Advancing the accuracy of ecological models for invasive species risk prediction requires fundamental integration of physiological mechanisms, genomic information, and ecological dynamics into unified modeling frameworks. Mechanistic niche models incorporating physiological constraints represent a promising avenue, yet their practical application requires substantial investment in determining physiological tolerance limits across the full range of traits determining survival and reproduction[27][30]. Recent developments in comparative physiology and environmental physiology provide increasingly powerful methods for characterizing these relationships, but technological capacity to measure physiology now outpaces the computational resources dedicated to incorporating physiological data into spatially explicit models[27][30].

Genomic approaches offer additional promise for incorporating adaptive potential directly into ecological models. Identifying genetic variants associated with phenotypic plasticity and local adaptation enables prediction of how populations might evolve in response to novel environments[31][34][57]. Rather than treating invasive populations as fixed entities with constant characteristics, genomically-informed models could predict how populations evolve over generations, how standing genetic variation constraints adaptive potential, and how multiple introductions might introduce genetic variation enabling rapid adaptation[31][34][57]. However, such approaches remain largely theoretical; few invasive species have been studied with sufficient genomic depth to enable robust predictions of evolution during range expansion[31][34][57].

Common garden experiments conducted across environmental gradients represent another critical avenue for improving model accuracy[14][32][35]. By growing populations from across a species' native and invaded ranges under standardized conditions, researchers can partition phenotypic variation into genetic and plasticity components, quantify reaction norms, and identify local adaptation[14][32][35]. When coupled with species distribution models, common garden data enable development of ΔTraitSDMs that explicitly account for how trait expression and plasticity vary across geographic and environmental space[14][32][35]. However, common garden experiments require multi-year commitment and substantial resources, limiting their application to species of particular management concern or academic interest[14][32][35].

## Complex Trait-by-Environment Interactions and Modeling Complexity

The fundamental complexity revealed by research on phenotypic plasticity—that plasticity is environment-specific, trait-specific, and context-dependent—creates a challenging paradox for ecological modeling. Each additional layer of biological realism that might improve model accuracy increases the number of parameters requiring estimation, ultimately creating overfitting risks where models describe training data perfectly but generalize poorly to novel situations[27][39][46]. Species distribution models must balance biological realism against parsimony and available data, a balance that shifts depending on model application[27][39][46]. For invasive species risk assessment during early invasion stages, when data are limited, simple correlative models may outperform complex mechanistic models that require more parameters than available data can reasonably estimate[27][39][46]. However, for predicting range expansion in well-documented invasions or for climate change projections, more complex models accounting for evolutionary and plastic responses may better capture true dynamics[27][39][46].

The multiplicity of environmental variables influencing invasive species distributions—temperature, precipitation, soil composition, competitive environment, disease and herbivory pressure, human land use patterns—means that incorporating plasticity parameters for all relevant traits across all relevant environmental dimensions rapidly becomes computationally intractable[27][46]. Ecological models must make strategic choices regarding which environmental variables to prioritize, which traits to measure for plasticity, and how to weight different sources of information[27][46]. These choices can substantially affect model predictions, yet they are often made pragmatically based on data availability rather than theoretical principles[27][46]. The consequence is model heterogeneity where different analyses of the same invasive species generate divergent predictions based on different modeling choices, making it difficult to reach consensus on invasion risk[27][39][46].

## Conclusion: Bridging the Gap Between Theory and Practice

Phenotypic plasticity fundamentally shapes invasive species responses to novel environments and directly influences the predictive accuracy of ecological models used for risk assessment and management planning. Yet the relationship between phenotypic plasticity and model accuracy proves far more complex than simple theoretical expectations would predict. While invasive species generally demonstrate higher phenotypic plasticity than non-invasive species, this plasticity does not uniformly enhance invasion success and frequently proves insufficient to overcome other constraints on population growth[1][15][18]. Traditional species distribution models that fail to account for phenotypic plasticity systematically mispredict invasive species distributions, yet incorporating plasticity parameters into models introduces additional complexity and data requirements that frequently exceed available information during early invasion stages when management intervention could prove most effective[2][37][50].

The emerging generation of trait-based models and mechanistic niche models offers genuine promise for improving prediction accuracy by explicitly incorporating how phenotypic plasticity and local adaptation enable populations to persist across broader environmental ranges[10][27][30][32][35]. However, the practical implementation of such approaches remains limited by data requirements, computational complexity, and the need for detailed knowledge of trait-environment relationships often unavailable for newly invasive species[10][27][30][32][35]. The field requires continued investment in common garden experiments, genomic characterization of adaptive potential, and mechanistic physiological studies to provide the foundational data enabling next-generation predictive models[10][27][30][32][35].

For management practitioners confronting invasion decisions with limited information and uncertain predictions, acknowledging model limitations becomes paramount. Rather than treating species distribution model predictions as definitive risk assessments, practitioners should employ models as decision-support tools while recognizing substantial underlying uncertainty[27][37][46][50]. Field validation of model predictions using independent occurrence data provides critical reality-checks on model accuracy and can identify contexts where models systematically fail[50][53]. Adaptive management approaches that initiate control efforts while monitoring outcomes and updating predictive models as new information accumulates represent practical frameworks for managing invasive species despite imperfect predictive tools[46][53].

The challenge of predicting invasive species dynamics represents a critical frontier where fundamental biological understanding must integrate with applied management need. As global trade, climate change, and habitat modification continue driving biological invasions at accelerating rates, improving the accuracy of risk prediction models becomes increasingly urgent. Achieving such improvement requires embracing the complexity of phenotypic plasticity, recognizing the multifaceted roles it plays in invasion ecology, and developing modeling frameworks sophisticated enough to capture this complexity without exceeding practical implementation capacity. The synthesis of ecological niche modeling, mechanistic physiological approaches, genomic prediction, and adaptive management represents the most promising path forward for improving invasion risk prediction accuracy while acknowledging the inherent uncertainty in predicting how organisms with substantial phenotypic plasticity will respond to novel environments they have never encountered.
