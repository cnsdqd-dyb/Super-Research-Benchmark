# Indications for rTMS Treatment in Depression: Balancing Therapeutic Response Thresholds with Seizure Risk Safety Boundaries

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has emerged as a critical therapeutic option for patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) when conventional treatments fail, yet its clinical application requires careful navigation between achieving meaningful clinical improvement and maintaining safety boundaries, particularly regarding seizure risk. The therapeutic decision to initiate rTMS in depressed patients involves weighing the probability of achieving clinically significant response and remission against the potential for adverse events, with seizure induction representing the most serious but fortunately rare complication. This comprehensive analysis examines how medical professionals and regulatory agencies determine appropriate indications for rTMS treatment while simultaneously establishing and maintaining safety parameters that minimize the risk of treatment-emergent seizures and other adverse effects.

## Defining Treatment-Resistant Depression and FDA-Approved Indications for rTMS

Treatment-resistant depression represents a significant clinical challenge, affecting a substantial proportion of patients with major depressive disorder despite adequate pharmaceutical intervention. The formal definition of treatment resistance varies across regulatory agencies and clinical settings, but generally requires evidence of inadequate response to one or more adequate trials of antidepressant medication in the current depressive episode.[1][4] The FDA's approval of rTMS for depression in 2008 was based on evidence demonstrating that approximately 15 to 33 percent of patients with MDD do not respond to multiple antidepressant drugs, creating a substantial population for whom alternative treatment modalities become medically necessary.[13] Treatment resistance can be defined by failure of at least one antidepressant trial of adequate dose and duration, though most clinical definitions and insurance coverage policies require evidence of failure with two or more antidepressant medications from different pharmacological classes to establish true treatment resistance.[4][16]

The FDA has progressively expanded the approved indications for rTMS since its initial clearance in 2008. The original indication included treatment-resistant depression in adults defined as failure to achieve satisfactory improvement from prior antidepressant medication in the current episode.[1] Subsequently, the regulatory agency granted FDA clearance for rTMS in obsessive-compulsive disorder in 2018, with the deep TMS system receiving specific approval for this indication.[1][30] The FDA also approved rTMS for treating pain associated with certain migraine headaches and approved TMS devices for helping individuals stop smoking when standard treatments have not proven effective.[1] Most recently, in 2021 and 2022, the FDA cleared two different rTMS device protocols specifically for treating depression with comorbid anxiety symptoms, recognizing that approximately half of patients with MDD also meet criteria for at least one anxiety disorder and that this comorbidity has historically presented challenges for treatment response.[44][50]

The clinical definition of when rTMS becomes medically necessary has evolved substantially as evidence accumulated regarding its efficacy in various patient populations. The Veterans Health Administration and other major healthcare systems have established specific criteria indicating that rTMS is medically necessary for MDD when all of the following conditions are met: confirmed diagnosis of MDD by a qualified mental health professional with at least moderate level of documented depression, and documented failure of at least one adequate course of antidepressant treatment with inadequate improvement.[4] Additionally, the VA criteria recognize that rTMS may be indicated when patients experience inability to tolerate antidepressant medications due to intolerable side effects not expected to diminish with continued administration, or when documented history demonstrates successful prior response to TMS in a previous depressive episode with at least 50 percent improvement on a standard depression rating scale.[4] These criteria reflect the medical consensus that rTMS should be employed as an adjunctive or alternative treatment specifically for patients who have demonstrated inadequate response to or cannot tolerate more conventional first-line interventions.

## Therapeutic Response Thresholds: Defining Meaningful Clinical Outcomes

The concept of therapeutic response in rTMS treatment has been standardized across clinical research and practice through specific quantitative metrics that allow clinicians to assess whether treatment objectives have been achieved. Clinical response is typically defined as a reduction of at least 50 percent in the baseline depression rating scale score, with this threshold representing a clinically meaningful improvement that correlates with patients' subjective experience of symptom amelioration and functional recovery.[5][27][45] In contrast, remission refers to an even greater therapeutic achievement, generally defined as depression rating scale scores falling below established cutoff values indicating minimal to no symptom severity, with different scales employing different thresholds to define remission status.[2][27]

Meta-analytic evidence synthesizing numerous randomized controlled trials demonstrates that rTMS achieves response rates ranging from approximately 37 to 54 percent in treatment-resistant depression populations, while remission rates range from approximately 16 to 36 percent depending on the specific patient population studied, rTMS protocol employed, and depression rating scale used to measure outcomes.[2][5][9][12] A comprehensive meta-analysis of 19 studies evaluating rTMS efficacy in TRD found that nearly 40 percent of TRD patients responded to adjunctive active rTMS treatment, compared to just over 10 percent in the sham-treated placebo group, indicating that rTMS-treated patients were approximately 2.25 times more likely to achieve response than those receiving placebo stimulation.[2] When examining remission rates specifically, the same analysis revealed that approximately 36 percent of TRD patients achieved complete remission with active rTMS treatment compared to barely 8 percent in the sham group, indicating that treated patients were nearly three times more likely to achieve complete remission status than placebo recipients.[2]

Recent naturalistic clinical experience suggests that these therapeutic response thresholds may be achievable more rapidly than previously expected. Researchers at UCLA Health conducting a precision medicine approach to rTMS treatment found that 54 percent of 708 patients exhibited clinical response defined as at least 50 percent improvement when examined using multiple depression rating scales, with most patients reporting initial symptom improvements within one week of starting treatment despite the fact that the full therapeutic benefit continues to accumulate over the entire treatment course of several weeks.[5] This rapid onset of response represents a significant clinical advantage of rTMS compared to antidepressant medications, which typically require two to three weeks minimum to demonstrate noticeable clinical effects. The UCLA research additionally identified that early improvements reported within five to ten treatment sessions significantly predicted sustained and enhanced response throughout the complete treatment course, providing clinicians with an objective marker allowing them to assess whether continuing the full planned treatment course would likely prove beneficial for individual patients.[5]

Response thresholds also vary depending on the specific depression rating scale employed and the clinical population being treated. Among youth aged ten to twenty-five years with depression, meta-analytic evidence demonstrates that rTMS produces significant reductions in depression severity with effect sizes comparable to those observed in adult populations, with evidence of therapeutic onset as early as the second week of treatment, and the capacity to achieve response and remission rates in the range of 30 to 50 percent and 20 to 35 percent, respectively.[6] For patients with bipolar depression, which has historically been considered a more treatment-resistant depression subtype, recent systematic review evidence demonstrates response and remission rates of approximately 46.81 and 28.25 percent respectively, demonstrating that these therapeutic thresholds are achievable in this traditionally more difficult-to-treat population.[55]

## Understanding Seizure Risk and Safety Parameters in rTMS Administration

The most serious and concerning adverse effect associated with rTMS treatment is the induction of seizures, which represents the primary safety boundary limiting treatment intensity and stimulation parameters. However, comprehensive review of extensive clinical experience with rTMS demonstrates that seizure induction remains an extraordinarily rare event when treatment protocols adhere to established safety guidelines and patients are carefully screened for seizure risk factors. Contemporary systematic review evidence examining 93 placebo-controlled clinical trials including 4,144 total participants found an extremely low seizure risk of 0.1 percent in the active TMS-treated group and 0.2 percent in the placebo-treated group, translating to fewer than two seizures per ten thousand treatment sessions.[38] Other analyses report seizure rates ranging from approximately 0.003 to 0.087 percent, with most reported cases occurring when stimulation parameters exceeded established safety guidelines or in patients with pre-existing seizure risk factors.[11][28]

The mechanisms underlying rTMS-induced seizure risk relate to fundamental neurophysiological principles governing cortical excitability. Seizures result from hypersynchronous discharges of neuronal populations in gray matter, typically arising from an imbalance favoring excitatory over inhibitory synaptic activity.[11][28] High-frequency rTMS delivered at relatively high stimulus intensities with short intervals between stimulus trains can induce heightened cortical excitability that, in susceptible individuals, may progress to seizure activity.[3][11] The temporal pattern of stimulation proves critical to seizure risk, with intervals between repetitive stimulus trains of less than one second significantly increasing seizure probability, whereas intervals exceeding twenty seconds appear extremely safe and allow dissipation of excitatory neuronal activity.[41]

The International Safety Consensus Group established the Wassermann safety guidelines and subsequent updated Rossi safety parameters defining a three-dimensional safety space specified by stimulus intensity, frequency, and duration of stimulation trains.[39][42] These guidelines specify that for conventional rTMS protocols using figure-eight coils, high-frequency stimulation should not exceed 20 Hz with maximum train durations of 10 seconds and interstimulus intervals between trains of at least one to five seconds depending on the exact frequency employed.[7][39] Stimulation intensity is typically calibrated to individual resting motor threshold, with conventional protocols utilizing 80 to 120 percent of maximum stimulator output or 100 to 120 percent of the individual's motor threshold.[1][17] When applied within these established safety parameters to patients without pre-existing seizure risk factors, the seizure risk becomes negligible.[3][38]

Risk factors significantly increasing seizure probability during rTMS include previous personal or family history of epilepsy, structural brain lesions such as stroke or traumatic brain injury, medications that lower seizure threshold particularly certain antidepressants and antipsychotics, sleep deprivation, alcohol or substance use, and neuropsychiatric conditions including bipolar disorder and severe depression itself.[11][25][28][35][38][41] The temporal distribution of rTMS-induced seizures demonstrates that 62 percent of all reported seizures occurred during the patient's first exposure to TMS, with 75 percent occurring within the first three treatment sessions, indicating that heightened vigilance during initial treatment sessions provides important safety safeguards.[28] However, most TMS-induced seizures when they do occur are self-limiting, brief in duration, and similar in character to the patient's baseline seizure characteristics if a seizure history exists, and have not been associated with serious complications or long-term neurological sequelae in the clinical literature.[11][28][38]

Specific medications warrant particular consideration regarding seizure risk interaction with rTMS. Despite historical concerns about combining bupropion, an antidepressant with acknowledged seizure risk even when used alone, with rTMS treatment, comprehensive literature review found zero cases of TMS-induced seizures in patients taking bupropion at the time of seizure occurrence, suggesting that the combination does not substantially increase seizure risk beyond that associated with either treatment individually.[8] Similarly, contemporary evidence demonstrates that patients successfully continue most antidepressant and antipsychotic medications during rTMS without substantial increase in seizure risk, provided that stimulation parameters remain within established safety guidelines and patients are carefully monitored.[35][38]

## Patient Selection and Risk Stratification: Clinical Implementation of Safety Boundaries

The practical clinical application of rTMS indications requires careful pre-treatment evaluation and risk stratification to identify patients most likely to benefit from rTMS while identifying those for whom seizure risk or other safety concerns might outweigh potential therapeutic benefits. The TMS Adult Safety Screen questionnaire and similar pre-treatment screening instruments systematically assess for absolute contraindications and relative risk factors that should prompt careful clinical consideration and possible modifications to standard rTMS protocols.[35]

Absolute contraindications to rTMS treatment include active epilepsy or uncontrolled seizure disorders, certain metallic implants in or near the head including ferromagnetic aneurysm clips, cardiac pacemakers, cochlear implants, and deep brain stimulation devices that could malfunction or be adversely affected by magnetic fields.[1][15][25] Additionally, metal fragments remaining from traumatic head injuries represent absolute contraindications due to potential for dangerous heating or movement of the foreign material within the skull.[15][25] Active brain tumors and other space-occupying lesions generally represent contraindications due to uncertain effects of rTMS on tumor biology and risk of increased intracranial pressure.[15][25] Pregnancy represents a relative contraindication due to the lack of adequate safety research in this population rather than demonstrated harmful effects.[1][15][25]

Relative contraindications require careful clinical evaluation of the risk-benefit ratio and possible protocol modifications rather than absolute exclusion from rTMS treatment. History of head trauma with loss of consciousness, even remote in time, increases seizure risk and necessitates heightened safety precautions, though rTMS has been successfully administered to patients with such histories when benefits are deemed to outweigh risks.[28][38][41] Recent traumatic brain injury presents particular caution given evidence that TBI is associated with increased risk of both early and late spontaneous seizures independent of rTMS.[11] Patients with psychotic depression or active suicidal ideation warrant consideration of alternative treatments with more established evidence in these specific presentations, such as electroconvulsive therapy, which carries both greater efficacy for acute suicidality and psychotic depression but also greater risks of cognitive side effects and requires anesthesia.[37][48]

Severity of baseline depression represents an important predictor of rTMS response thresholds and treatment success. Studies examining baseline depression severity as a predictor of remission demonstrate that patients with mild to moderate depression reach remission at significantly higher rates following rTMS compared to those with severe depression.[57] Among 41 patients receiving rTMS treatment, those with mild-to-moderate baseline depression achieved response in 55 percent of cases compared to 33 percent in the severe depression group, and remission rates demonstrated even more pronounced differences based on baseline severity.[57] These findings suggest that earlier intervention with rTMS for depression, before illness severity reaches extreme levels, may optimize the probability of achieving complete remission rather than mere partial response.

Age represents another important patient selection factor requiring consideration of therapeutic response thresholds and optimal treatment protocols. Early influential studies suggested that rTMS was less effective in older adults compared to younger patients, potentially leading to insurance restrictions and reduced access to this treatment modality in geriatric populations. However, contemporary large naturalistic cohort data analyzing over 500 patients comparing older versus younger adults demonstrated that while older adults achieved similar overall response rates as younger patients, they responded more slowly, suggesting that treatment duration should be extended rather than intensified in this population.[56] These findings indicate that older adults should receive at least six weeks of rTMS treatment with adequate total pulse doses to achieve efficacy comparable to younger patients, and that policies restricting rTMS access to older adults due to presumed lower efficacy lack adequate empirical support.[56]

## Balancing Response Achievement Against Safety Constraints: The Clinical Decision Framework

The clinical determination of whether rTMS indications are met requires integration of multiple considerations including disease severity and duration, extent of prior treatment failures, individual seizure risk profile, patient preferences and circumstances, and the comparative risk-benefit analysis between rTMS and alternative treatment options. This decision-making framework necessarily balances the therapeutic response thresholds that patients hope to achieve against the safety boundaries that must be respected to maintain treatment safety.

The severity and chronicity of depression powerfully influence the risk-benefit calculus underlying the decision to initiate rTMS. Patients with mild depression who have failed only one antidepressant trial might reasonably be advised to attempt sequential trials of additional antidepressant medications before pursuing rTMS, particularly given that sequential medication trials often yield response rates of 30 to 40 percent, comparable to rTMS response rates. However, patients with moderate to severe depression who have already failed two or more antidepressant medications face substantially reduced probability of achieving remission with additional antidepressant trials; meta-analytic evidence suggests that patients failing two or more medication trials experience remission rates of less than 10 percent with subsequent antidepressant monotherapy, making rTMS a more rational next step despite the need for daily clinic visits and potential adverse effects.[2][13][37]

The duration of the current depressive episode represents an important clinical consideration influencing rTMS response probability and thus the risk-benefit analysis. Longer duration of current depressive episodes predicts poorer rTMS response, with patients experiencing depressive episodes lasting 24 months or longer showing significantly lower odds of achieving response compared to those with shorter illness duration.[60] This temporal relationship between episode duration and treatment response suggests that earlier intervention with rTMS for patients identified as treatment-resistant may optimize the probability of achieving meaningful therapeutic benefit before illness becomes entrenched and treatment resistance deepens.

Specific symptoms within the depressive syndrome demonstrate varying trajectories during rTMS treatment and may help clinicians anticipate which patients will ultimately achieve remission. Early improvements in specific depressive symptoms including initiative, emotional involvement, and overall depressive symptom burden within the first two weeks of rTMS treatment significantly predict sustained response throughout the full treatment course, allowing clinicians to identify likely responders early and potential non-responders for whom alternative strategies might be considered.[60] This early prediction capability represents an important clinical advantage, allowing clinicians to adjust treatment strategies during the course of therapy rather than committing patients to the full standard six-week treatment protocol before assessing likelihood of benefit.

The risk of treatment-emergent seizures must be weighed against the substantial morbidity and mortality associated with untreated or inadequately treated major depression. Depression represents the leading cause of disability and a major contributor to suicide, particularly in individuals aged fifteen to forty-four years.[4] The suicide risk associated with severe untreated or inadequately treated depression substantially outweighs the minimal seizure risk associated with appropriately monitored rTMS treatment. Comparative analysis of seizure rates between rTMS and psychotropic medications demonstrates that rTMS seizure rates of less than one percent are comparable to or lower than seizure rates associated with many medications routinely prescribed for psychiatric conditions, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, and mood stabilizers.[28] This comparison provides important perspective that the seizure risk associated with rTMS should be understood within the context of comparative safety of alternative psychiatric treatments rather than as an absolute unacceptable risk.

## Special Populations and Modified Treatment Approaches

Certain patient populations warrant modified approaches to rTMS treatment balancing therapeutic goals against specific safety considerations. Adolescents and young adults with depression represent an important group increasingly receiving rTMS treatment, with meta-analytic evidence of 16 studies including 1,295 patients aged ten to twenty-five years demonstrating that active rTMS significantly reduced depression scale scores with effect sizes and response rates comparable to adult populations.[6] However, adolescents warrant particular safety monitoring given evidence that this age group may experience somewhat higher rates of suicidal ideation as a treatment-emergent adverse effect in some studies, though meta-analytic evidence suggests efficacy for improving suicidal ideation is inconclusive.[6] The seizure risk in youth remains low at approximately 0.62 percent, comparable to adult populations, though careful individual risk assessment remains important given the developing nature of the adolescent brain and potentially different seizure thresholds in this population.[6]

Patients with bipolar depression represent another special population requiring modified clinical approaches to balancing therapeutic response thresholds against safety boundaries. Depression is the most common mood state in bipolar disorder, occurring more frequently than mania or mixed states, yet rTMS indications for bipolar depression have historically been more limited than for unipolar depression due to concerns about potential mood destabilization or affective switching during treatment. Recent systematic review evidence examining 34 studies with 611 bipolar patients demonstrates that rTMS produces clinically meaningful reductions in depressive symptoms across different mood states, with response and remission rates of approximately 46.81 and 28.25 percent respectively, comparable to rTMS outcomes in unipolar depression.[55] Importantly, affective switching events were uncommon and generally mild in character, suggesting that appropriately supervised rTMS can safely treat bipolar depression when patients receive concurrent mood stabilizing medications. The major advantage of rTMS in bipolar depression relates to its favorable side effect profile compared to antidepressant monotherapy, which carries greater risk of treatment-emergent mania or mood destabilization if not used in conjunction with adequate mood stabilization.[55]

Patients with comorbid anxiety disorders represent an increasingly recognized special population for whom rTMS indications have been formally expanded by the FDA through approval of specific deep TMS protocols for treating depression with comorbid anxiety. Approximately 50 percent of patients with major depression also meet criteria for at least one anxiety disorder, and this comorbidity has historically been associated with somewhat poorer treatment response to antidepressant medications and psychotherapy.[44][47] Recent clinical experience with both traditional and deep TMS protocols demonstrates that rTMS is equally effective for treating depressive symptoms in patients with or without comorbid anxiety, with response rates of approximately 45 percent in the comorbid anxiety group compared to similar rates in depression without comorbidity.[44][47] Notably, anxiety symptoms improve in parallel with depressive symptoms during rTMS treatment, suggesting that the shared neurobiological underpinnings of these conditions allow single treatment modality to address both aspects of the clinical presentation.[44][47]

## Maintenance Therapy and Long-Term Management: Extending the Risk-Benefit Analysis Beyond Acute Treatment

The clinical decision regarding rTMS indications necessarily extends beyond the acute treatment phase to consideration of long-term maintenance strategies for preventing relapse and maintaining achieved symptomatic improvement. Major depressive disorder is fundamentally a chronic and relapsing condition, with approximately 80 percent of patients who respond to acute rTMS treatment experiencing relapse within weeks or months following discontinuation of treatment if no ongoing treatment is continued.[20] This high relapse rate necessitates consideration of maintenance strategies extending beyond the acute six-week treatment course.

Maintenance rTMS protocols reduce relapse risk substantially compared to no ongoing treatment. A randomized controlled trial comparing maintenance rTMS to lithium pharmacotherapy found that approximately 85 percent of patients maintained in remission at 24 weeks with weekly or biweekly rTMS, compared to approximately 65 percent with lithium maintenance treatment.[33] Notably, rTMS maintenance treatment was considerably better tolerated than lithium, with only 3 adverse events in the rTMS group compared to 16 adverse events in the lithium group, suggesting that maintenance rTMS offers superior safety profile compared to long-term medication maintenance in patients who have responded to acute rTMS treatment.[33] These findings indicate that maintenance rTMS represents a reasonable and potentially superior alternative to continuation antidepressant medication for patients who have achieved response with acute rTMS and wish to prevent relapse.

The development of accelerated rTMS protocols delivering higher pulse doses over shorter timeframes has expanded rTMS indications to include patients with urgent treatment needs who cannot commit to traditional six-week treatment courses. Accelerated rTMS protocols delivering 10,500 pulses daily for six days over three weeks achieve similar total pulse counts to standard protocols completed over six weeks, allowing achievement of therapeutic benefits in one-third the timeframe.[31] Clinical research on accelerated protocols demonstrates response rates of approximately 42 to 43 percent with remission rates of approximately 28 percent, comparable to standard protocols, while maintaining similar safety profiles with side effect rates comparable to standard rTMS administration.[31] These accelerated approaches expand rTMS indications to include patients with severe acute suicidality requiring rapid intervention, potentially reducing suicide risk within one to two weeks compared to the three-week minimum for standard protocols.

## Neurobiological Mechanisms Underlying rTMS Efficacy and Their Relationship to Safety Thresholds

Understanding the neurobiological mechanisms by which rTMS produces antidepressant effects provides important context for comprehending how therapeutic response thresholds relate to safety boundaries. Rather than producing temporary symptomatic suppression, rTMS induces lasting neuroplastic changes in brain structure and function that generate sustained mood improvement persisting beyond the treatment period. Repetitive TMS stimulation activates the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling cascade, which promotes neuronal growth, differentiation, and survival through activation of the tyrosine receptor kinase B (TrkB) pathway and downstream signaling molecules including extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI-3K).[21][24] These molecular cascades facilitate long-term potentiation and depression, the fundamental mechanisms underlying learning and memory, and promote hippocampal neurogenesis and prefrontal cortical remodeling in brain regions compromised by depression.[21][24]

The structural brain changes produced by rTMS treatment directly correlate with clinical antidepressant effects. Neuroimaging studies demonstrate that rTMS increases gray matter volume in the anterior cingulate cortex, regions involved in cognitive regulation and appraisal of emotion, with the magnitude of anterior cingulate volume increase correlating significantly with improvement in depression severity.[22] Additional gray matter volume increases occur in the anterior insula, involved in conscious emotional experience, superior temporal gyrus, and angular gyrus, with these widespread cortical changes reflecting rTMS-induced modulation of the default mode network and restoration of normal functional connectivity patterns disrupted in depression.[22][24]

These neuroplastic mechanisms provide important context for understanding why seizure risk increases when stimulation parameters exceed safety thresholds. The same excitatory mechanisms that promote beneficial neuroplasticity can, when pushed beyond physiological limits through excessive stimulus intensity, frequency, or train duration without adequate inter-train intervals, exceed the brain's capacity to contain neuronal activity within the target region and instead trigger widespread synchronous discharge characteristic of seizures. The safety guidelines specifying maximum stimulus intensities, frequencies, and inter-train intervals represent empirically determined boundaries ensuring that stimulation induces beneficial neuroplastic change while remaining below thresholds at which pathological seizure activity becomes probable. Adherence to these safety boundaries thus represents not merely risk mitigation but fundamental optimization of the therapeutic mechanism itself, ensuring that stimulation produces sustained antidepressant effects rather than acute adverse events.

## Conclusion: Integrating Therapeutic Goals with Safety Imperatives in rTMS Practice

The appropriate indications for rTMS treatment in depression represent a carefully balanced synthesis of therapeutic response thresholds and safety boundaries, reflecting both the substantial evidence demonstrating rTMS efficacy in treatment-resistant depression and the minimal but real seizure risks that must be continuously monitored and managed. Patients with major depressive disorder who have failed adequate trials of two or more antidepressant medications from different pharmacological classes, or who cannot tolerate antidepressant medications due to intolerable side effects, represent appropriate candidates for rTMS treatment, particularly when baseline depression severity ranges from moderate to severe and when early clinical response trajectories predict eventual achievement of meaningful therapeutic improvement.

The therapeutic response thresholds of 50 percent reduction in depression symptom severity for response and achievement of minimal symptom severity for remission reflect realistic and clinically meaningful improvement targets achieved by rTMS in approximately 40 to 55 percent and 16 to 36 percent of treatment-resistant depression patients respectively. These response rates compare favorably with alternative pharmacological and neuromodulatory treatments available for treatment-resistant depression, justifying rTMS as a first-line next-step intervention for patients failing initial antidepressant trials.

The seizure risk safety boundaries established by international expert consensus provide clear parameters for administering rTMS in fashion minimizing seizure risk while optimizing therapeutic efficacy. Seizure risk of less than one percent remains extraordinarily low when stimulation parameters remain within established safety guidelines, substantially lower than seizure risks associated with many psychiatric medications routinely prescribed as alternatives to rTMS. Careful pre-treatment screening identifying absolute contraindications and relative risk factors, heightened safety monitoring during initial treatment sessions when seizure risk is greatest, and rigorous adherence to established safety parameters regarding stimulus intensity, frequency, and inter-train intervals together ensure that rTMS can be administered safely across diverse patient populations.

The evidence increasingly suggests that rTMS indications should be expanded beyond patients meeting strict treatment-resistant depression definitions to include patients with moderate to severe depression willing to forgo additional medication trials, older adults and geriatric depression patients previously presumed to benefit less from rTMS, adolescents with depression, patients with bipolar depression requiring treatment without mood destabilization risk, patients with comorbid anxiety disorders, and patients requiring rapid treatment response due to acute suicidality. The development of accelerated protocols, maintenance rTMS strategies, and deep TMS technologies with expanded brain region coverage further expands the population for whom rTMS represents an evidence-based indicated treatment. Future research should continue refining patient selection strategies through identification of objective biomarkers and early response predictors allowing clinicians to identify individuals most likely to benefit from rTMS, thereby optimizing allocation of this resource-intensive but highly effective treatment to patients with greatest probability of meaningful therapeutic benefit.
