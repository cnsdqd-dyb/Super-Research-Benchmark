# The Impact of Markov Chain Properties on Activity Sequence Prediction Reliability Under Changing Socioeconomic Attributes

The application of Markov chains to model activity sequences represents a powerful analytical approach for understanding and predicting human behavioral patterns, yet this methodological framework faces significant challenges when socioeconomic attributes shift dynamically across populations and time periods. Research examining this question reveals that the core assumptions underpinning Markov chain models—particularly the time-homogeneity assumption and the memoryless property—become increasingly problematic in the presence of changing socioeconomic status (SES), leading to systematically degraded prediction reliability. The Markov assumption that future states depend only on the current state becomes violated when unobserved socioeconomic transitions drive correlated changes in activity patterns, and the time-invariant transition probabilities that anchor discrete-time Markov models fail to capture the dynamic reallocation of time and resources that accompanies economic and social mobility. This comprehensive analysis synthesizes theoretical foundations, empirical evidence, and methodological considerations to demonstrate how socioeconomic heterogeneity and temporal changes in SES status fundamentally alter the predictive accuracy and operational validity of Markov chain models for activity sequence forecasting.

## Theoretical Foundations of Markov Chains in Activity Sequence Modeling

The application of Markov chain theory to human activity sequences originates from the core assumption that individuals transition between discrete behavioral states according to probabilistic rules that depend only on the current state[1][32]. In activity modeling, states typically represent distinct activities or combinations of activities and locations, such as working, traveling, being at home, or engaging in leisure activities. The probability structure of a Markov chain is characterized by a transition matrix, denoted as **P**, where each element represents the probability of transitioning from one state to another[1][4]. For a time-homogeneous Markov chain—the most commonly assumed form—these transition probabilities remain constant across time, allowing researchers to compute long-term behavioral patterns through matrix multiplication and eigenvalue analysis[1][6]. This framework has proven remarkably useful for diverse applications spanning from text generation algorithms to reliability engineering to behavioral prediction[1][3]. The elegance of the Markov approach lies in its computational tractability and its ability to generate synthetic activity sequences that preserve observed behavioral patterns, enabling researchers to construct stochastic models of how different demographic groups distribute their time across activities and locations[25][28].

However, the mathematical convenience of assuming time-homogeneous Markov processes comes with substantial assumptions about population stability. The central assumption of the Markov property—that future states depend only on the current state and not on the historical path through which the current state was reached—implies that the population itself must be reasonably homogeneous with respect to factors that drive state transitions[1][19]. When researchers apply Markov chains to real activity data, particularly data drawn from large, diverse populations recorded over extended periods, this assumption becomes increasingly strained. Time-homogeneous Markov chains implicitly assume that the composition of the population and the behavioral propensities of individuals remain stable across the observation period[1][17][22]. Yet research on activity patterns consistently demonstrates that human behavior is not statistically independent of socioeconomic characteristics including income, employment status, education level, marital status, and neighborhood context[7][8][10]. These socioeconomic variables themselves are neither fixed nor uniformly distributed across populations; rather, they shift both at the individual level as people transition between employment states, income quintiles, and life stages, and at the population level through macroeconomic changes, policy interventions, and broader social transformation[18][57].

The theoretical problem emerges when socioeconomic attributes change, either for individuals or populations. If an individual transitions from unemployment to employment, or if a neighborhood undergoes economic development, the underlying state space and transition probabilities for activity sequences effectively change. The person who becomes employed faces different temporal constraints, different income constraints, and different social expectations about how to allocate their time. Yet a standard time-homogeneous Markov model trained on historical data—which may reflect earlier socioeconomic circumstances—would continue to apply the same transition probabilities regardless of these changes. This represents a form of non-stationarity that violates the homogeneity assumptions upon which the model's predictive validity rests[14][17][22][35].

## Heterogeneity in Activity Patterns and Socioeconomic Stratification

Empirical research examining human activity patterns across diverse populations reveals that socioeconomic attributes are among the strongest predictors of how individuals allocate their time across different activities and locations. A global study analyzing 202,898 participants from 22 countries found that physical activity levels—a key component of daily activity patterns—varied substantially by demographics including age, gender, marital status, employment, education, religious service attendance, and immigration status[8]. The same study documented that individuals aged 60-69 demonstrated higher activity levels than both younger and older groups, men reported higher activity levels than women, those in domestic partnerships reported more activity than single individuals, and self-employed individuals were more active than employees[8]. Most significantly for understanding SES effects, individuals with higher educational attainment and those born in their current country of residence (compared to immigrants) demonstrated substantially higher activity levels[8]. These patterns suggest that socioeconomic position fundamentally shapes the activity opportunity set available to individuals and how they navigate temporal constraints.

The relationship between socioeconomic status and specific activity patterns extends beyond simple overall activity levels to encompass the composition and sequencing of activities throughout the day. Research comparing work and leisure time physical behaviors among low socioeconomic status (SES) adults found that their leisure time was predominantly sedentary while their work time was predominantly standing, and critically, work physical behaviors differently influenced day-to-day leisure time behaviors across different days of the week[10]. This finding illustrates an important principle: the marginal effect of current state on future state transitions is itself dependent on socioeconomic circumstances. For a low-SES adult whose job requires extensive standing, a day spent standing at work creates different incentive structures and physiological constraints affecting evening leisure activity choices than it would for a high-SES office worker. In other words, the transition probability from work state to leisure activity state is not invariant across socioeconomic groups—it is conditioned on these broader contextual factors[10]. Such conditional dependencies violate the Markov assumption that transition probabilities depend only on the current state, not on underlying attributes that produce systematic variation in how individuals respond to being in a particular state.

Multiple studies have documented substantial socioeconomic stratification in the use of time across different locations and activities. Longitudinal analysis of time-use patterns in three age groups found that day-type and season influenced time spent in many location and activity categories, but also that longitudinal changes occurred—young children slept less over time, transit time increased for some groups, and time spent on working and shopping decreased during the study period[41]. The research team attributed these changes to "human physiological changes with age and changes in macro-economic factors such as gas prices and the economic recession," highlighting how broad economic shifts alter activity patterns[41]. Another study examined whether children from low-income backgrounds who attended high-quality early intervention programs experienced different developmental trajectories, finding that extended program participation was associated with higher school achievement, lower grade retention, and better educational outcomes[29]. This research suggests that interventions targeting socioeconomic circumstances create systematic shifts in activity patterns and life trajectories, demonstrating that the relationship between SES and activities is causal and dynamic rather than merely correlational[29].

## The Time-Homogeneity Problem in Non-Stationary Populations

The assumption of time-homogeneity in Markov chain models requires that transition probabilities remain constant across the observation period[1][32]. This assumption is mathematically elegant and computationally essential for many common applications, including the computation of long-run steady states through eigenvalue analysis and the simplified calculation of multi-step transition probabilities through matrix exponentiation[1][6]. However, populations are rarely truly stationary with respect to the socioeconomic factors that drive activity patterns. At the individual level, people experience employment transitions, income shocks, household composition changes, and geographic mobility—all factors that reshape the activity opportunity set and behavioral incentives[26][41][45]. At the aggregate population level, economic booms and recessions, policy changes, technological innovations, and demographic shifts collectively alter how populations spend their time[8][41][46].

Research on non-stationary Markov processes has developed theoretical frameworks for understanding how prediction reliability degrades when transition probabilities themselves change over time[14][17][35]. Non-stationary Markov processes—where transition probabilities are time-dependent or depend on external variables—introduce substantially greater model complexity but also provide more realistic representations of systems where underlying drivers change[17][35]. A study of non-stationary Markov decision processes in stochastic environments found that when environmental parameters evolve over time, the optimal policy is non-stationary in the most general case, meaning that strategies that were optimal at one time point may become suboptimal as the environment changes[14]. This principle directly applies to activity sequence prediction: strategies for allocating time and the probability structure of activity transitions that were accurate during one economic period or for one socioeconomic stratum may not predict activity patterns in subsequent periods or for different social groups[14].

The hidden Markov model framework provides one approach to addressing non-stationarity by introducing latent states that capture unobserved heterogeneity and time-varying conditions[16][34][51]. Heterogeneous hidden Markov models explicitly acknowledge that different subgroups within a population may follow distinct transition processes, with the mixture component capturing unobserved heterogeneity between clusters[34]. When applied to financial time series, these models successfully identified distinct regime-switching behaviors across market subgroups that would have been obscured by assuming a single stationary Markov process[34]. Applied to activity sequences and socioeconomic transitions, similar latent heterogeneity could theoretically capture the distinct behavioral regimes associated with different SES positions. However, this solution requires researchers to specify or learn the number and characteristics of latent classes in advance, and it does not solve the problem that an individual's latent class assignment may itself change as their socioeconomic circumstances shift[34][51].

Research on continuous-time Markov chains with time-dependent transition rates offers another theoretical avenue for addressing non-stationarity[44][50][54]. These models specify transition rate functions that vary as a function of time and covariates, allowing the intensity of transitions between states to change as external conditions evolve[44][50][54]. A study applying continuous-time Markov chains to analyze health behavioral changes through a transtheoretical model found that different intervention and control groups exhibited systematically different transition rates between stages of change, suggesting that external factors (the intervention) created non-homogeneous transitions[44]. When applied to activity sequences where socioeconomic transitions create systematic changes in behavioral propensities, time-dependent transition rate functions could in principle capture how activity pattern stability changes as people move between socioeconomic states[44][54]. However, such models require explicit parametric specification of how covariates affect transition rates, imposing assumptions about functional form that may not be empirically justified[44][54].

## Empirical Evidence on Socioeconomic Effects and Prediction Accuracy

Empirical research examining prediction accuracy for human behavioral outcomes reveals consistent evidence that population heterogeneity—particularly socioeconomic heterogeneity—substantially reduces both cross-validated prediction accuracy and the stability of learned behavioral patterns. A study investigating how population heterogeneity affects predictive accuracy examined whether models trained on diverse populations performed adequately when applied to equally diverse test populations[55]. The researchers found that diversity exerted substantial impact on the generalization accuracy of predictive models and on the pattern stability of extracted behavioral patterns, even after rigorous matching and deconfounding procedures[55]. Specifically, they demonstrated that participants heterogeneous in their demographic and socioeconomic characteristics could not be reliably classified using models trained on less diverse populations[55]. This finding has direct implications for activity sequence prediction: models learned from heterogeneous populations may fail to generalize when applying them to forecast individual activity patterns, particularly when individuals move between socioeconomic strata during the prediction period.

The American Time Use Survey, the primary source of national data on how Americans spend their time across activities and locations, has been extensively analyzed to understand how activity patterns vary across demographic and socioeconomic groups[23][28][56]. A comprehensive analysis using machine learning to classify activity sequences in the American Time Use Survey found that demographic variables including age, gender, work status, marital status, and other socioeconomic characteristics were highly predictive of activity patterns[28][56]. The researchers developed unsupervised classification methods that identified 76 distinct activity classes with demographic composition ranging from a minimum of 25 individuals to a maximum of 1,237 individuals per class[28]. The substantial variation in class sizes and the necessity for machine learning to discover multiple distinct activity classes suggests that treating the population as a single homogeneous Markov process would fail to capture meaningful behavioral diversity[28][56]. More importantly, the temporal structure of activities within each demographic-activity class differed from other classes, indicating that the transition probabilities between activity states are not invariant across socioeconomic groups[28][56].

A longitudinal analysis of physical activity patterns using latent transition analysis—a methodological approach closely related to Markov chain analysis—found that among 2,023 older adults in New York City, seven distinct activity pattern classes existed, with the majority of subjects (54-66%) retaining the same activity patterns between observation waves[38]. However, the researchers found substantial heterogeneity in the determinants of activity pattern transitions: neighborhood unemployment was the only variable consistently associated with changing between activity latent classes across different model specifications[38]. This finding suggests that macroeconomic conditions and neighborhood-level socioeconomic characteristics fundamentally shape the stability of activity patterns. During economic downturns (periods of high neighborhood unemployment), individuals are more likely to shift activity patterns, presumably due to employment disruptions and income changes. This represents precisely the type of socioeconomically-driven non-stationarity that violates time-homogeneous Markov assumptions: the transition probabilities between activity pattern states themselves depend on macroeconomic and neighborhood conditions that are exogenous to the individual's current activity state[38].

Research on employment prediction using machine learning found that demographic and socioeconomic variables were highly predictive of employment status and day program participation for individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities[43]. The most accurate models achieved 89 percent accuracy on test data, but notably, the single most important variable for prediction was whether community employment was listed as a goal in the person's service plan[43]. This finding illustrates how institutional and social factors—factors correlated with but not identical to the individual's current activity state—substantially shape behavioral trajectories. A standard Markov model conditioning only on current activity state would miss this institutional context that determines transition probabilities to future employment-related activities[43].

## Non-Stationary Population Dynamics and Composition Bias

Beyond individual-level changes in socioeconomic status, population-level shifts in SES composition create systematic bias in aggregate-level predictions derived from Markov chain models. Research on how population heterogeneity and compositional change affect causal inference demonstrates that average treatment effects estimated at one point in time may differ substantially from treatment effects estimated at later time points, even when the true causal effects at the individual level remain constant[53]. This "composition bias" arises when treatment propensity is systematically associated with heterogeneous treatment effects and when the composition of treated and untreated populations changes over time[53].
