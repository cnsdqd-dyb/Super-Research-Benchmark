# Biogeochemical Processes in Water Quality Models and Their Effects on Treatment Thresholds Given Baseflow Recharge

This comprehensive report examines how biogeochemical processes embedded within water quality models fundamentally shape treatment thresholds and regulatory standards, particularly under baseflow conditions when groundwater discharge dominates streamflow. The interaction between biogeochemical transformations—including nitrification, denitrification, redox-sensitive processes, and nutrient cycling—and baseflow recharge creates complex scenarios where contaminant concentrations may either increase or decrease depending on hydrological and geochemical conditions within the subsurface. Advanced multiscale modeling approaches now incorporate biogeochemical reactions occurring in hyporheic zones and riparian aquifers to better predict water quality at points of compliance, which directly influences the feasibility of meeting maximum contaminant level (MCL) standards and the design of treatment systems. The spatial heterogeneity of redox conditions, the legacy effects of historical contamination transported through groundwater, and the temporal variability in baseflow chemistry all interact to create nonlinear responses that traditional static treatment thresholds may not adequately address.

## Fundamentals of Biogeochemical Processes in Aquatic Systems

Biogeochemical processes represent the fundamental mechanisms by which organisms and inorganic chemical reactions transform the chemical composition of water as it moves through watersheds, aquifers, and stream networks. These processes encompass a broad spectrum of transformations driven by microbial metabolism, chemical reactions, and physical-chemical interactions with aquifer materials and channel sediments. Understanding these processes is essential because they directly determine the concentration of contaminants and nutrients that water systems must treat before water reaches consumers or before discharge standards can be met.

The **nitrogen cycle** serves as one of the most consequential biogeochemical processes affecting water quality. Nitrogen exists in multiple chemical forms including organic nitrogen, ammonia (NH₄⁺), nitrite (NO₂⁻), and nitrate (NO₃⁻), with transformations between these forms occurring through both biological and chemical pathways[8][11]. Nitrification, the oxidation of ammonia to nitrate, represents a critical process occurring primarily under aerobic conditions and is catalyzed by specialized autotrophic bacteria such as Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter[11]. This process consumes approximately 7.1 milligrams per liter of alkalinity as calcium carbonate for each milligram per liter of ammonia nitrogen oxidized, which has important implications for pH buffering and treatment system chemistry[11]. Conversely, denitrification converts nitrate into nitrogen gas under anoxic conditions, effectively removing bioavailable nitrogen from aquatic systems[8]. The efficiency of denitrification depends critically on the availability of organic carbon as an electron donor and the maintenance of anoxic conditions, both of which vary substantially between summer and winter periods and between surface water and hyporheic zone environments.

**Phosphorus cycling** interacts complexly with iron and other redox-sensitive elements to influence overall water quality. Iron-bound phosphorus and aluminum-bound phosphorus represent important bioavailable forms that respond to changes in redox conditions and pH[50]. When oxygen concentrations decline in sediments, iron(III) oxides and hydroxides dissolve, releasing associated phosphorus into pore water, which can then diffuse into overlying surface water and fuel eutrophication[50]. Conversely, in oxidizing environments such as flowing river systems, dissolved and particulate iron typically exists in the ferric form and can sorb phosphorus, potentially removing it from the bioavailable pool[50]. This dynamic interaction between iron redox state and phosphorus availability means that the same management action—such as reducing oxygen demand from wastewater inputs—may simultaneously affect both iron and phosphorus transport through mechanisms that are not immediately apparent from simple mass balance considerations.

**Redox processes** fundamentally control the mobility and toxicity of numerous contaminants including arsenic, chromium, uranium, and various organic compounds[14][17]. Redox conditions in groundwater are established by the balance between oxygen supply from recharging water and the reactivity and abundance of electron donors such as organic matter, pyrite, and other reduced minerals[14]. The redox classification scheme identifies distinct zones including oxic, suboxic, anoxic with nitrate reduction, iron reduction, sulfate reduction, and methanogenic zones[14]. The likelihood of oxic conditions varies with depth below the water table, increasing in unconsolidated sand and gravel aquifers and decreasing in sedimentary aquifers, which typically have greater electron donor availability[14]. Predicting redox conditions at the national scale using machine learning approaches has revealed that depth below water table, percentage of fine-grained sediments, and poorly drained soils represent the most important predictive variables[14]. These findings underscore that simple treatment approaches based on surface water quality alone may miss important subsurface transformations occurring during groundwater residence time and discharge.

## Baseflow Recharge Dynamics and Water Quality Composition

Baseflow represents the portion of streamflow sustained by groundwater discharge during periods without precipitation, serving as the primary water source during dry seasons and droughts when human demand peaks. The baseflow index, defined as the ratio of baseflow to total flow expressed as a percentage, varies geographically from 0.45 to 0.85 depending on aquifer properties, with higher indices in areas dominated by stratified-drift deposits that facilitate groundwater storage and slow recharge[5]. Understanding baseflow is essential for water quality management because groundwater-derived water often carries different contaminant and nutrient signatures than surface runoff-dominated flow, and these differences persist over extended periods when surface water contributions become negligible.

The **chemical composition of baseflow** differs fundamentally from stormflow because groundwater has been filtered through sediments and spent extended time in aquifers where biogeochemical processes have already transformed initial recharge water composition. Studies of the Long Island Sound watershed found that nitrate plus nitrite concentrations showed little seasonal variation between growing and nongrowing seasons during baseflow conditions[20]. This constancy contrasts sharply with ammonia plus organic nitrogen, which was generally lower during the nongrowing season (less than 0.07 to 0.83 milligrams per liter) compared to the growing season (0.11 to 0.98 milligrams per liter), suggesting that organic nitrogen derives largely from soils and vegetation while inorganic nitrogen is more conserved during groundwater transport[20]. Mean annual groundwater-recharge rates ranged from 17.5 to 22.4 inches per year at study locations, with mean annual rates ranging from 2 to 7 inches per year higher than baseflow itself, indicating that significant water infiltrates during recharge but does not immediately discharge to streams[5].

The **temporal persistence of baseflow chemistry** has profound implications for treatment thresholds. Baseflow nitrate yields have been estimated through statistical relationships with land cover and underlying geology across watersheds, with agricultural and developed land covers contributing the majority of nitrate to groundwater baseflow[51]. These loadings represent largely persistent inputs rather than episodic peaks, meaning treatment systems must be designed to handle sustained, steady concentrations rather than being sized for maximum daily loads. In the Chesapeake Bay watershed, modeled baseflow nitrate concentrations varied by geologic unit, with lower concentrations in Delmarva, igneous and metamorphic, and sandstone and carbonate formations compared to pure carbonate units for similar land uses[51]. This geographic variation reflects different soil development, weathering rates, and organic matter availability that affect nitrogen transformation and sorption processes across the region.

The **relationship between recharge timing and baseflow quality** reveals that seasonal shifts in recharge patterns have differential effects on groundwater and baseflow drought sensitivity[2]. Across Germany, groundwater heads in northern regions showed greater sensitivity to long-term recharge while responses in the Central German Uplands showed greater sensitivity to short-term recharge variations[2]. Groundwater drought recovery time depends primarily on hydrogeological conditions, with slow recovery in porous aquifers and faster recovery in fractured rock aquifers[2]. These findings indicate that a seasonal shift of recharge toward more winter recharge and less summer recharge generally produces lesser effects on groundwater and baseflow drought severity[2], suggesting that climate change impacts on recharge seasonality may have manageable consequences for baseflow chemistry compared to changes in total recharge volume.

## Integration of Biogeochemical Processes into Water Quality Modeling Frameworks

Advanced water quality models now incorporate detailed biogeochemical process representations that extend from local-scale hyporheic zone chemistry to regional watershed-scale nutrient loading predictions. The **multiscale modeling approach** developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory incorporates important biogeochemical processes happening in river corridors through the award-winning Amanzi-ATS software and high-performance computing[1]. This framework recognizes that reactions occurring in microbially active zones near streams have major influences on the cycling of carbon, nutrients, and contaminants at basin scales, and these fine-scale processes manifest at much larger scales through emergent properties that traditional reach-averaged models may miss[1].

The **SWAT (Soil and Water Assessment Tool) model** represents one of the most widely applied watershed modeling frameworks for water quality assessment and has been extensively validated across diverse hydrological and climatic settings[43][46]. SWAT divides watersheds into subbasins and hydrological response units to represent spatial variability in land use, soil properties, and management practices[46]. The model simulates separate nitrogen cycle processes including mineralization, nitrification, denitrification, and plant uptake across different zones within each HRU[46]. When applied to irrigated agricultural watersheds in Mediterranean climates, SWAT revealed that nearly 40 percent of diverted irrigation water was directly draining into the drainage system as bypass flow, and considerable amounts of nitrate (40 to 60 kilograms per hectare) were unaccounted for in budget calculations[43]. This finding suggests that traditional hydrologic calibration may obscure important nitrogen transformation pathways, and more detailed biogeochemical modeling may be necessary to identify where and how nitrogen is being removed or sequestered during transport through the landscape.

The **QUAL2Kw water quality model** simulates reach-scale processes including temperature, algae dynamics, nutrients, and dissolved oxygen using a simplified kinetic approach suitable for preliminary TMDL (Total Maximum Daily Load) analyses[48]. When applied to Clarks Creek in Washington State, QUAL2Kw predicted that reducing elodea (aquatic macrophytes) and polluted stormwater runoff would improve dissolved oxygen concentrations in the impaired reaches[48]. However, the model's performance depends critically on accurate specification of kinetic parameters including algal growth rates, respiration rates, and nutrient uptake coefficients, which often exhibit spatial and temporal variability that single-value parameterization cannot capture.

The **WASP (Water Quality Analysis Simulation Program) model** provides a dynamic compartmental framework for simulating conventional pollutants including nitrogen, phosphorus, dissolved oxygen, and biochemical oxygen demand, as well as organic chemicals, metals, mercury, and pathogens in aquatic systems[39][42]. WASP can be linked with hydrodynamic and sediment transport models that provide flows, depths, velocities, temperature, salinity, and sediment fluxes as forcing conditions[39]. The model includes optional sediment diagenesis modules that predict sediment oxygen demand and nutrient fluxes from underlying sediments, recognizing that benthic processes constitute a major source of nutrients during baseflow periods when surface water contributions are minimal[39]. Despite these capabilities, WASP has documented limitations including low accuracy in simulating variations in suspended solids loading and inflows of fine silt and clay sediments from tributaries[42], and it does not incorporate processes such as non-aqueous phase liquids or metals speciation that may be important for certain contaminant classes.

## Hyporheic Zone and Riparian Aquifer Biogeochemistry

The **hyporheic zone**, defined as the region of surface water and groundwater mixing that extends laterally into riparian aquifers and vertically into subsurface sediments beneath the channel, functions as a critical biogeochemical reactor that modulates contaminant and nutrient export[13][16]. Surface water quality along river corridors can be substantially modulated by hyporheic zones that are ubiquitous and biogeochemically active, yet watershed management practices often overlook their potentially important role[13]. The SWAT-MRMT-R model integrates the SWAT watershed model with multirate mass transfer reactions to represent hyporheic exchange and reactive transport, revealing that hyporheic zones can attenuate peak nitrate concentrations in streams with mass transfer and biogeochemical reactions producing an average concentration reduction of 11.6 percent compared to simulations without hyporheic processing[13].

The **residence time distribution within hyporheic zones** critically controls the extent of biogeochemical transformation. Simulations applied to the Hanford Reach of the Columbia River revealed that given a residence time distribution, the way exchange fluxes to hyporheic zones are approximated using multirate mass transfer models can significantly change the amount of nitrate consumed through denitrification[13]. High-concentration pulses of nitrate from irrigation return flows may remain in the stream for extended periods in reaches with longer residence times, suggesting that high-concentration point sources of contamination entering reaches with biogeochemically active hyporheic zones may not achieve complete removal even with favorable conditions[13]. This finding indicates that treatment threshold decisions must account for the spatial distribution of hyporheic processing capacity, not just the total mass loading of contaminants.

**Denitrification efficiency in riparian zones** depends on the extent of river water infiltration and the availability of bioavailable organic carbon. River water infiltration into riparian aquifers enhances denitrification efficiency because infiltrated river water carries dissolved organic carbon that fuels heterotrophic microbial respiration[49]. Data from a Central German stream showed that highest rates of oxygen consumption and denitrification in the riparian aquifer occur where the fraction of infiltrated river water is high and groundwater temperature is elevated, with river discharge and depth to groundwater representing additional but less important explanatory variables[49]. The implication is that groundwater-surface water interaction zones near locations with high river water infiltration and favorable temperature conditions may remove substantially more nitrogen than zones where minimal river water infiltrates the riparian aquifer. Consequently, management actions that increase river-riparian aquifer interaction—such as floodplain restoration or installation of permeable reactive barriers—might substantially reduce nitrogen loading downstream compared to in-channel treatment alone.

**Nitrogen transformation pathways in subterranean estuaries** reveal the importance of residence time and redox zonation on nutrient cycling. A study of boreal microtidal sandy beaches found that inland groundwater supplied high concentrations of nitrate and dissolved organic nitrogen to subterranean estuaries, while inputs from seawater infiltration were very limited[26]. Non-conservative behavior occurred along the groundwater flow path, leading to low nitrate and high ammonium concentrations in the discharge zone[26]. The long transit time of groundwater within the beach (approximately 166 days), coupled with oxygen-depleted conditions and high carbon concentrations, created a favorable environment for nitrogen transformations including heterotrophic and autotrophic denitrification and ammonium production[26]. Fresh inland groundwater delivered 37 moles of nitrate per year per meter of shoreline to the subterranean estuary, but near the discharge zone, the potential export of nitrogen species shifted dramatically with ammonium comprising the dominant form and nitrate becoming essentially depleted[26]. This transformation has major implications for coastal water quality management because even if agricultural and wastewater sources are controlled to reduce nitrate loading to groundwater, the combination of residence time in the subsurface and biogeochemical transformations may fundamentally alter the form of nitrogen delivered to surface waters.

## Effects on Maximum Contaminant Levels and Regulatory Standards

The **regulatory framework for drinking water quality** establishes maximum contaminant level goals (MCLGs) representing nonenforceable public health targets and legally enforceable maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) set as close as possible to MCLGs using the best available treatment technology and considering costs[22][40]. MCLs are typically expressed in milligrams or micrograms per liter and apply to all public water systems[22]. The EPA has issued 88 standards comprising 78 MCLs and 10 Treatment Techniques for microorganisms, chemicals, and radionuclides as of 2019[22]. For some contaminants, EPA establishes Treatment Techniques (TTs) instead of MCLs, with TTs representing enforceable procedures that drinking water systems must follow rather than numeric limits[22]. MCLs and TTs are known jointly as National Primary Drinking Water Regulations and represent legally enforceable standards that water utilities must meet.

The **relationship between biogeochemical processes and contaminant mobility** directly affects whether treatment thresholds can be achieved. Redox-sensitive contaminants such as nitrate and arsenic are among the most common constituents exceeding MCLs in public and domestic supply wells, with redox processes markedly affecting their transport[14]. Denitrification of nitrate from agricultural sources may lead to mobilization of trace metals, while irrigation-induced changes in redox conditions may mobilize arsenic[14]. Managed aquifer recharge may shift redox conditions and cause release of arsenic and other geogenic contaminants that were previously immobilized[14]. The implication is that a treatment strategy focused solely on nitrate removal without considering coupled redox-driven processes may inadvertently increase arsenic or other contaminant concentrations at the point of water supply.

The **legacy effect of historical contamination** complicates achievement of treatment thresholds under baseflow conditions. Winter low-flow conditions in streams provide an opportunity to evaluate the importance of legacy nitrate in catchments because peak flows are suppressed, revealing the baseline groundwater contribution more clearly[54]. Streams in watersheds dominated by oxic water may be more likely to have high nitrate concentrations during baseflow conditions, particularly when baseflow recharges the aquifer a decade or more prior to discharge, representing legacy contamination from historical agricultural practices[14]. The recovery time from legacy contamination depends on aquifer residence times and the extent of denitrification occurring during transport, with implications for setting realistic timelines for achieving water quality standards through source control alone.

The **effect of seasonal recharge dynamics on contaminant concentrations** in baseflow suggests that climate change impacts on precipitation patterns may require recalibration of treatment thresholds. A seasonal shift of recharge toward more winter precipitation and less summer precipitation generally has lesser effects on groundwater and baseflow drought severity compared to changes in total recharge volume[2]. However, if winter recharge increases while summer recharge decreases substantially, the net effect on annual baseflow concentrations of nutrients derived from agriculture and wastewater sources may increase due to reduced dilution during the high-recharge winter period when biogeochemical transformation is slower.

## Biogeochemical Hotspots and Spatial Heterogeneity in Water Quality

The concept of **biogeochemical hotspots** recognizes that nutrient transformation rates and efficiencies vary dramatically across space due to differences in organic matter availability, redox conditions, temperature, and hydrological residence times[10]. A novel modeling framework developed to simulate the role of biogeochemical hotspots in driving nitrogen export in watersheds improves projections of nitrogen export in watersheds where hotspots play an increasingly important role in water quality[10]. These hotspots may occur in riparian zones, hyporheic sediments, or other locations where conditions favor enhanced biogeochemical processing, yet they remain largely absent from many reach-scale models that average properties over long stream segments. Incorporation of hotspot representation in models allows more accurate prediction of how distributed management actions—such as riparian buffer installation or streambank stabilization—will affect nitrogen removal efficiency at different locations within a watershed.

## Nutrient Cycling and Estuarine Biogeochemistry

The **effect of riverine phosphorus on ocean nitrogen cycle feedbacks** reveals how biogeochemical processes operating at the land-ocean interface propagate consequences far beyond local watersheds. Modeling studies found that riverine export of bioavailable phosphorus alone or in conjunction with nitrogen affects marine biology on millennial timescales more than riverine nitrogen alone[7]. Biogeochemical feedbacks in the marine nitrogen cycle are strongly influenced by additional phosphorus, such that where bioavailable phosphorus increases with river input, nitrogen concentration generally increases as well except in regions with diminishing oxygen concentrations[7]. High phosphorus burial rates in marine sediments decrease biological production significantly, and the addition of riverine phosphorus in models leads to elevated primary production rates in coastal and open oceans[7]. These findings suggest that phosphorus control in freshwater systems may have consequences for nitrogen cycling in marine environments, complicating optimization of treatment thresholds that focus on individual nutrient species.

## Parameter Sensitivity and Model Identifiability

The **challenge of parameter selection in biogeochemical models** arises because only small subsets of the complete parameter set have unique and identifiable effects on model outputs. A sensitivity analysis of a coastal biogeochemical model used for hypoxia prediction in the Gulf of Mexico found that variation in sensitivity had direct correspondence with identifiability, such that only small subsets of parameters had sufficiently unique effects for accurate calibration[31]. Eight of 51 total parameters had sufficiently unique effects on model output for reliable parameter estimation[31]. This finding has major implications for model complexity and transferability; models with high parameter counts may achieve good fits to one data set through compensating errors in unidentifiable parameters, leading to poor predictive skill when applied to different systems or future conditions. Water quality managers must balance the desire for mechanistic biogeochemical detail against the practical ability to parameterize complex models with available data.

## Contaminant Fate and Transport Mechanisms

The **physical and chemical processes affecting contaminant migration** in groundwater and surface water include advection, dispersion, diffusion, adsorption, and biogeochemical transformation[30]. Contaminant presence in groundwater results from operational activities at contaminated sites, with subsequent migration through water flow in the subsurface[30]. Chlorinated hydrocarbons that are relatively easily leached from soil into groundwater typically undergo progressive dehalogenation under appropriate conditions, though the time required for each step varies widely and degradation may or may not occur depending on availability of nutrients, microorganisms, and appropriate redox conditions[30]. The presence of chlorinated hydrocarbons in unsaturated soils serves as a renewable source of groundwater contamination, as the water table fluctuates over time and the saturated portion repeatedly comes in contact with contaminated soil, causing recharge of contaminants into groundwater[30].

The **reactive transport of metal contaminants** in groundwater illustrates how equilibrium assumptions may fail for certain reaction types. A one-dimensional reactive-transport model developed using PHREEQC to analyze transport in Pinal Creek Basin, Arizona, found that assumptions of local geochemical equilibrium were generally valid for reactions involving gypsum and rhodochrosite, but not valid for oxidation-reduction reactions involving iron and manganese oxides[21]. Calcite dissolution in the transition zone between acidic and neutral groundwater was not sufficient to establish equilibrium, and kinetically controlled oxidation-reduction reactions continued in the acidic flow path for years following passage of the transition zone[21]. These findings underscore that biogeochemical models based on equilibrium assumptions may overpredict the extent of contaminant attenuation and thus overestimate the feasibility of meeting treatment thresholds through natural processes alone.

## Contaminant Removal in Hyporheic Zones and First-Order Reaction Kinetics

The **removal of stream-borne contaminants through hyporheic exchange** can be quantified using analytical models that incorporate first-order reaction kinetics and mass transfer between surface water and subsurface sediments[32]. A simple analytical model demonstrates that contaminant concentrations are higher in downwelling zones than upwelling zones, reflecting the strong coupling between transport and reaction in these systems[32]. When flow-averaged, the concentration difference across upwelling and downwelling zones drives a net contaminant flux into the sediment bed proportional to the average downwelling velocity[32]. The dimensionless Damkohler number, defined as the ratio of reaction rate to mass transport rate, fully determines contaminant removal by hyporheic exchange and first-order reaction[32]. When the Damkohler number is small, contaminants are transported through the hyporheic zone with minimal removal because the residence time is too short for significant reaction; conversely, when the number is large, reaction rates dominate and contaminants are nearly completely removed[32]. This framework provides a mechanistic basis for predicting how stream bed form geometry, sediment hydraulic properties, and contaminant degradation rates jointly determine the fraction of surface water contaminant load that is removed by the hyporheic zone.

## Dissolved Organic Matter Dynamics and Biogeochemical Composition

The **composition and fluxes of dissolved organic matter (DOM) from headwater streams** reveals how hydrology controls the biogeochemical signatures of water discharged from watersheds. An Alpine headwater stream study monitoring DOM over three years found that discharge is a primary driver of DOM composition in both streamwater and hyporheic zones[33]. Elevated discharge induced a terrigenous imprint on streamwater and hyporheic DOM as indicated by elevated specific UV absorbance and contributions of soil-derived fluorescent components[33]. At baseflow conditions, streamwater DOM exhibited greater contributions from autochthonous sources compared to hyporheic DOM, suggesting that in-stream biological production dominates DOM sources during low-flow periods while terrestrial DOM dominates during high-flow periods[33]. The canonical correlation analysis revealed that discharge, photosynthetically active radiation, and streamwater temperature collectively explained DOM composition variation between streamwater and hyporheic zones[33]. These findings indicate that baseflow periods not only carry legacy contaminant loads but also exhibit DOM composition signatures distinct from stormflow, with implications for how biogeochemical processes in downstream treatment systems respond to variations in source water organic matter characteristics.

## Practical Implications for Water Treatment and Water Quality Standards

The **integration of biogeochemical process knowledge into treatment plant design and operation** represents a frontline application of advanced water quality science. Industrial and domestic wastewater treatment facilities face major challenges in managing nitrogen content in the form of ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate, with inefficient nitrification and denitrification processes causing failures to meet wastewater quality standards and increased operational costs[8]. Optimization of nitrification and denitrification processes requires proper system design separating aerobic nitrification zones from anoxic denitrification zones, continuous monitoring of critical parameters such as dissolved oxygen, oxidation-reduction potential, and nitrate/nitrite concentrations, management of microbial communities including supplementation through bioaugmentation when necessary, and addition of external carbon sources when carbon limitation reduces denitrification efficiency[8]. Extended aeration systems demonstrate superior nitrification performance due to long aeration and mean cell residence times, allowing development of slow-growing nitrifying populations that would be washed out of shorter-residence-time systems[11].

The **Total Maximum Daily Load (TMDL) process** represents the regulatory framework for addressing water quality impairments and establishes the relationship between pollutant loading and water quality standards[44]. TMDLs are developed using techniques ranging from simple mass balance calculations to complex water quality modeling approaches, with the degree of analysis varying based on waterbody type, complexity of flow conditions, and the specific pollutant causing impairment[44]. The TMDL equation partitions the allowable loading among point sources through wasteload allocations and nonpoint sources through load allocations, with a margin of safety added to account for uncertainty[44]. For dissolved oxygen impairments in Clarks Creek, Washington, the TMDL study used landscape-scale hydrological modeling and reach-scale water quality modeling to determine that reducing sediment loading, reducing dissolved oxygen deficit, controlling elodea density, and increasing riparian shade would collectively restore water quality by 2034[48]. This approach explicitly recognizes that biogeochemical processes operating at multiple scales must be considered together to achieve water quality standards.

## Drinking Water Treatment Standards and Biogeochemical Processes

The **application of drinking water quality standards** assumes that treatment systems can achieve required removal efficiencies regardless of source water biogeochemical composition, yet in reality, the form and bioavailability of contaminants in source water significantly affects treatment feasibility and costs. The National Primary Drinking Water Regulations establish maximum contaminant level goals and maximum contaminant levels for microorganisms, disinfectants, disinfection byproducts, inorganic chemicals, organic chemicals, and radionuclides[3][19][37]. For microorganisms, surface water treatment rules require systems using surface water or groundwater under the direct influence of surface water to both disinfect and filter water, or meet specific criteria for avoiding filtration[15]. For Giardia lamblia and Cryptosporidium, disinfection must achieve at least 99.9 percent inactivation[15]. However, the extent of treatment required depends on the initial concentration and form of these organisms in source water, which varies with watershed characteristics, seasonal patterns, and biogeochemical conditions affecting their survival and transport through soils and groundwater.

## Conclusion and Management Implications

Biogeochemical processes fundamentally shape water quality by transforming contaminant and nutrient concentrations during residence in groundwater, hyporheic zones, and riparian aquifers. The integration of mechanistic biogeochemical process descriptions into multiscale water quality models has substantially advanced the ability to predict how treatment thresholds must adjust based on baseflow composition and dynamics. Under baseflow conditions when groundwater discharge dominates streamflow, contaminant concentrations reflect the cumulative effects of biogeochemical transformations occurring during long residence times in the subsurface, often spanning years to decades. The redox conditions, organic matter availability, temperature, and microbial community composition all jointly determine whether biogeochemical processes remove or mobilize contaminants, with implications for achievability of maximum contaminant level standards.

The relationship between recharge patterns and baseflow composition reveals that seasonal shifts in precipitation characteristics and potential future changes from climate change may require recalibration of treatment thresholds. Water quality managers must recognize that treatment systems designed for average baseflow concentrations may encounter substantially different conditions when antecedent recharge is unusually high or low, because these conditions alter the residence times in which biogeochemical transformation occurs. The challenge of parameter identifiability in complex biogeochemical models suggests that simpler models incorporating only the most sensitive and identifiable parameters may provide more robust predictions when applied to new systems than highly parameterized models overfitted to single data sets.

Legacy contamination transported through groundwater represents a persistent management challenge because even if source control successfully eliminates new inputs, historical contamination continues to discharge to streams through baseflow for decades. The rate of decrease in baseflow contaminant concentrations depends on aquifer residence times and the extent of biogeochemical transformation during subsurface transport. Strategies that enhance biogeochemical hotspots—such as riparian buffer restoration that increases river-groundwater interaction and organic carbon availability—may substantially accelerate contaminant removal compared to in-stream treatment alone. Future progress in water quality management will require continued refinement of multiscale biogeochemical models that bridge the gap between field observations of local processes and watershed-scale predictions of treatment feasibility and costs under varying hydrological and biogeochemical conditions.
